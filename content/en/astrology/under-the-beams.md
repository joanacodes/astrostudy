Under the beams began as an observer's phrase: the planet is up, but only by day, so no one can see it. It is the widest of the three zones around the Sun, and every planet enters it on the way to each meeting with the Sun and leaves it on the way out.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A planet is under the beams when it stands within about 15° of the Sun along the zodiac. Seen from Earth, it rises and sets close to the Sun, so it is up only in daylight or in bright twilight, and the Sun's light hides it. The zone has two tighter rings inside it: [combustion](/astrology/combustion/), where the planet is more deeply lost in the glare, and [cazimi](/astrology/cazimi/), within 17 minutes of arc of the Sun's centre, which tradition reads as a strength.

Tradition listed being under the Sun's beams among the accidental debilities, with combustion and [retrograde](/astrology/retrograde-explained/) motion: conditions of a planet's situation, not of its sign. In reception, where one planet assists another, a planet under the beams is reckoned of less use to the planet it receives.

The 15° is a convention, not a law of optics. Whether a planet can actually be seen near the Sun depends on how bright it is, on the season and the latitude, and on the weather. [Venus](/astrology/venus/), the brightest planet, shows itself closer to the Sun than faint Saturn does.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Coming out of the beams is the moment astronomers call a heliacal rising: the first morning the planet can be seen again, low in the east just before dawn ([Heliacal rising](/astrology/heliacal-rising/)). For Mercury and Venus there is also an evening version, a first sighting low in the west after sunset. Which side a planet reappears on is the question of [oriental and occidental](/astrology/oriental-and-occidental/) planets.

At any moment, a planet under the beams is either sinking towards the Sun, about to disappear entirely, or pulling away from it, about to be seen. The geometry is the same; the direction is opposite.

The Moon goes under the beams every month. It moves about 12° a day away from the Sun, so it spends roughly a day either side of each [new moon](/astrology/new-moon/) within 15° of it: the last thin crescent of the [balsamic moon](/astrology/balsamic-moon/) disappears into the dawn, and the first crescent appears in the evening a day or two later. Those days make up about a twelfth of every month, so about 1 person in 12 is born with the Moon under the beams.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

A planet under the beams works in the shade of the Sun, which in my readings stands for identity and purpose: it is active, but not yet separately visible. I read it as a part of you that operates before it is noticed, by you or by others, often serving the larger project of who you are rather than standing out on its own.

Direction adds a shade of meaning. A planet pulling away from the Sun, about to reappear, reads to me as a function emerging; one sinking towards it, about to vanish into the glare, as a function being absorbed. Neither is better. Both describe where a function sits in its cycle with the Sun.

The Moon is the placement here that a birth date can't settle: within a single day it can cross the 15° line, so the hour of birth decides which side of it you were born on ([The Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Under the beams is the same as combust.”** It is the wider zone around combustion. A planet can be under the beams without being combust.
- **“A planet under the beams does nothing.”** It is hidden from view, not inactive. Its function works out of sight.
- **“Every planet spends the same time under the beams.”** The faster a planet moves against the Sun, the shorter its stay: about two and a half days for the Moon, months for Mars, which keeps nearly the Sun's pace around their meeting.
- **“A planet under the beams is retrograde.”** Only Mercury and Venus can be both at once, when they pass between us and the Sun. From Mars outwards, a planet under the beams is always moving forward: its retrogrades come around the opposition, on the far side of the sky.
