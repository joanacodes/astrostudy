Capella is the little she-goat of the sky, the brightest star of Auriga, the Charioteer, and from anywhere in Britain it never sets. At 22°13′ Gemini in 2026, it outshines Aldebaran, Regulus, Antares and Spica, the bright stars close to the Sun's path, though it rides almost twenty-three degrees north of that path.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

With a magnitude of 0.08, Capella, α Aurigae, is the sixth-brightest star of the night sky and the third-brightest of the northern sky, after Arcturus and Vega. It looks like one star and is four, two yellow giants circling each other every 104 days and a faint pair of red dwarfs far out, about 43 light-years from us. Both giants have used up the hydrogen in their cores. No other first-magnitude star sits closer to the north celestial pole, so north of latitude 44° it never sets: all of Britain, most of Europe. South of 44° south, it never rises.

[Gemini](/astrology/gemini/) has held it for every birth in living memory, at 20°52′ in 1930, 21°51′ in 2000 and 22°13′ in 2026, though the star belongs to the Charioteer ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)). Its latitude is 22.9° north of the ecliptic, and precession, which carries the goat along the zodiac a degree every 72 years, does nothing to bring it closer ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

*Capella* is Latin for a little she-goat, a translation of the Greek Aix, the Goat. Greek myth made it Amalthea, the goat who suckled Zeus, and whose broken horn became the cornucopia, the horn of plenty, filled with whatever its owner wished. In Ptolemy's *Almagest* it is the goat that the Charioteer carries; Johann Bayer's star atlas of 1603 put it on the charioteer's back instead.

The Bedouin of the Negev and Sinai called it the Capella of the Pleiades, because it points the way to that little cluster ([Alcyone and the Pleiades](/astrology/alcyone-and-the-pleiades/)), and Hawaiian navigators set it in a great canoe-bailer of stars with [Sirius](/astrology/sirius/), Procyon, Castor and Pollux.

R. H. Allen's *Star Names* (1899) records its astrological reputation: civic and military honours, and wealth. Medieval magic gave the goat star a place among the fifteen Behenian stars, with Jupiter and Saturn for its planets and the sapphire for its stone, and Cornelius Agrippa drew its sign under the name Hircus, Latin for goat.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

By 12 or 13 June the Sun has reached 22° Gemini and Capella's degree: a [Sun in Gemini](/astrology/sun-in-gemini/) in its third decan. For that Sun, as for any planet at 21° to 23° Gemini, the contact the tradition counts is a shared zodiac degree with a star nearly twenty-three degrees away in the sky, within about a degree ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)).

Capella is a star I could show anyone in Britain on any clear night of the year, and one I never show in a chart, because the system behind my readings leaves every star out ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). Honours and wealth are just the kind of promise I'd leave in Allen's pages.
