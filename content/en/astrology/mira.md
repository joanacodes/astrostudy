Mira is the star that comes and goes: over about 332 days its light swells to easy naked-eye brightness, then fades until no eye can find it. It stands at 1°53′ Taurus in 2026, in the neck of the sea monster Cetus, and its name, Latin for wonderful, is not yet four centuries old.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

The magnitude the Yale Bright Star Catalogue gives ο Ceti, 3.04, is one snapshot of a star that never holds still. At an average peak it reaches about magnitude 3.5; some peaks have reached 2.0, others only 4.9. At minimum it sinks to between 8.6 and 10.1, out of sight without a telescope. It takes about 100 days to brighten and about twice as long to fade, and between its brightest peaks and faintest troughs its light changes some 1,700 times.

It is a pulsating red giant about 300 light-years away, near the end of its life, with a white dwarf for a companion. As it moves through space it sheds gas behind it: a tail 13 light-years long, built up over some 30,000 years and seen in ultraviolet light in 2007.

Its degree has been in [Taurus](/astrology/taurus/) since about 1891, 1°31′ in 2000 and 1°53′ in 2026: it crossed over from Aries, carried by the slow drift of the equinox ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)). It lies 15.9° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), too far for the Moon ever to pass in front of it.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Mira has almost no ancient tradition, because it had almost no place in ancient astronomy. The catalogues of Ptolemy, al-Sufi, Ulugh Beg and Tycho Brahe don't mention it at all, even as an ordinary star. Ptolemy noted the faint cluster astrologers call [Acumen](/astrology/acumen/); Mira, which can outshine it, isn't there.

Its recorded story begins on 3 August 1596. David Fabricius, following what he took for Mercury (it was Jupiter), chose a nearby star of the third magnitude to measure against. By 21 August it had brightened by a magnitude, and by October it had vanished; he took it for a new star until he saw it again on 16 February 1609. In 1638 Johannes Holwarda timed its returns at about eleven months, and in 1662 Johannes Hevelius named it Mira, wonderful, in a short book about a star that behaved like no other then known.

Apart from the exploding stars, and perhaps [Algol](/astrology/algol/), Mira was the first star found to change in brightness, and it lends its name to a whole class of stars that pulse the same way.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Around 21 and 22 April each year, a day or two after entering Taurus, the Sun passes Mira's degree ([Sun in Taurus](/astrology/sun-in-taurus/)). Mira itself is then lost in the Sun's glare. The slow planets came by in the 1930s: Uranus for births in parts of 1934, 1935 and early 1936 ([Uranus in Taurus](/astrology/uranus-in-taurus/)), then Saturn in 1939–40, and again in 1969–70 and 1998–99 ([Saturn in Taurus](/astrology/saturn-in-taurus/)).

Its degree is as steady as any star's; only its light moves. So a conjunction with Mira is the same arithmetic whether the star was at its peak or out of sight on the day of a birth. Whether that difference should count is a question for astrologers who read stars. I don't: the system I built doesn't compute them, and I describe Mira here as a star with a remarkable history rather than a meaning to hand on ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).
