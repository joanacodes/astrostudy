In the third week of December, the Sun passes in front of the heart of our galaxy. Nobody sees it happen: the Galactic Centre lies behind thick clouds of dust, about 26,000 light years away. Projected onto the zodiac, it sits near the end of Sagittarius.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Galactic Centre is the centre of the Milky Way, the point the whole galaxy turns around. At its heart is a black hole of about four million times the Sun's mass, called Sagittarius A*. From the Earth it lies in the direction of Sagittarius, Ophiuchus and Scorpius, where the Milky Way looks brightest, but dust blocks its light, and what astronomers know of it comes from radio waves, infrared light and X-rays.

Finding it took most of the 20th century. In 1918 Harlow Shapley noticed that the globular clusters around the Milky Way seemed to be centred on the star clouds of Sagittarius. In 1954 radio astronomers working in Sydney found an intense source in the middle of a belt of radio emission there and named it Sagittarius A; in 1958 astronomers adopted its position as the zero point of galactic longitude. No astrologer before the 20th century could have marked it in a chart.

In a chart it is a degree: about 25°52′ Sagittarius in 1930, 26°51′ in 2000, 27°13′ in 2026 and 27°33′ in 2050. It lies about 5.6° south of the ecliptic, so a planet "on" it shares its zodiac degree while passing a little to the north.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/) is measured from the equinoxes, and the equinoxes slide back against the stars by about a degree every 72 years ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)). So the Galactic Centre, fixed in the sky, creeps forward through the signs at that pace. It will cross into Capricorn around 2225.

Who has a planet on it, within a degree, between 1930 and 2030:

- **The Sun:** births around 17 to 20 December, depending on the year, less than three degrees before the December solstice.
- **Jupiter:** about every twelve years, most recently late in 2019.
- **Saturn:** in 1958, in 1988 and in 2017.
- **Uranus:** in 1987 and 1988. **Neptune:** in 1982 and 1983. **Pluto:** in 2006 and 2007.

Eclipses pass over it too. At the total lunar eclipse of 19 December 1964, the Sun stood less than a degree from it, which made that eclipse the [prenatal eclipse](/astrology/prenatal-eclipse/) of everyone born in the months that followed.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The system I built for my readings computes no stars, and the Galactic Centre is, for a chart, one more fixed point of the sky, like the stars on [fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/). I describe it; I don't read it, any more than I read a planet's [conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/). My notebook never tested it either, so I have no count of my own for how often a planet on it says something ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

If your Sun is at 27° [Sagittarius](/astrology/sagittarius/), I read a Sagittarius Sun in its house, with its aspects, the way I would at any other degree of the sign. The planet does the work in a chart, and the house, which needs the birth time, says where. The degree is shared with everyone born the same few days, and for the slow planets, with everyone born over a year or two.

The Milky Way, for its part, belongs to a larger family of galaxies, and astrologers mark its centre too ([Super-Galactic Centre](/astrology/super-galactic-centre/)). What goes into my readings, and what stays out, is in [how I read](/how-i-read/).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“You can see the Galactic Centre.”** Dust hides it from the eye; it is known from radio, infrared and X-ray observations.
- **“Ancient astrologers used it.”** Its position wasn't known until the 20th century.
- **“It always sits at 27° Sagittarius.”** It moves forward about a degree every 72 years, and will enter Capricorn around 2225.
- **“It's the centre of the universe.”** It's the centre of one galaxy, ours, which in turn belongs to a larger cluster of galaxies.
