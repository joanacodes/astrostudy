The biseptile is the second step of the sevens: two planets two-sevenths of the circle apart, about 102°51′, past the square but short of the trine. As I read it, it's the aspect of branching: one pattern, carried forward in two directions.

## What it means {#what-it-means}

Two sevenths of 360° is 102°51′: three signs and almost thirteen degrees. A planet in roughly the first seventeen degrees of its sign makes it to the sign three on; one later in its sign, to the sign four on. The signs change, the angle doesn't, and the angle is what I read.

I read it as differentiation that repeats. Each time the two planets meet a situation, they branch a little further apart, each developing its own strand, and yet the strands stay coordinated, more like a network than a line. Nothing in it is fixed in advance, magical or certain: it doesn't say what will branch, or when, only that these two parts of you grow by dividing the work between them.

Its orb stays at about a degree (see [orbs](/astrology/orbs/)), and with reason: the [tredecile](/astrology/tredecile/), at 108°, is only about five degrees away, and a looser orb would let the two blur.

It sits 12°51′ past the [square](/astrology/square/) and 17°09′ short of the [trine](/astrology/trine/), closer to friction than to flow, and belongs to neither. Doubled, it makes 205°43′, which is the triseptile measured the other way round: the sevens fold back on themselves. For astrologers who work with [harmonic charts](/astrology/harmonic-charts/), it's part of the seventh harmonic, and the chart that multiplies every position by seven brings it to a [conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/), as it does every septile.

## Between which planets {#between-which-planets}

Biseptiles have no pair pages here; what I look at first is the speed of the two bodies.

Two personal planets in biseptile, among the Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus and Mars, tend to split the work of daily life: one keeps the routine, the other keeps exploring, and the arrangement keeps renewing itself. With a slow planet on one side, the branching takes years, as a personal habit keeps differentiating from a larger, slower pattern; that's the case I give most attention to. Between two slow planets, the branching belongs to everyone born in those years; the houses they occupy in your chart show where it reaches you.

Its neighbours among the minor aspects are its own family. The [septile](/astrology/septile/), at 51°26′, is the pattern as it first emerges; the [triseptile](/astrology/triseptile/), at 154°17′, is the pattern drawing its strands back together.

## The biseptile in synastry {#in-synastry}

In synastry, a biseptile describes two people who become more distinct by being together: each develops a different strand of something shared, a skill, a household, a project, and the strands stay coordinated rather than competing. Beside a [sextile](/astrology/sextile/) or an [opposition](/astrology/opposition/) between the same two charts, it's a quiet thread, and I mention it in [synastry readings](/synastry/) only when it's tight and involves a personal planet, read in both directions.

## The biseptile in transits {#in-transits}

Transiting biseptiles pass almost unnoticed. From the Moon and the inner planets they're a matter of hours or days; Jupiter may hold one for a few weeks, and only the outer planets stay for months. If one registers at all, it's as a moment when two parts of a situation start to go their separate ways, without a break between them. Weather, not a forecast: a biseptile transit shows a branching, not where either branch leads.
