Alfred Witte had the lord of the underworld in his charts before anyone had found Pluto. He called the point Hades, and when a planet named after the same god's Roman title turned up in 1930, Uranian astrology found itself with two.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Of the four points Witte proposed beyond Neptune, Hades and [Cupido](/astrology/cupido-uranian-point/) are the pair whose colours he described, in articles collected long after his death in *Der Mensch – eine Empfangsstation kosmischer Suggestionen*. The other two were [Zeus](/astrology/zeus/) and Kronos, and in 1927 Friedrich Sieggrün added four more ([The Hamburg School (Uranian astrology)](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/)).

The name belongs to the Greek god of the dead and of riches, king of the underworld; since antiquity it has generally been taken to mean the unseen one. He was the eldest son of Cronus and Rhea. With his brothers Zeus and [Poseidon](/astrology/poseidon/) he overthrew the Titans, and the three shared out the world: Zeus took the sky, Poseidon the sea, Hades the realm of the dead, with the earth left to all three.

Around the fifth century BC, perhaps wary of saying his name aloud, Greeks began to call him Plouton, the rich one, for the wealth that comes up out of the ground, crops and metals. Latin made it Pluto, the name the body found in 1930 now carries ([Pluto](/astrology/pluto/)). Roman writers later folded other underworld gods into him under that name, Dis Pater and Orcus among them, and the dwarf planet [Orcus](/astrology/orcus/) carries one of them. His queen was Persephone, daughter of Zeus and Demeter, whom he carried off below; a planet once imagined beyond Pluto has carried her name too ([Persephone (hypothetical planet)](/astrology/persephone-hypothetical/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Uranian astrologers read Hades as a near relation of Pluto. They tie it to esoteric callings and to work devoted to healing, and also to emotional and mental difficulties and the wounds a life leaves.

In a Uranian chart the two underworld lords stand apart. Pluto sits where astronomers observe it, on its orbit of about 248 years; Hades sits where the school's calculations put it, and each has its own aspects and midpoints. Witte's school wanted exactly that: it added its points to account for events that the astronomically certified planets didn't clearly explain.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Hades isn't part of my readings. When a reading turns to old wounds, I start with [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/), which in my notebook was one of the three placements that held far more often than the rest, 75 to 90% of the time ([The three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/)), and with Pluto by sign, house and aspect.

Mental health is where I'm most careful. A chart is not a diagnosis, and no point, calculated or observed, can tell anyone what their mind is going through; if that is the question, a doctor or a therapist is the person to ask ([Astrology and mental health](/astrology/astrology-and-mental-health/)).

A calling to healing work, too, I'd look for in the whole chart, in what its planets and houses say about work and care, rather than in one point that no one has observed ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Hades is Pluto under its Greek name.”** In myth, Pluto began as one of Hades's titles. In a Uranian chart they are two separate positions, and Witte had his Hades before the planet was found.
- **“Hades shows when someone will die.”** It carries the name of the god of the dead, but no point can say when a life will end, and I never read a chart for that ([Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)).
- **“Hades is all darkness.”** The god ruled riches as well as the dead, and Uranian astrologers tie the point to healing callings as much as to wounds.
