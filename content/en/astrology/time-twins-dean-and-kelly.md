A birth chart is a picture of the sky at the minute you were born, from the place you were born. So two people born at almost the same moment, in the same town, carry almost the same chart, and if the chart describes the person, those two ought to resemble each other. They're called time twins, and they make a clean test.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

No effect. In a study of people born within five minutes of each other, run to see whether being born at nearly the same moment made any discernible difference, none was seen. Geoffrey Dean and Ivan Kelly, who studied time twins themselves, also reported a large test of more than a hundred measures, of thinking, behaviour, the body and more, that found no support for astrology.

That's the finding, and I'm not going to dress it up. If a chart shaped people the way it's often said to, time twins are where it would show most clearly.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**An old question, turned round.** The objection is almost as old as astrology. Cicero argued that twins, born close together, can have very different lives, and Augustine later developed the same argument. Time twins turn the question round: instead of asking why twins differ, ask whether people born at the same moment turn out alike.

**Why minutes matter.** A chart changes through the day, but not everywhere at once. The horizon moves about a degree every four minutes, so the [rising sign](/astrology/ascendant/) and the house cusps shift, while the planets barely move: most stay in the same sign from one day to the next. Two people born five minutes apart in the same town have charts that differ by about a degree at the angles and hardly at all elsewhere. It's also why [the birth time decides so much](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#what-the-time-decides), and why I don't read a chart without it.

**Addey's criteria.** The astrologer John Addey surveyed time twins himself. Dean and Kelly called his work “perhaps the most extensive survey of time twins made by an astrologer”. In their 2003 paper, in the *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, they describe their own, independent study, which took Addey's criteria for how close in time two births had to be.

**The results.** The same paper reports a large-scale test, by Dean, a scientist and former astrologer, and Kelly, a psychologist, that covered more than one hundred cognitive, behavioural, physical and other variables, and found no support for astrology. The philosopher of science Massimo Pigliucci describes a study of people born within five minutes of each other, run to see whether there was any discernible effect: none was seen.

**What it leaves out.** Notice what this kind of test doesn't need: no astrologer's judgement, no reading to rate, nobody to flatter. It compares people with people. That makes it a good partner to [Carlson's double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/), where astrologers tried to match charts to personality profiles and did no better than chance. Dean has also suggested an explanation for [the Mars effect (Gauquelin)](/astrology/mars-effect-gauquelin/): that some parents reported birth times that suited their beliefs.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

Dean and Kelly asked whether the same chart makes the same person. My notebook asked something smaller: whether each placement in a chart described the person it belonged to. I checked every placement of my own chart, Sun, Moon, rising, each planet in its sign and house, with a tick or a cross, then did the same for family, friends and strangers, with their consent.

Overall, about a third held: 30 to 40%, with the Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury among them, the placements everyone reads first. Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90% of the time.

It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general. It certainly doesn't outweigh research like this. What it gives me is a rule for readings: when something rests on a placement that held less often in my tests, I tell you so. The count itself is in [my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).
