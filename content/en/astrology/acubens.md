Acubens is a faint star with a sharp name: Arabic for the claws, the star at the tip of one of the Crab's pincers. It is easy to overlook in the sky and in the books, and near enough to the Moon's path to be hidden by it now and then.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Acubens is α Cancri, magnitude 4.25 in the Yale catalogue: visible without a telescope under a dark sky, easy to miss in a town. It is a white star about twice the Sun's mass, some 178 light-years away, with an eleventh-magnitude companion about 11″ from it.

Acubens reached exactly 14°00′ [Leo](/astrology/leo/) in 2026, after 13°38′ in 2000 and 12°39′ in 1930. In Ptolemy's day it stood in the sign of Cancer, like its constellation; precession has since carried it into Leo, as it has the cluster beside it.

Its latitude is 5.1° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), at the edge of the band the [Moon](/astrology/moon/) travels in. So the Moon can pass in front of it, and astronomers used one such occultation, in 2014, to look for a close companion that earlier observers had suspected, and found none ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

The name comes from the Arabic al-zubānā, the claws, and a second name, Sertan, from al-saraṭān, the crab. Both were once shared with ι Cancri, which marks the other claw. Johann Bode later lettered it α² Cancri, keeping α¹ for another star, but the superscripts didn't last.

The constellation around it is an old one. Aratus called it Karkinos, the crab, and Hipparchus and Ptolemy followed him; Manilius and Ovid called it Litoreus, the shore-dweller. Eratosthenes saw more in it: the Crab, the Asses and the Crib, the last being [Praesepe](/astrology/praesepe/), the star cluster 6° before Acubens in the zodiac.

The record I can check ends there: a dim star at the tip of a claw, with two old names and an occultation to its credit, and no astrological reading of its own.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

For a 2026 birth the tradition's window runs from 13°00′ to 15°00′ Leo; for a 1930 birth, from 11°39′ to 13°39′, so the two overlap by only 39′ ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). The Sun meets the star's degree on about 5 and 6 August, a [Sun in Leo](/astrology/sun-in-leo/) almost halfway through the sign.

Because Acubens is within the Moon's range, a Moon on its degree can stand right beside the star, or in front of it, depending on where the Moon is in its monthly swing north and south. [Polaris](/astrology/polaris/), the next star on this site, is the opposite case: a bright star whose degree, 28°55′ Gemini, is 66° from the Sun's path.

A faint star, an orb of a degree, and an Ascendant that crosses that degree in about four minutes: Acubens on an angle would rest entirely on the birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). My readings don't compute fixed stars, so I leave the claw here, described.
