Koch is the house system most often set beside [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/), and the two are close relations: both cut the sky by time, both keep the angles on the cusps. Where they part is in whose time they measure. Placidus times every degree; Koch times one.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Koch is a quadrant system. The [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) opens the 1st house, the IC the 4th, the Descendant the 7th and the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) the 10th, exactly as in Placidus. The difference is in the cusps between them.

Koch starts from the Midheaven's degree, the one culminating at birth, and asks how long ago it was rising on the eastern horizon. That stretch of time, from its rising to its culmination, is cut into three equal parts. The 11th and 12th cusps are the degrees that were rising over the birthplace at the end of the first and second thirds. The cusps below the horizon come from the same count carried forward past the birth, and the remaining cusps lie opposite.

So every Koch cusp is a degree that rises over the place of birth at some moment close to the birth, an Ascendant borrowed from a nearby moment. That's why it's also called the birthplace house system.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Walter Koch (1895–1970), a German astrologer, put the system forward in the early 1960s; his tables were published in 1965 and appeared in English in 1971. Its critics answered that, for all the claims of originality, it is a modification of an older method, [Alcabitius](/astrology/alcabitius/). It became popular with research astrologers in the United States and with German-speaking astrologers, and later lost some ground in Central Europe to the [Krusiński](/astrology/krusinski/) system.

At the equator, Koch and Placidus give the same houses, because every degree there takes the same six sidereal hours to climb, the Midheaven's included. Away from the equator, the two drift apart, and at the latitude of London their in-between cusps can differ by several degrees, enough to move a planet near a cusp into the next house. Like Placidus, Koch produces unequal houses and [intercepted signs](/astrology/intercepted-signs/), and like Placidus it can't be drawn beyond the polar circles, at about 66° north or south ([houses at high latitudes](/astrology/houses-at-high-latitudes/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I use Placidus, not Koch, and the reason is the measure. Placidus divides each degree's own climb; Koch divides one degree's climb and borrows the horizon at three moments. Both are reasoned answers, and Koch's idea, that every cusp should be a real horizon of the birthplace, is a coherent one. I keep to Placidus because a reading needs one consistent frame, and every house on this site is written in it ([which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/)).

If your chart was drawn in Koch, nothing is broken. Your signs, degrees, aspects and angles are the same as in Placidus; only some intermediate cusps differ, so check any planet close to one before comparing descriptions. Both systems need an exact birth time, since every cusp turns with the sky ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Koch differs more from the systems outside its family. [Equal houses](/astrology/equal-houses/) keep the Ascendant on the 1st cusp but let the Midheaven float; [Whole Sign houses](/astrology/whole-sign-houses/) give each sign a house and keep neither angle on a cusp. A planet can move a whole house between Koch and Whole Sign, far more than between Koch and Placidus ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Koch is just Placidus under another name.”** They share the angles and agree at the equator. Elsewhere their in-between cusps differ, sometimes by several degrees.
- **“The birthplace system is the only one that uses the birthplace.”** Every house system needs the place of birth. The name says how Koch uses it: each cusp is a horizon of that place.
- **“Koch works at any latitude.”** Like Placidus, it stops at the polar circles.
