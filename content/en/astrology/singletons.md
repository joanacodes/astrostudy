Some charts put nine planets on one side and a single planet on the other. That lone planet is called a singleton, and in a chart drawn like that it's hard to look anywhere else.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A singleton is a planet standing by itself in a share of the chart the others have left. The share can be half the wheel: the only planet above the horizon, say, or the only one west of the meridian ([Hemisphere emphasis](/astrology/hemisphere-emphasis/)). It can be a quarter ([Quadrants](/astrology/quadrants/)). It can also be an element or a modality: the only planet in fire, the only one in fixed signs ([Element balance and missing elements](/astrology/element-balance-and-missing-elements/)).

Not every astrologer uses every sense. Some keep the word for a planet alone in a hemisphere, others apply it to elements and modalities as well, and one chart can have a singleton by one count and none by another. I always say which count I mean.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A singleton works by contrast. Nine planets crowding one side describe where the chart's energy gathers; the tenth, alone on the other side, becomes the only channel into the ground they left. Astrologers who use the idea give it extra weight for that reason: whatever that half, element or modality stands for, it passes through one planet.

The count is only as good as its list. Adding Chiron or the nodes can make a singleton appear or disappear, which is one more reason to say which points went in before drawing anything from the result.

The hemisphere and quadrant senses rest entirely on the angles, so the birth time decides them. As the wheel turns through the day, the horizon sweeps across the planets, and a planet that was alone above it at noon can have company there by evening. I want the time before I call anything a singleton ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). By element or modality, the picture usually holds for the whole day, unless the Moon changes sign during it.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read a singleton as a point of focus. A single planet in the upper half, with everything else below, often becomes the one through which the person meets the world. A single planet in water, in a chart otherwise of fire and air, carries that whole receptive, feeling way of meeting life by itself.

East and west work the same way. With every planet but one gathered around the Ascendant, the lone planet in the west often carries most of the chart's dealings with other people: a single planet in the 7th house, for instance, becomes the place where partnership gets its say.

What the planet is matters as much as where it stands. A singleton Moon brings feelings and needs into focus; a singleton Saturn, structure and responsibility; a singleton Jupiter, growth and the search for meaning. The planet keeps its own function, and being alone simply concentrates it.

Alone in position isn't alone in contact. A singleton can make several aspects that tie it to the crowd on the other side; a planet with no major aspects at all is a different case ([Unaspected planets](/astrology/unaspected-planets/)). When a planet is both, alone in its half and unaspected, I give it my longest look, and my gentlest.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A singleton means you're lonely.”** The planet is alone, not the person. It describes a focus in the chart.
- **“A singleton is always the most important planet.”** It draws the eye, but the Sun, the Moon and the rest still count.
- **“Singletons and unaspected planets are the same thing.”** One is alone by position, the other by contact.
- **“Everyone agrees on what counts as a singleton.”** The senses vary, so two astrologers can find different singletons in the same chart.
