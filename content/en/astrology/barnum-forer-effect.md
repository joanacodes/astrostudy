Read a personality sketch that seems to be about you, and you'll very likely find it accurate, even if everyone around you was handed the same one. That's the Barnum effect, also called the Forer effect, and it's the trap I most wanted my own testing to avoid.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Yes, it's real, and it bears directly on astrology. People give high accuracy ratings to descriptions they believe were written for them, even when those descriptions are vague and general enough to fit almost anyone, and the effect can partly explain why astrology, fortune telling and some personality tests feel so accurate. So a reading that feels true proves very little on its own. The question to ask of any statement is whether it could just as well describe someone else.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**Forer's classroom.** In 1948 the psychologist Bertram Forer gave a psychology test, his “Diagnostic Interest Blank”, to 39 of his students, and told them each would receive a short personality sketch based on their results. A week later each student was handed a sketch that seemed to be theirs, and asked to rate how well it applied. Every one of them had received the same sketch, with lines such as “You have a great need for other people to like and admire you” and “At times you have serious doubts as to whether you have made the right decision or done the right thing”.

**The ratings.** On a scale from 0 to 5, five being the most accurate, the class rated the sketch highly. Accounts of Forer's 1949 paper differ slightly on the average: 4.26 in one, 4.30 in another. Only after the ratings were in did Forer reveal that everyone had the same sketch, and where it came from: astrology, a news-stand astrology book according to one account of the paper, a newspaper astrology column according to another. Forer called what he had shown the fallacy of personal validation.

**The name.** *Barnum effect* came later, in 1956, from the psychologist Paul Meehl, who likened the vague personality descriptions of some psychological tests to those of the showman P. T. Barnum.

**What makes it stronger.** Later studies found that people rate such a description as more accurate when they believe it was made for them personally, when they believe in the authority of the person giving it, and when it lists mostly positive traits. Replication studies point to two factors in particular: the balance of positive and negative statements, and whether the person trusts the honesty of whoever is giving the feedback. Popular horoscopes, one study noted, use vague wording built around ordinary, everyday activities.

**Beside it.** Forer's paper came out three years before Jung described his own statistical test of astrology: [Jung’s marriage experiment](/astrology/jungs-marriage-experiment/). And a related trap, seeing a link that isn't there, comes up again in [Do full moons change behaviour?](/astrology/do-full-moons-change-behaviour/)

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My notebook was set up against exactly this. The rule was a tick only if a placement described me in a way I couldn't have written about anyone else, and a cross if it was vague, wrong, or would have fitted my neighbour just as well. No half marks: half marks are where wishful thinking lives. I checked every placement of my own chart that way, Sun, Moon, rising, each planet in its sign and house, then did the same, with their consent, for family, friends and strangers, because I knew myself too well to be fair.

Overall, about a third held: 30 to 40%. The Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury, the placements everyone reads first, are among those. That's close to what a flattering, vague horoscope scores, so I hold them lightly. Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90% of the time.

A rule like mine narrows the door; it doesn't shut it. It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general. What it gives me is a habit: when something in a reading rests on a placement that held less often in my tests, I say so. The whole method is in [my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).
