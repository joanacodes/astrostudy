Everyone knows their Sun sign, which is exactly what makes it hard to test. Ask people to describe themselves and, if they know their sign, you are partly asking them about the sign.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

No link that survives a fair test. People who know astrology do tend to describe themselves the way their [Sun](/astrology/sun/) sign is said to be, and more so when they know the study is about astrology; people who don't know what their sign is supposed to mean show nothing. The sign seems to work through what people know about it, not through where the Sun stood when they were born.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**Self-attribution.** The pattern has a name and a long record. In a 2003 paper in *Personality and Individual Differences*, Edgar Wunder set out the studies behind it. From Silverman in 1971 and Delaney and Woodyard in 1974 onwards, people familiar with their sign's traits were found to rate themselves in line with them, and the finding was repeated by Eysenck and Nias, by Fichten and Sunerton, by Kelly, by van Rooij and others. It held when astrology wasn't mentioned until the debriefing, and even in data collected for purposes that had nothing to do with astrology, but it grew stronger when people were given a cue that the study was about it. It is a close relative of the [self-fulfilling prophecy](/astrology/self-fulfilling-prophecy/).

**The decisive comparison.** The studies that settle it tested people who didn't know the code. In the eight that Wunder lists, published between 1972 and 1986, people unfamiliar with what the Sun signs are supposed to mean showed no effect at all. A trait that appears only in people who have read about it points to the reading, not to the sky.

**Blinded tests.** Tests that take self-description out altogether point the same way. Reviews of the controlled studies, which include Geoffrey Dean's 1985 paper on whether astrology can predict extraversion and neuroticism, report that with a control group and proper blinding, horoscopes have shown no effect beyond chance. Whole charts fared no better than Sun signs: in [Carlson's double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/), astrologers couldn't match birth charts to personality profiles more often than guessing would.

**Beyond personality.** In 2024 Mohsen Joshanloo, writing in the journal *Kyklos*, found zodiac signs no more use than random numbers for predicting people's well-being and quality of life.

**Why it still feels true.** Sun-sign descriptions are often broad enough to fit almost anyone, and we rate a description as accurate when we believe it was written for us: the [Barnum (Forer) effect](/astrology/barnum-forer-effect/). Where the time of year you were born does leave a trace, it comes through the seasons and the school calendar, not through the sign ([Season of birth: the real effects](/astrology/season-of-birth-the-real-effects/)). The Moon has been put to the same kind of test, and its supposed hold over behaviour didn't survive either ([Do full moons change behaviour?](/astrology/do-full-moons-change-behaviour/)).

## What I found {#what-i-found}

The Sun sign was on my invoice like every other placement: Sun, Moon, rising, each planet in its sign and house, a tick or a cross beside each. It held about a third of the time, with the rising sign, Venus and Mercury, the placements everyone reads first; overall, about a third of what I checked held, 30 to 40%, close to what a flattering horoscope scores. Three did far better: Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90% of the time.

The people I tested marked their own lines, which is the very situation the self-attribution studies describe, so I hold the Sun sign's third lightly. My count was kept in a notebook, not run as a study, and it demonstrates nothing about astrology in general. In a reading, when something rests on your Sun sign alone, I tell you so you can weigh it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)); the count itself is in [my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).
