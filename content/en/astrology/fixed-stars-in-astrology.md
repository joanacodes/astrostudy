To the old astronomers, every light in the sky was a star. Seven of them wandered: the Sun, the Moon and the five planets you can see. The rest kept their places, night after night and century after century, and those were the fixed stars. The name stayed, and so did a small, precise layer of astrology built on them.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

*Planet* comes from the Greek for wandering star. Everything else was fixed, and thought to sit on the firmament, the outermost sphere of the sky. The fixed stars aren't fixed for an astrologer, though. The tropical zodiac is measured from the March equinox, and the equinox slides slowly backwards against the stars ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)), so every star creeps forward along the zodiac, about 1° in 72 years. [Regulus](/astrology/regulus/) stood at 29°49′ Leo in 2000; by 2026 it had crossed into Virgo. A star's degree has to be worked out for the year of birth, never copied from an old table.

A star's zodiac sign isn't its constellation's name either. [Spica](/astrology/spica/), the brightest star in the constellation Virgo, sits at 24° Libra in the tropical zodiac ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).

Two more numbers come with every star. Its brightness is given in magnitudes, where lower means brighter: Regulus is 1.35, [Sirius](/astrology/sirius/), the brightest star of the night sky, −1.46. And its latitude says how far it lies north or south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), the Sun's path. Regulus lies half a degree from it, so the Moon and planets can pass right by it. Sirius lies 39.6° south: no planet ever comes near it in the sky, and a chart can only line them up by zodiac degree.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The oldest list of what stars mean is Ptolemy's. In the *Tetrabiblos* (book I, chapter 9) he describes each bright star by comparing it with the planets: [Aldebaran](/astrology/aldebaran/), "called the Torch", has a nature like that of Mars, and the stars in the feet of Gemini act like Mercury and, more mildly, Venus. He presents these as what earlier observers had noted, not as his own findings.

Later centuries added to the list. Medieval magic singled out fifteen stars, the Behenian stars, each tied to a gemstone and a plant for making talismans; [Algol](/astrology/algol/), Sirius and Aldebaran are among them. Algol carries one of the darkest reputations in the old books, an unfortunate star in the tradition's words, and William Lilly counted any planet within five degrees of it as afflicted. Vivian Robson's *The Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* (1923) gathered the lore for the twentieth century.

In a birth chart, the rule is conjunction, and close. Nicholas DeVore's *Encyclopedia of Astrology* (1947) gives stars no effect by aspect: a star counts only when a birth planet sits on it, and he allowed up to 5° along the zodiac and 1° of latitude. The pages of this site keep to about one degree of zodiac position ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). Others look at the real sky instead: a star and a planet on the horizon or the meridian at the same moment, wherever they lie in longitude ([parans](/astrology/parans/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I describe the stars here; I don't read them. The system I built for my readings doesn't compute them, and they weren't among the placements I tested in my notebook, so I have nothing of my own to say about how often they hold ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

Three things make me cautious about them as well. The orb is tiny: a star on the Ascendant or the Midheaven depends on the birth time to within a few minutes, because the angles move a degree every four ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). The latitude can make a conjunction a matter of arithmetic, not of sky. And the old meanings run to verdicts, royalty, fame, ruin, violence, in a way I won't pass on to anyone. Algol's reputation is a story about a star, not a forecast about a person, and no star can say how or when anyone will die ([Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)).

If another astrologer points a star out in your chart, ask three questions: which year was its degree taken for, how close is the conjunction, and how far does the star lie from the Sun's path?

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Fixed stars don't move.”** Against the tropical zodiac they move about a degree every 72 years. Regulus changed sign between 2000 and 2026.
- **“A star belongs to the sign named after its constellation.”** Spica is the brightest star of the constellation Virgo and stands at 24° Libra.
- **“Fixed stars belong to sidereal astrology.”** Any zodiac can place a star. In the [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/), the one I use, a star's degree slowly advances; in the [sidereal zodiac](/astrology/sidereal-zodiac/), anchored to the stars, it barely moves.
- **“A star on your Sun means fame, or ruin.”** Those are the old books' promises, said as such. A star can colour a planet's tone in a reading that uses it; it can't decide a life.
