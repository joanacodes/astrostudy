The hours of astrology are not the hours on your watch. Each stretch of daylight is cut into twelve, each night into twelve more, and every one of those twenty-four pieces is handed to a planet in turn. That is the system of planetary hours, and it is where the names of the weekdays come from.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A planetary hour is a twelfth of the daylight or a twelfth of the night, ruled by one of the [seven traditional planets](/astrology/seven-traditional-planets/). The first hour after sunrise belongs to the planet the day is named after: the Sun on Sunday, the Moon on Monday, Saturn on Saturday ([planetary days](/astrology/planetary-days/)). The hours that follow run through the planets in the [Chaldean order](/astrology/chaldean-order/), from slowest to fastest, and start again at Saturn after the Moon.

On a Wednesday, then, [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/) rules the first hour, the Moon the second, Saturn the third, then Jupiter, Mars, the Sun and Venus, and Mercury again in the eighth. The night takes over at sunset with its own first hour, which falls five places on from the planet of the day, because twelve hours make one round of seven and five more. Vettius Valens gave each half, light and dark, to the planet of its first hour: daylight that opens with the Sun is followed by a night that opens with Jupiter.

The scheme was developed in Hellenistic astrology, possibly from older Babylonian roots. Like the [Thema Mundi](/astrology/thema-mundi/), the imaginary horoscope those same astrologers drew for the birth of the world, it is a construction laid over the sky, not something you can see in it: nothing changes overhead when Venus's hour gives way to Mercury's.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The hours are unequal. Daylight is divided by twelve and so is the night, so a planetary hour lasts sixty minutes only around the equinoxes, when day and night are about the same length. The farther from the equator and the nearer the solstices, the wider the gap. In London near the summer solstice, a daytime planetary hour lasts about 83 minutes and a night hour about 37; near the winter solstice it is roughly the other way round.

To work them out for a given day:

1. **Find sunrise and sunset** for the place and the date.
2. **Divide the daylight by twelve** for the day hours, and the night, sunset to the next sunrise, by twelve for the night hours.
3. **Give the first day hour to the planet of the day**, then follow the Chaldean order hour by hour, through the night as well.

Because the count needs sunrise and sunset at one place, two cities have different planetary hours on the same day, and the same city has different ones from week to week.

Astronomical tables of the late 15th and 16th centuries often printed the planetary hours with their meanings. Even then they counted for little in astrology as practised, and the Renaissance astrologer Cornelius Gemma said outright that he gave them little weight. Books of magic made more of them: the 16th-century *Key of Solomon* keeps the hours of Mercury for experiments with games and jests, and those of the Moon for recovering stolen property.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't time anything by planetary hours, I don't choose dates for people (that is the work of [electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/)), and I leave the planetary hour of your birth out of my readings. Your planets' own positions, by sign, house and aspect, say far more than which of them ruled the clock.

Your birth time matters to me for a different reason: it sets the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) and the houses, which turn with the sky, roughly a degree in four minutes, and without it I don't read the chart at all ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

If you enjoy following the hours, take them as the old calendar's texture, like the names of the days. They say which planet the tradition put in charge of the clock, not what will happen while it is; what I do read, and how, is in [how I read](/how-i-read/).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A planetary hour lasts sixty minutes.”** Only near the equinoxes. Otherwise day hours and night hours differ, sometimes by more than double.
- **“The hour of Saturn or Mars is bad for everything.”** That's a tradition's verdict on its two malefics, carried over to the clock ([benefics and malefics](/astrology/benefics-and-malefics/)). An hour describes no one.
- **“Planetary hours come straight from Babylon.”** The system as astrology knows it was built in Hellenistic times; older Babylonian roots are possible, not shown.
- **“The planetary hour of your birth is a hidden ruler of your chart.”** It is a reading of the old clock, shared by everyone born in that hour in your town. Your planets' own positions say far more.
