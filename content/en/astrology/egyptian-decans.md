Egypt had its own way of reading the night long before the zodiac arrived: not a belt of twelve signs, but thirty-six star groups coming up over the horizon one after another. The decans began as a clock and ended up as the three faces of every sign.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

The decans were 36 small groups of stars that ancient Egyptians used to tell time at night. As each group rose in the east, a new hour of the night began. Over the year they kept a second count: every ten days a new decan reappeared at dawn, just before sunrise, after weeks hidden in the Sun's glare, a [heliacal rising](/astrology/heliacal-rising/). That is why the Greeks later called them *dekanoi*, “tenths”. Thirty-six decans of ten days made 360 days, and five more days completed the Egyptian year.

The oldest surviving lists are painted on the inside of wooden coffin lids from the First Intermediate Period. These diagonal star tables, made from the 9th Dynasty until the 12th, set the decans out in columns, and Egyptologists sometimes call them star clocks. The sequence was led by [Sirius](/astrology/sirius/), the star the Greeks named Sothis. Honesty about the evidence matters here: the Egyptologist Sarah Symons points out that while we know the names of the decans, and can translate some of them, which stars they were is still not known.

Counting the night by risings left a lasting habit. It produced a night of twelve hours and a day of twelve, whose length changed with the seasons, and only later the twenty-four equal hours we keep now.

Then the two skies met. Egypt was conquered by the Persians in 525 BC, and the historian Tamsyn Barton sees Mesopotamian influence in the Egyptian zodiac, which shares the Balance and the Scorpion with the Babylonian one ([Babylonian astrology](/astrology/babylonian-astrology/)). The Dendera zodiac shows the meeting in stone: a round relief from the ceiling of a chapel of Osiris in the temple of Hathor at Dendera, with the signs of the zodiac inside and 36 figures for the decans around them. The Louvre, which holds it, dates it to 50 BC, and Sylvie Cauville and Éric Aubourg reached the same date from the planets and two eclipses carved on it.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

In Alexandria, under the Ptolemies, the decans were fitted to the zodiac. Each became ten degrees of a sign, three to every sign, and the decan rising in the east took on a particular weight. Texts from the second century BC already make predictions from the planets' signs at the rising of certain decans, Sothis above all.

Astrologers then gave the decans rulers, and more than one scheme grew up. The best known, the [faces](/astrology/faces/), follows the [Chaldean order](/astrology/chaldean-order/) of the planets, Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury, the Moon, round and round the zodiac: Mars for the first ten degrees of Aries, then the Sun, then Venus. Firmicus Maternus sets out three decans to a sign, each with its planet, in the fourth century, and William Lilly still printed the table of faces in 1647. A second scheme, common in modern tropical astrology, gives each decan to a sign of the same element, the logic of the [triplicities](/astrology/triplicities/). On this site each decan has its own page, starting with [Aries, first decan](/astrology/aries-first-decan/), and each gives both rulers with their sources.

The decans also kept their pictures. Hermetic writings and the *Picatrix* describe images of them, and the decans travelled as far as India, where scholars think they arrived by way of the Greeks.

In a birth chart, the decan that matters most is the one on the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/), and it changes fast: a sign takes about two hours on average to rise, so each decan passes in roughly forty minutes. It's one more detail that only the time of birth can settle, and one more reason I don't read a chart without it ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

That stress on whatever is rising at a given moment went into [Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/), which built a whole chart around the rising degree.
