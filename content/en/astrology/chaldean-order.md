Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon. Say the seven traditional planets in that order and you are walking down the ladder ancient astronomy used for the heavens, from the slowest light to the quickest. Astrology hung its clock on that ladder: the hours, the days of the week and the faces of the signs all follow it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The order ranks the [seven traditional planets](/astrology/seven-traditional-planets/) by how long each takes to go round the zodiac, as seen from Earth: Saturn about 29½ years, Jupiter about 12, Mars nearly two, the Sun a year, and the Moon 27⅓ days. In the ancient model of nested spheres, slower meant farther, so the same list reads from the outermost sphere, Saturn's, down to the Moon's, the nearest to Earth.

Venus and Mercury are the awkward pair. Seen from Earth, both keep close to the Sun and go round the zodiac in a year on average, just as it does, so speed alone can't rank the three. The model settled it by placing the Sun in the fourth sphere, three planets above it and three below, and that is where it sits in the order.

The name is a compliment to Babylon, not a record of it. Greeks and Romans tied astrology so closely to Chaldea, their other name for Babylonia, that a Chaldaean came to mean an astrologer. But the label is modern, and nothing shows the sequence itself came from Babylonian astrology. A Babylonian list of the seven runs Moon, Sun, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn, Mercury, Mars; the ladder we call Chaldean looks like a Hellenistic innovation of around the 2nd century BC.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The order is a cycle: after the Moon, it starts again at Saturn. Its main use is to hand out time and space in turn.

- **Hours.** Each hour of the astrological day is given to a planet, one after another in this order, starting with the ruler of the day at sunrise ([planetary hours](/astrology/planetary-hours/)).
- **Days.** Twenty-four hours make three full rounds of seven, with three hours over, so the first hour of the next day, and with it the whole day, falls three places further on. From Saturn, three places on is the Sun; from the Sun, the Moon. That skip produces the week: Sunday, Monday, then the days of Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus and Saturn ([planetary days](/astrology/planetary-days/)).
- **Faces.** The thirty-six faces, ten degrees each, are ruled by the planets in this order, starting from Mars in the first ten degrees of Aries ([Aries, first decan](/astrology/aries-first-decan/)): Mars, Sun, Venus for Aries, Mercury, Moon, Saturn for Taurus, and on round the zodiac. Thirty-six is five rounds of seven and one over, so Mars, which opens the faces, closes them too, in the last ten degrees of Pisces ([faces](/astrology/faces/)).
- **Domiciles.** The same ladder shows in the [Thema Mundi](/astrology/thema-mundi/), the mythical chart that set each planet in its own sign: outward from the Moon's Cancer and the Sun's Leo, the signs go to Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, in the order of their spheres.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read the order itself: it isn't a placement, and it says nothing about your chart that your planets don't. It is scaffolding. What it builds, the hours and the faces, has pages of its own; my readings are built on the planets by sign, house and aspect, and on the timing techniques I describe in [how I read](/how-i-read/).

The ladder does earn its keep in one way. It is also a ranking by how long a planet's [transits](/astrology/transits/) last: [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/) takes about two and a half years to cross a sign, the [Moon](/astrology/moon/) about two and a half days. That's why my forecasts lean on the slow end of it, where a transit lasts long enough to be lived with, and why a passing Moon is a mood rather than a season.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The Chaldeans of Babylon invented it.”** The Babylonians listed the seven in another order, and the name is modern. The sequence looks Hellenistic.
- **“It's the order of the planets from the Sun.”** It is an Earth-centred ranking, with the Sun in the middle of the ladder rather than at its foot.
- **“It's the order of the days of the week.”** The days follow it three places at a time; read straight down, the order would give Saturday, Thursday, Tuesday.
- **“It ranks the planets from best to worst.”** It ranks them by speed and by distance in an old model of the sky, nothing more.
