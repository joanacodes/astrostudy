A chart program will add as many asteroids as you ask for, and each one lands somewhere that sounds meaningful. The skill lies in adding few, adding them late, and keeping only those that repeat what the chart already says.

## Step by step {#step-by-step}

1. **Read the chart without them first.** The planets, signs, houses and aspects carry the story; an asteroid adds a detail and never starts one. If you can't yet say what your Moon, Venus and Saturn are doing, asteroids will only add noise ([How to read your birth chart, step by step](/astrology/how-to-read-your-birth-chart-step-by-step/)).
2. **Choose your list before you look.** Start with the first four found, between 1801 and 1807, each named after a goddess and given a symbol from the start: [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/), [Pallas](/astrology/pallas/), [Juno](/astrology/juno/) and [Vesta](/astrology/vesta/), the four that Eleanor Bach's ephemeris of 1973 brought into astrology. Decide which you'll use before you see where they fall, so the chart doesn't choose them for you.
3. **Count what you're adding.** Ten planets make 45 possible pairs. Add four asteroids and there are 91; add Chiron and Lilith too and there are 120. Each body brings more possible contacts than the one before, and that is how a chart drowns.
4. **Keep the contacts close.** Read an asteroid only when it sits within a degree or two of a planet or an angle, and give the conjunction first place, then the opposition and the square; with wider orbs, something will always seem to touch something ([Orbs](/astrology/orbs/)).
5. **Read it as a detail of the planet beside it.** Ceres sits naturally with the [Moon](/astrology/moon/), Juno with [Venus](/astrology/venus/), Pallas with Mercury and Vesta with Saturn. Ask what the asteroid adds to that planet's story, in a word or two: care, strategy, commitment, focus.
6. **Look for repetition.** An asteroid earns its place when it repeats what the planets already say: Juno close to Venus in a chart where the 7th house is full says more than Juno alone. A theme comes from repetition, not from one isolated factor.
7. **Give it a house only with the birth time.** An asteroid's house depends on the time of birth, like every house: in Placidus, the house system I use, every cusp moves with the Ascendant, about a degree every four minutes ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## Common mistakes {#common-mistakes}

- **Reading the asteroids before the planets.** Ceres in the 4th house says little until you know where the Moon is and what Saturn is doing.
- **Adding everything the program offers.** Fifteen asteroids were known by the end of 1851 and the hundredth was found in 1868; today the Minor Planet Center holds data on more than 1.4 million minor planets. A list that long will always say something, which is exactly why it says nothing.
- **Hunting for an asteroid with your own name.** Discoverers propose names within guidelines set by the International Astronomical Union, so an asteroid that shares your name, or your partner's, tells you about its discoverer's choice, not about you.
- **Reading a myth as a forecast.** Juno was the goddess of marriage and Ceres a mother, but neither predicts a wedding, a break-up or a child, and I never read a chart for those ([Can astrology predict pregnancy?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-pregnancy/)).
- **Comparing someone's asteroids without asking.** Adding asteroids to a synastry doesn't change the rule: two charts are compared only with both people's agreement ([Synastry and consent](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)).
- **Lumping Chiron in with them.** [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/), found in 1977, is a centaur, and it was on my invoice: with Saturn and Lilith, it held 75 to 90% of the time ([The three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/)). The asteroids weren't in my notebook, and the system behind my readings doesn't compute them, so I describe them here rather than read them in your chart.
