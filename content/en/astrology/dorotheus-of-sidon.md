Dorotheus of Sidon taught astrology in verse, and the poem has come down to us the long way round: out of Greek, most likely by way of Middle Persian, into Arabic around the year 800, and into English only in the twentieth century. Its fifth book is where the astrology of choosing moments begins.

## Who {#who}

He was a Greek astrologer and poet of the first century AD, and that is nearly all anyone can say of the man. Even his birthplace comes from a later colleague: [Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/), writing in Latin more than two centuries afterwards, calls him a native of Sidon.

His work was a teaching poem in five books, the *Pentateuch*, known in the West as the *[Carmen Astrologicum](/astrology/dorotheus-carmen-astrologicum/)*, “the astrological poem”. Of the Greek, only fragments remain; the philologist Viktor Stegemann gathered them in two volumes, in 1939 and 1943. The complete text is an Arabic version, made around 800 by ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān al-Ṭabarī, known in the West as Omar Tiberiades, a Persian astrologer from Tabaristan and one of the Iranian astrologers of the early Abbasid court, like [Masha’allah](/astrology/mashaallah/). The historian James Holden says he worked from a Middle Persian version; others think he may have had a Greek manuscript in front of him. Either way, the Dorotheus most of us read has passed through at least one other language, and modern readers meet him in English through David Pingree's translation of 1976 and Benjamin Dykes' of 2017; French readers have had a complete translation since 2023.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He taught how to choose a moment. His fifth book, on beginnings, is the oldest surviving treatise on what is now called electional astrology, and the practice as astrologers still know it can be traced back to it. It is a branch I leave alone. I don't choose dates for anything ([electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/)), and many of the dates people would like chosen, an operation or a court hearing among them, sit on my list of what I won't read ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). An election rests on the idea every horoscope rests on, that the moment something begins colours what follows. I keep that idea for a birth, which nobody chooses.

He gave each element its own rulers. In the scheme that carries his name, every triplicity, the three signs of one element, has a planet in charge of charts cast by day, another for charts cast by night, and a third that shares in both: air, for instance, has Saturn by day, Mercury by night and Jupiter alongside ([triplicity rulers](/astrology/triplicity-rulers/)). Of all the ways a planet could be strong in a sign, this was the one Dorotheus rated highest. It also makes the hour of birth count twice: once for the houses, and once for whether the Sun stood above or below the horizon, which decides whose turn it is to lead ([sect: day and night charts](/astrology/sect-day-and-night-charts/)).

He also worked with the lots, points measured from the Ascendant ([doctrine of the lots](/astrology/doctrine-of-the-lots/)), a century before [Vettius Valens](/astrology/vettius-valens/) set them out at length in prose.

What I take from him is a reminder. Nothing of the man survives except his rules, and they reached us through more than one language and many centuries of copying. Rules that have travelled that far deserve respect and a check, not obedience; my own check was a notebook, a tick or a cross beside every placement ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

From around 800 his book could be read in Arabic among the astrologers of Baghdad, and its later history belongs to [Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/). Its first home was [Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/).
