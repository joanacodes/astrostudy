Fomalhaut is the bright star in the mouth of the Southern Fish, best seen low over the southern horizon in autumn from Europe. In 2026 it stands at 4°13′ Pisces, the last of the four royal stars in astrology books, and the one whose crown rests on the shakiest ground.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Fomalhaut is α Piscis Austrini, magnitude 1.16, and one of the Sun's near neighbours at about 25 light-years. Ptolemy counted it as part of Aquarius; Johann Bayer gave it to the Southern Fish in the seventeenth century, and that is where it stays. From 40° north it climbs only 20° above the horizon and is up for eight hours, so from Britain or France it never rises far.

Astronomers know it for its wide rings of dust. Fomalhaut b, the first candidate planet ever photographed in visible light, now looks like an expanding cloud of debris from a collision rather than a planet.

In the tropical zodiac it has moved from 2°52′ [Pisces](/astrology/pisces/) in 1930 through 3°51′ in 2000 to 4°13′ in 2026, so everyone born in those years has it in the opening degrees of Pisces. Its latitude, 21.1° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), matters more than the degree. When the Sun reaches Fomalhaut's longitude in late February, it is passing more than twenty degrees to the north of the star: the contact is a line drawn along the zodiac, not a meeting in the sky.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Its name is Arabic, fam al-ḥūt, the mouth of the fish, a translation of the way Ptolemy described its place in the figure; the Latin names say the same, *os piscis meridiani*, the mouth of the southern fish. Early Arab sky-watchers had a folk name for it too, the first frog.

Fomalhaut's royal title comes from R. H. Allen's *Star Names* (1899), which places it among the Persians' four royal stars with [Antares](/astrology/antares/), Aldebaran and Regulus. The Persian sources are less obliging. The Zoroastrian texts do set a commander over the southern quarter of the sky, Sadwēsh, and older scholars identified him with Fomalhaut, or else with Canopus. Both identifications have been challenged, and in 1942 W. B. Henning argued that the southern commander was most likely Antares. Of the four royal stars, the Southern Fish's is the crown most in doubt.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The [Sun conjunct Fomalhaut](/astrology/sun-conjunct-fomalhaut/) belongs to late-February birthdays, about the 21st and 22nd: roughly 1 person in 183 from 1930 to 2009, a Sun in the first degrees of Pisces. [Moon conjunct Fomalhaut](/astrology/moon-conjunct-fomalhaut/) is about as common, 1 in 184; Venus and Mars meet it less often, [Venus conjunct Fomalhaut](/astrology/venus-conjunct-fomalhaut/) about 1 in 216 and [Mars conjunct Fomalhaut](/astrology/mars-conjunct-fomalhaut/) 1 in 210; [Saturn conjunct Fomalhaut](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-fomalhaut/) about 1 in 183, for births from March to November 1935, from April 1964 to January 1965, or in February and March 1994.

A crown in doubt, a contact made by degree alone, and a system for my readings that places no stars: three reasons this page describes Fomalhaut rather than reads it. The rule the tradition uses is set out in [conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/), and if Fomalhaut sits on one of your planets, that planet's own sign and house will say far more about you than the mouth of a fish ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).
