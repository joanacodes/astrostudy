For a time after its discovery, Varuna was the largest body known in the classical Kuiper belt, large enough to be given a round number, 20000. Its namesake is one of the oldest gods of the Vedic hymns, and he is still honoured on India's western coast.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Varuna circles the Sun every 279 years on an almost circular path, between 40.3 and 45.1 times the Earth's distance, tilted 17° to the Sun's path, close to [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/)'s tilt. It keeps clear of any rhythm with [Neptune](/astrology/neptune/), which makes it a classical Kuiper belt object, unlike the plutinos [Ixion](/astrology/ixion/) and Orcus. Its last close pass came in 1932; its farthest point comes in 2073.

It turns in about 6.34 hours, quickly for its size, and the spin has drawn it out lengthwise; [Haumea](/astrology/haumea/), more than twice its size, spins faster still. That shape makes Varuna hard to measure. A star it passed in front of in February 2010, watched from Brazil, gave a chord of about 1,003 km, probably along its longest axis, while occultations in 2013 and 2014 and infrared measurements point to an average near 670 km.

Its surface is moderately red, with water ice that collisions may have exposed. The density, estimated at just under that of water, suggests a loose, porous interior, and a light curve studied in 2019 hints at a moon orbiting too close to be seen.

Robert McMillan found it on 28 November 2000 with the Spacewatch telescope at Kitt Peak, Arizona. The survey software missed it; he caught it by comparing scans by eye. In January 2001 the Minor Planet Center gave it the number 20000, to mark its size and the 200th anniversary of the discovery of [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/), which fell that same month.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Varuna is among the oldest gods of the Rigveda, the earliest Hindu scripture, though only ten of its hymns are his alone. There he is a sovereign of the sky, credited with laying out the path of the Sun, and the stars are his watchful eyes. Above all he upholds ṛta, the order that governs both the cosmos and human conduct: he punishes falsehood and broken vows, yet is begged for forgiveness, and a hymn gives him a thousand remedies along with his weapons.

Over the centuries his realm moved from the sky to the waters. In the epics and Puranas he is lord of oceans and rivers, with a palace under the sea, guardian of the west, riding the makara and holding a noose. He is honoured still: on Narali Poornima, the full moon of the month of Shravan, fishing communities on the coast of Maharashtra offer him rice, flowers and coconuts.

The name came from the Indian choreographer Mrinalini Sarabhai, and the IAU approved it in March 2001, in keeping with its rule that bodies on orbits like this one take the names of creator gods. Another water god's name went farther out, to [Gonggong](/astrology/gonggong/), from Chinese myth.

There is no standard astrological symbol. Denis Moskowitz proposed one built from the Devanagari letter va, व, and Varuna's snake-noose, and a few astrology websites carry it; a variant of Neptune's glyph is sometimes seen instead, treating Varuna as Neptune's Hindu counterpart. In a chart, its near-circular orbit would carry it through the signs at an almost steady pace, a little over two decades to each.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Varuna is a god people still pray to, and the name reached the sky through an Indian artist rather than an astronomer's search for a story. If I proposed a meaning from it, I'd start where the hymns start, with truth-telling and the keeping of vows, before the sea. It would stay a proposal: Varuna has no settled meaning in astrology, not even a settled glyph.

Varuna has no place in the charts I prepare ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). My notebook didn't cover it either. Of what I did check, about a third held ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)), and nothing in that count speaks for a body astrology has had only since 2000.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Varuna is simply the Hindu Neptune.”** That is his later role. In the oldest hymns he is a sovereign of the sky and of cosmic order; the waters came to him over time.
- **“Varuna was the 20,000th asteroid found.”** Its number was chosen, once its orbit was secure, to honour its size and the bicentenary of Ceres.
- **“Varuna and Uranus are one god.”** In 1934 Georges Dumézil made a cautious case that Varuna and Ouranos, the Greek sky god whose Latin name the planet [Uranus](/astrology/uranus/) bears, shared an origin. The link between their names is now widely rejected, though parallels in their roles are still discussed.
