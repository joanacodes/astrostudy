Three house systems divide the sky by time, and what sets them apart is whose time they measure. Placidus times every degree, Koch the Midheaven's degree, and Alcabitius, the oldest of the three, the Ascendant's.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Alcabitius leaves the four angles where they are, the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) on the 1st cusp and the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) on the 10th, and times only what lies between. For those cusps it takes a single degree, the one rising at birth, and asks how long that degree will take to climb from the eastern horizon to the meridian. That stretch of time is cut into three equal parts, and the thirds fix the cusps between the two angles.

It is the forerunner of [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/), which divides each degree's own climb in the same way, and it largely took the place of [Porphyry](/astrology/porphyry/), which divides the zodiac rather than time. In medieval Europe it was very popular until [Regiomontanus](/astrology/regiomontanus/) arrived.

The name is the Latin form of al-Qabisi, Abu al-Saqr Abd al-Aziz ibn Uthman (died 967), an astrologer, astronomer and mathematician who lived and worked at the palace of Sayf al-Dawla, the Hamdanid ruler of Aleppo. His *Introduction to the Art of Judgments of the Stars*, dedicated to Sayf al-Dawla, was put into Latin by John of Seville in 1144. It survives in at least twenty-five Arabic manuscripts and more than two hundred Latin ones, and twelve printed editions of the Latin appeared between 1473 and 1521 ([Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Timing one degree rather than every degree is what defines Alcabitius. In Placidus, each intermediate cusp is a degree that has made its own third or two thirds of the climb. In Alcabitius, the Ascendant's climb is the yardstick, and the other cusps are set by it. The further from the equator, the more the climbing times of different degrees diverge, so the more the two systems part.

Its neighbours in the history of European houses show the range of answers. [Campanus](/astrology/campanus/) divides space instead: the dome of the sky cut into equal slices around the zenith. [Morinus](/astrology/morinus/), in the 17th century, divided the celestial equator and left the horizon out altogether. Alcabitius stays with the horizon and with time, but asks only one degree for its time.

The idea came back in the 20th century. When Walter Koch put forward his birthplace system in the early 1960s, its critics answered that, for all the claims of originality, it was a simple modification of Alcabitius: Koch times the Midheaven's degree where Alcabitius times the Ascendant's ([Koch](/astrology/koch/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Alcabitius makes it easy to say why I use Placidus. The two share a principle, angles on the cusps and time as the measure; where they part is that Placidus lets each degree keep its own pace, and that is the measure I want for houses built from a birth time ([which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/)). Alcabitius chose a single, sensible yardstick, the rising degree, and generations of astrologers found it good enough. It is a coherent answer, not a crude one.

A medieval chart drawn in Alcabitius is still legible today: its angles are the ones Placidus would give, and only the cusps between them need a second look ([house cusps](/astrology/house-cusps/)). And since its yardstick is the Ascendant, one of the fastest-moving points in any chart, it needs the birth time as much as Placidus does ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Alcabitius is an Arabic system that never reached Europe.”** It was very popular in medieval Europe, before Regiomontanus took over.
- **“Koch came first and Alcabitius copied it.”** The other way round: Koch's system was described as a modification of Alcabitius.
- **“Alcabitius divides the zodiac into equal houses.”** It divides time, the Ascendant degree's climb to the meridian, so its houses vary in size.
