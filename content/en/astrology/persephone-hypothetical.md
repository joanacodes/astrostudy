Persephone is the planet beyond Pluto that fiction kept finding and astronomy never did. Astrologers have wanted it too, under her Latin name, as the ruler a sign was supposed to be missing.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Once Pluto was found in 1930, the next planet out became a fixture of science fiction, and the name it was given most often was Persephone: in Jack Williamson's story “The Blue Spot” of 1937, in Arthur C. Clarke's “Rescue Party” of 1946 and in his novel *Rendezvous with Rama* of 1973, among others. Astronomers searched beyond Neptune in earnest, a story with its own page ([Planet X and Planet Nine](/astrology/planet-x-and-planet-nine/)). Astrologers have pictured more than one planet out there, too ([Transpluto (Isis)](/astrology/transpluto-isis/)).

The name had a logic. Persephone, daughter of Zeus and Demeter, was carried off by Hades, the god the Romans called Pluto, and became queen of the underworld; her return to the surface each year brought the spring. As Pluto's consort, she was the natural name for whatever lay beyond him, in fiction and among astronomers alike.

Astronomers came close to using it twice. When Pluto's moon was found in 1978, colleagues of its discoverer at the US Naval Observatory proposed Persephone; he kept to Charon. When Eris was found, Mike Brown considered Persephone, which had just won a poll run by *New Scientist*, but an asteroid found in 1895 already had it ([Persephone (asteroid 399)](/astrology/persephone-asteroid/)), and Eris was being named as a minor planet too.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

In astrology the planet turns up under her Latin name. Some astrologers have held that every sign will one day have a ruler of its own, which leaves two still to be found: one for [Taurus](/astrology/taurus/), which shares Venus with Libra, and one for Gemini, which shares Mercury with Virgo. [Dane Rudhyar](/astrology/dane-rudhyar/) proposed that the true ruler of Taurus is an undiscovered planet beyond Neptune, and named it Proserpine.

In Poland the name took another path. Polish astrologers, and the astrology program Urania, work with a Proserpina they also call Kora, and draw it with the symbol ♁; in cult, Persephone herself was often simply Kore, the Maiden. They identify it with [Eris](/astrology/eris/), the dwarf planet found in 2005, so for them the hypothetical planet has a body after all: one astronomers named after a different goddess.

Fiction kept the joke going. In the BBC radio version of Douglas Adams's *Mostly Harmless*, a newly found tenth planet called Persephone, nicknamed Rupert, sends a reporter to ask an astrologer what it will do to astrology.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Persephone isn't in my readings, under either name. Taurus has a ruler in them, [Venus](/astrology/venus/), as Libra does. The three signs that have both a modern and a traditional ruler, Scorpio, Aquarius and Pisces, get both from me, because I read both; I don't keep a seat free for a planet nobody has found.

Nor do I read Eris, the body Polish astrologers call Proserpina. Giving a hypothetical planet a real body doesn't change my answer: my readings rest on the planets, Chiron, Lilith and the nodes, and a dwarf planet found in 2005 hasn't joined them ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Persephone was found, and renamed Eris.”** Brown considered the name for Eris, but asteroid 399 already had it; only some astrologers treat Eris as their Proserpina.
- **“Taurus has no proper ruler.”** It has Venus, in the rulerships Ptolemy set down; the wish for a planet of its own is a modern one.
- **“The Persephone in the asteroid tables is this planet.”** Those are 399 Persephone and [Proserpina (26)](/astrology/proserpina-26/), small bodies of the main belt between Mars and Jupiter.
