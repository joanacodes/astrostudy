Tycho Brahe is remembered as the astronomer who measured the sky better than anyone before the telescope. In his own lifetime he was known as an astrologer and alchemist too, and what he really thought of the astrology is still argued over.

## Who {#who}

He was born Tyge Ottesen Brahe on 14 December 1546 at Knutstorp Castle, in Scania, then part of Denmark, heir to several noble families. At twelve he entered the University of Copenhagen, and there the solar eclipse of 21 August 1560 made a lasting impression: it had been predicted, but the prediction was a day off, and he concluded that better forecasts of the sky needed better observations of it.

In 1563, on his student travels, he watched a close conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn and found that both the Copernican and the Ptolemaic tables had predicted it badly ([Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/)). He began a journal of every observation, and in the same years drew up horoscopes of famous people. In April 1567 he came home, his biographer writes, with a firm intention of becoming an astrologer.

On 11 November 1572 he saw a brilliant new star in Cassiopeia. It showed no daily parallax and kept its place among the fixed stars, so it lay far beyond the Moon, in the heavens Aristotle's philosophy declared unchangeable. His little book *De nova stella* (1573) coined the word nova and scolded those who refused to look: “O thick wits. O blind watchers of the sky.”

King Frederick II gave him the island of Hven, where he began building Uraniborg in 1576, and Tycho served the crown as its astrologer in return. After losing favour under Christian IV he left in 1597, and from 1599 he worked in Prague for the emperor Rudolf II. There his duties to patrons included charts for births, weather forecasts ([astrometeorology](/astrology/astrometeorology/)) and astrological readings of the new star and of the great comet of 1577. He died in Prague on 24 October 1601.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He kept his astrology out of his own name. Two of his most astrological treatises, one on weather predictions and an almanac, appeared under his assistants' names, though he had worked on them himself. Historians disagree about why. Some think he lost faith in horoscopes over his career; others that he only changed what he said in public, because a link with astrology could harm the reception of his astronomy. I've taken the opposite course with my own small test: the count is public, misses included ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

He changed the sky astrology stood on. A new star where nothing was supposed to change, and a comet he showed to be no vapour of the air, broke up the Aristotelian heavens, and with them the picture of a cosmos in which astrology made easy sense. Within little more than a century it had lost its place among the learned ([the decline after the 17th century](/astrology/decline-after-the-17th-century/)).

He made the planets' places exact. Working with his assistant in Prague, he began the star catalogue built on his own positions that became the Rudolphine Tables, and the ephemerides computed from tables of that kind are what every chart is drawn from ([ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)). Before he left Hven he had also finished a catalogue of 1,000 stars. I describe the fixed stars on this site rather than read them ([fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)).

A century before him, in Florence, [Marsilio Ficino](/astrology/marsilio-ficino/) had treated astrology as philosophy and medicine; Tycho treated the sky first as something to measure. His observations, and the unfinished tables, passed to the assistant who had worked beside him in Prague, [Johannes Kepler](/astrology/johannes-kepler/), and the wider story of their century is [Renaissance astrology](/astrology/renaissance-astrology/).
