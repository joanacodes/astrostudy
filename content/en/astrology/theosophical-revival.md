Astrology came back into English-speaking life through a religious movement. Theosophy gave it a philosophy and a network of readers, and Alan Leo gave it a new task: describing a person instead of announcing what would happen to them.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

The Theosophical Society was formed in New York on 17 November 1875 by Helena Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott, William Quan Judge and sixteen others, drawing on Neoplatonism and occultism as well as on Hinduism and Buddhism. Several of the astrologers who brought astrology back after its long eclipse ([the decline after the 17th century](/astrology/decline-after-the-17th-century/)) were theosophists. Walter Gorn Old, who wrote as Sepharial, was one, and so was Alan Leo.

[Alan Leo](/astrology/alan-leo/), born William Frederick Allan in 1860, wrote and published book after book: the *Astrology for All* series from 1903, *The Progressed Horoscope* in 1906, *The Art of Synthesis* in 1912, *Esoteric Astrology* in 1913. He travelled to India in 1909 and 1911 to study Indian astrology ([Vedic astrology (Jyotish)](/astrology/vedic-astrology-jyotish/)). *The Art of Synthesis*, which gave the planets titles such as “[Mars](/astrology/mars/) the Energiser”, was probably one of the influences on Gustav Holst's suite *The Planets*. In 1915 he founded the Astrological Lodge of London.

His real change was to the purpose of a reading. Leo is credited with being the first astrologer to argue for a loose interpretation of possible trends rather than the specific prediction of events. The historian James Holden describes what followed: “event-oriented” astrology gradually receded in favour of character analysis.

The law helped push him there. In 1914 he was prosecuted for pretending to tell fortunes; the case was dismissed for lack of evidence, but it convinced him that astrology needed revising. “Let us part company with the fatalistic astrologer,” he urged other astrologers, and “call Astrology the science of tendencies.” In 1917 he was tried again and, although he insisted that he told tendencies and not fortunes, he was convicted on 16 July and fined £5 plus costs. He died on 30 August that year, at Bude, in the middle of rewriting his work to run “more along the lines of character reading and less as the assertion of an inevitable destiny”. In New York the courts were kinder: [Evangeline Adams](/astrology/evangeline-adams/), arrested for fortune-telling in 1911, 1914 and 1923, was never convicted, and at her trial of May 1914 the judge acquitted her of all wrongdoing.

Popular astrology took another road. In August 1930 the *Sunday Express* printed a horoscope by R. H. Naylor; the paper gave him a regular column, other British newspapers soon had their own, and by 1937 his column, *Your Stars*, gave a forecast for each of the twelve Sun signs.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

The idea that a chart describes a character, not a fate. I'd put it the same way today: a chart describes how you're inclined to move, not where you must end up ([how I read](/how-i-read/)). Leo reached it under legal pressure as well as by conviction, and it outlived him.

Astrology in print for everyone. Leo's books and magazines put the chart in ordinary hands, and he wrote on timing as well as on the birth chart, in *The Progressed Horoscope* ([secondary progressions](/astrology/secondary-progressions/)).

The Sun-sign column, too: the [horoscope](/astrology/horoscope/) of the newspapers, built on the [Sun](/astrology/sun/) alone. I'm wary of it. In my notebook the Sun sign, one of the placements everyone reads first, held about a third of the time, while Saturn, Chiron and Lilith held far more often ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). A whole chart says more than its Sun.

Leo is also credited with starting the movement towards a more psychological reading of the chart. The next generation took that word literally: [psychological astrology](/astrology/psychological-astrology/).
