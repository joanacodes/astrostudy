If you're reading this because it happened to you, or to someone you love: you're far from the first, and there is still something useful to do.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Stop paying, call your bank, keep every trace, and report it in two places: to the national fraud service where you live, and to the platform where the contact began. Then say it out loud to one person close to you.

Where to report, as the services themselves describe it:

- **England, Wales and Northern Ireland:** Report Fraud, the national reporting service for fraud and financially motivated cyber crime, run for the police. It replaced Action Fraud on 4 December 2025, and a report receives a crime reference number.
- **Canada:** the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, the national anti-fraud police service. You can report online, anonymously if you choose, or by telephone; local police investigate, and the centre passes them what reports reveal.
- **United States:** the Federal Trade Commission, which takes reports from consumers about fraud.
- **Across the European Union:** if the trader is based in another member state, a European Consumer Centre in your own country can help with the dispute.
- **Anywhere else, or if you're unsure:** the police where you live.

## Why it matters {#why-it-matters}

Most frauds are never reported. In Canada, the national anti-fraud centre and its partners estimate that between one in twenty and one in ten reaches them. The rest stay invisible, and so do the people behind them. A report adds a name, a number or an account to what the services know, and in the United Kingdom the national service can ask for websites, phone lines and bank accounts used by fraudsters to be taken down.

Shame is what keeps most people quiet, and it's misplaced. These schemes are built to find people at their most open, after a loss, in a lonely stretch, when an explanation feels like relief ([Curse removal scams](/astrology/curse-removal-scams/)). Silence protects the seller, never you.

They're also very old. In the second century, Ptolemy already complained of people who traded on astrology's name for gain and claimed to foretell what can't be known (*Tetrabiblos*, book I; see [Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)). Whatever name a scheme uses, the method gives it away: fear, a fee, then another ([Fake psychics](/astrology/fake-psychics/)). What no honest reader sells, the lifting of a curse for money among it, is on my own list of refusals ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## What to do instead {#what-to-do-instead}

1. **Stop paying,** including the "last" fee that's supposed to finish the work or release a refund ([Paid “spiritual work”](/astrology/paid-spiritual-work/)).
2. **Call your bank or card issuer** at once: say what happened, and ask whether anything can be done about payments already made, and how to stop the next.
3. **Gather the evidence** before anything disappears: how the contact began, every date and amount, payment references, usernames, phone numbers, email addresses and websites, screenshots of the conversations, and any threats about what would happen if you stopped. Keep the originals.
4. **Report it** to the national service where you live, and to the platform, app or site where it started, so the account can be looked at.
5. **If you gave your birth details, or someone else's,** treat them as personal data that has leaked, and ask the company to delete them ([Your birth details are personal data](/astrology/your-birth-details-are-personal-data/)).
6. **Be wary of anyone who then offers to get your money back for a fee.** Some scammers return to their own victims with exactly that offer.

If it was a subscription rather than an outright fraud, cancelling and complaining to a consumer body may be the better route ([Subscription traps](/astrology/subscription-traps/)). And if what happened has left you shaken, anxious or unable to stop going over it, that deserves care of its own ([When to see a professional instead](/astrology/when-to-see-a-professional-instead/)).
