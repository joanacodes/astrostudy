Two planets pulling against each other across the chart, and a third caught between them, square to both: that's a T-square, one of the most common patterns there is and one of the most productive. It's tension with somewhere to go.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

Start with an [opposition](/astrology/opposition/): two planets facing each other, about 180° apart. Put a third planet halfway between them on one side, about 90° from each, so that it makes a [square](/astrology/square/) to both ends. Draw the lines and you get a T, or a right-angled triangle whose long side cuts the chart in two. The third planet, the focal or apex planet, sits where the two squares meet. Opposite it, on the far side of the chart, lies an empty point: the place a fourth planet would have to occupy to close the figure into a [grand cross](/astrology/grand-cross/).

Signs three and six apart share a modality, so a T-square usually falls in three signs of one: cardinal, fixed or mutable. The two ends of the opposition sit in complementary elements, and the apex in a third element of the same modality. Near the edge of a sign, one point of the T can slip into a sign of another modality, and the pattern loosens a little.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Three planets are all a T-square takes, which is why it's so common: about 3 people in 10 born from 1930 to 2009 have one among the ten planets in the count behind this site, squares and oppositions allowed 8° when the Sun or Moon is involved and 6° otherwise. That's before adding the Ascendant, the Midheaven, Chiron, Lilith or the nodes. The horizon is itself an opposition, so a planet square to both the Ascendant and the Descendant makes a T-square with them, and the count can't see it. Change the orbs by a couple of degrees and the share changes a great deal.

I read a T-square as an engine. An opposition on its own can swing between its two ends for years; the focal planet turns that swing into effort in one direction. The system I built for my readings calls the square constructive friction, friction that develops rather than defeats, so the apex is where you work hardest and where much of your competence comes from. Its function says what kind of effort: an apex Mars acts and pushes, an apex Mercury plans and talks it through, an apex Moon feels all of it first. When Saturn or Chiron sits at the apex, I read it most slowly, because they're two of [the three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/) far more often than the rest in my notebook, 75 to 90% of the time.

Many astrologers also look at the empty point opposite the apex, the T-square's missing leg, as the place where its strain can be released: the sign and house where balance tends to come from. That house, like the houses of the three planets, needs the birth time, and so does any T-square with the Moon in it. A pattern is read through the planets, signs and houses it joins; without the time, half of that is missing, which is why I [wait for the time before reading](/blog/unknown-birth-time/).

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Find the focal planet first.** Everything in the figure runs through it. Knowing which function carries the load tells you where to put your effort, and where to protect your energy.
- **Use the empty point.** Its sign and house describe a kind of activity the T-square doesn't do by itself. Practising it deliberately is the oldest advice there is about this pattern.
- **Let the opposition talk.** The two ends aren't enemies; they're two needs that both deserve a hearing before the apex acts.
- **Pace the engine.** A T-square can run hot. Choosing when to push is part of mastering it.

Its neighbour in this family, the [Yod (finger of God)](/astrology/yod-finger-of-god/), also has a planet at its point, but one that adjusts rather than pushes. A T-square is rarely comfortable and often useful: much of what people with one do well was learned at its apex.
