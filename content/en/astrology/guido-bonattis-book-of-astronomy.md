The *Liber astronomiae*, the Book of Astronomy, is the manual in which [Guido Bonatti](/astrology/guido-bonatti/), the most famous astrologer of his century, set out his art in Latin around 1277. Its title says astronomy; its subject is what we now call astrology. The historian James Lewis calls it the most important astrological work produced in Latin in the thirteenth century.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Bonatti wrote it about a century and a half after [Abu Ma’shar’s introductions](/astrology/abu-mashars-introductions/) were first put into Latin, and it draws on what those translations had brought. The astrologers who had written in Arabic, Sahl ibn Bishr, known in Latin as Zael, among them, shaped his work directly ([Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/)). By his day, Latin readers had been studying the Arabic astrologers for well over a hundred years ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)).

Its name varies with the copy: *Liber astronomiae*, *Liber astronomicus*, and in print the ten treatises. It circulated in manuscript for two centuries and kept its standing; King Henry VII of England owned a copy at the end of the fifteenth century. Print came in 1491, at Augsburg, from Erhard Ratdolt's press, in an edition by the physician and astronomer Johannes Engel, whose title announced ten treatises, *Decem continens tractatus astronomie*.

English readers met part of it in the seventeenth century. Bonatti's 146 “considerations”, translated from the Latin by Henry Coley, came out with William Lilly's *Anima Astrologiae*, dated 1676 in the list of Lilly's works and 1675 in another bibliography, and were reprinted with notes in 1886 as *The Astrologer's Guide*. Lilly had already named Bonatti's ten treatises in the appendix of [William Lilly’s Christian Astrology (1647)](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/), among the books he had worked from. Benjamin Dykes published an English translation, *The Book of Astronomy*, in 2007.

## What it says {#what-it-says}

It hands on the dignities. The table of the planets' strengths by sign found in [Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/) and in [Vettius Valens’ Anthology](/astrology/vettius-valens-anthology/), and printed again by Lilly four centuries later, stands in Bonatti too, give or take its finer columns ([The table of dignities](/astrology/table-of-dignities/)). What changes from book to book is how the columns are weighed, not the idea that a planet works more easily in some signs than in others.

It also divides a life into periods. Bonatti describes the [firdaria](/astrology/firdaria/), the stretches of years ruled in turn by the planets and then by the Moon's nodes ([Nodal axis](/astrology/nodal-axis/)), a technique that had reached Europe through Latin translations of Arabic works, and his account became influential in later European practice. On night births he is less explicit than his sources. The night order, he says, follows the day order “in all respects”, and some later readers took those words to put the nodes straight after Mars, so that from the age of 39 the two versions name different rulers for the same life. Firdaria aren't among the techniques I time a life with, but anyone who uses them should know which version their software follows.

That is how a phrase becomes two traditions. Old books are full of such places, where a technique hangs on how one sentence is read, and the honest thing is to say so rather than settle it quietly. It's the rule I keep for my own readings: when something rests on less, I tell you, and you weigh it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

The rest of Bonatti's reputation, the princes he served, the campaigns he timed and the place Dante gave him in hell, belongs to the man. The book outlived all of it: it was still a standard textbook two hundred years after he wrote it, and it is still being translated.
