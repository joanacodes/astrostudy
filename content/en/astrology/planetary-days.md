Every time you say Monday or Saturday, or lundi and mardi in French, you name a planet. The seven-day week took its names from the seven traditional planets, in an order that looks random until you know the astrology underneath it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Each day of the week has a ruler: the [Sun](/astrology/sun/) for Sunday, the [Moon](/astrology/moon/) for Monday, then Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus and Saturn. In Latin the names were plain: *dies Solis*, *dies Lunae*, *dies Martis*, *dies Mercurii*, *dies Iovis*, *dies Veneris*, *dies Saturni*. French kept five of them, lundi to vendredi, and replaced the two ends with the Lord's day (*dimanche*) and the Sabbath (*samedi*). English kept the Sun, the Moon and Saturn, and gave Tuesday to Friday to Germanic gods matched with the Roman ones: Tiw for Mars, Woden for Mercury, Thunor for Jupiter, Frige for Venus.

The order puzzled people early. It isn't the order of the planets in the sky, which astrology ranks from Saturn down to the Moon ([Chaldean order](/astrology/chaldean-order/)). Around AD 100, Plutarch is reported to have written a treatise on why the days named after the planets follow a different order from the real one; it is lost. Vettius Valens in the 2nd century and Dio Cassius in the early 3rd both explained it through the hours.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

In the old scheme, each hour of the day is ruled by a planet, in the Chaldean order, and a day takes its name from the planet of its first hour ([planetary hours](/astrology/planetary-hours/)). Count it through a Saturday. Saturn rules the 1st hour, and again the 8th, the 15th and the 22nd; Jupiter takes the 23rd and Mars the 24th. The 25th hour, the first of the next day, falls to the Sun, and that day is Sunday. Run the same count from Sunday and the next first hour lands on the Moon. The whole week falls out of that arithmetic.

When a day begins is part of the system. Modern tropical astrology starts the planetary day at sunrise, so the night belongs to the day before it: a birth at four in the morning on a Tuesday, before dawn, still falls in the Moon's day. Vettius Valens started the count at sunset instead, as Greeks and Babylonians did, and the two reckonings don't give the same name to the same day. A graffito at Pompeii calls 6 February AD 60 a Sunday; by the sunrise reckoning it was a Wednesday.

The planetary week spread through the Roman world, alongside the older eight-day market cycle, until Constantine made the seven-day week official in AD 321. Where it began is still argued over: the usual account makes it Hellenistic, while Ilaria Bultrighini and Sascha Stern argue that it appeared earlier in Roman Italy, perhaps prompted by the Jewish week.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The weekday you were born on isn't part of my reading. One birth in seven shares it, and your chart already shows where each of the seven planets actually stood when you were born, by sign, house and aspect: [Venus in the 7th house](/astrology/venus-in-7th-house/) says more about you than being born on a Friday. The [seven traditional planets](/astrology/seven-traditional-planets/) are in every chart whatever the day.

Planetary days belong to the calendar side of the tradition. Renaissance books of magic matched tasks to each planet's days and hours, and some astrologers who practise [electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/), the art of choosing a moment to begin something, still consult them. I describe it here because it explains the week you live in, not because I time anything by it; [how I read](/how-i-read/) lists what I use instead.

If you like to know your birth day's planet, the sunrise rule is the detail to check: before dawn, the previous day's planet still rules, so even here the [birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) settles it.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Your birth weekday is a hidden part of your chart.”** It is a calendar fact, shared by one birth in seven. The chart shows the planets themselves.
- **“Astrologers invented the week.”** The seven-day week has more than one root, the Jewish week among them. The planetary names are astrology's layer on top, and historians still debate where and when it was laid.
- **“A planetary day starts at midnight.”** It starts at sunrise, so the small hours belong to the day before.
