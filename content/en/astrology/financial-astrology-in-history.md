People have looked for money in the sky for about as long as they have looked for anything there. This page follows that search as history, because history is all it is on this site: I don't read markets, investments or financial decisions.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Financial astrology, also called business astrology, economic astrology or astro-economics, is the attempt to relate the movements of the planets to markets, prices and economies. It has no empirical basis, and its claims sit at plain odds with how economists and financial theory understand markets.

Its roots are in [mundane astrology](/astrology/mundane-astrology/), the astrology of nations. In the second book of the *Tetrabiblos*, Ptolemy set out how eclipses, comets and the new and full moons before each season were used to predict national economics, wars, epidemics, natural disasters and the weather ([Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)). The economy sat beside the weather in the same forecasts, which is why the history of money in the sky runs close to the history of [astrometeorology](/astrology/astrometeorology/).

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

Or how its practitioners have done it, in three broad periods:

- **The mundane tradition.** Charts for eclipses, lunations and the Sun's entry into the cardinal signs, read for a country and its year, with the economy among the things foretold.
- **The newsletter era.** In the early twentieth century, [Evangeline Adams](/astrology/evangeline-adams/), an astrologer based in New York, ran a large consulting practice and a newsletter, and had a reputation for calling the stock market. W. D. Gann, a trader born in 1878, sold market newsletters from 1919; his forecasting methods are said to draw on geometry, astronomy, astrology and time cycles, and in a private letter of 1954 he showed a student how he used planetary cycles to forecast the coffee market.
- **The modern trade.** Financial astrology became a niche service: in 2000 Bloomberg News even hosted a weekly show about it, and most of the demand was reported to come from the United States and Japan. Large financial firms have tended to ignore it.

A cousin from outside astrology is worth knowing. In 1875 the economist William Stanley Jevons read a paper on the influence of the sunspot period on the price of corn, reasoning that sunspots affected the weather, the weather the crops, and the crops the economy; the word *sunspottery* was coined for claims of that kind. That was a scientific hypothesis about the Sun's activity, not astrology, and it shows how attractive the idea of prices written in the sky has been.

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

Mostly how forecasts survive. Critics of Adams noted that her predictions were fuzzy, foretelling disaster without naming one, and that believers remembered her hits and forgot her misses; thousands of subscribers to her newsletter followed her advice to invest in stocks in the run-up to the crash of 1929. Gann's son later told the writer Alexander Elder that his father couldn't support the family by trading and earned his living by writing and selling courses.

Critics of financial astrology make two more points. Its predictions are vague and rarely give a date. And some of the configurations it leans on are so rare that they may never have happened before within a human lifetime, so there was nothing to learn from ([confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/)).

## Its limits {#its-limits}

I don't read money in a chart: investments and financial decisions are on my list of [what I won't read](/how-i-read/), and [Can astrology pick investments?](/astrology/can-astrology-pick-investments/) explains why. The [2nd house](/astrology/2nd-house/) can say something about your own ways with money; no part of a chart can say what a share, a currency or a house will be worth, or when to buy or sell. For that, the person to see is a qualified, independent adviser.

Its history belongs beside that of [medical astrology](/astrology/medical-astrology-in-history/): both are stories of the sky asked to answer questions that belong to other kinds of knowledge.
