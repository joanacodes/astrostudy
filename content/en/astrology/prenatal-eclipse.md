Some astrologers look a step further back than the new or full moon before a birth: to the last eclipse. Every birth has one, solar and lunar, because the sky never goes long without an eclipse.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

An eclipse is a new or full moon that falls close enough to the Moon's nodes for the Sun, the Earth and the Moon to line up exactly ([why eclipses follow the nodes](/astrology/why-eclipses-follow-the-nodes/)). At a [solar eclipse](/astrology/solar-eclipses/) the Moon passes in front of the Sun, both at one degree of the zodiac; at a [lunar eclipse](/astrology/lunar-eclipses/) the Moon, opposite the Sun, passes through the Earth's shadow. Counted separately, every birth has a last solar eclipse and a last lunar eclipse, and those are its prenatal eclipses. If the last new or full moon before a birth was itself eclipsed, the [prenatal lunation](/astrology/prenatal-lunation/) and the prenatal eclipse are one and the same.

The degree belongs to the eclipse rather than to the birth. Everyone born between one solar eclipse and the next carries the same prenatal solar eclipse: a whole season of births. What makes it differ from chart to chart is the house it falls in and the planets near it, and the house needs the birth time.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Eclipses come in [eclipse seasons](/astrology/eclipse-seasons/), about every six months, when the Sun is near one of the nodes. Each season lasts about five weeks and brings at least one solar and one lunar eclipse. Worked through for 1900 to 2100, that gives some firm limits:

- **Solar eclipses** are never more than 178 days apart, so a prenatal solar eclipse is never much more than six months old.
- **Lunar eclipses** keep the same limit if you count the penumbral ones, when the Moon only grazes the Earth's outer shadow. Count only partial and total eclipses, and the gap can stretch to 680 days, nearly two years.
- **Any eclipse at all** comes within 163 days of the last one, and every year has between four and seven.

A worked example shows how much is shared. For a birth in March 1965, the last solar eclipse was the partial one of 4 December 1964, at 11°56′ Sagittarius, and the last lunar eclipse the total one of 19 December 1964, with the Moon at 27°11′ Gemini and the Sun opposite at 27°11′ Sagittarius, less than a degree from the [Galactic Centre](/astrology/galactic-centre/) as it stood then. Everyone born between 19 December 1964 and 30 May 1965 has those same two prenatal eclipses.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The eclipses I read come after birth, not before it. When an eclipse falls within about three degrees of a natal planet or angle, at either end of its axis, I read a period of emphasis for what that point stands for, alongside the transits and progressions of the same months ([eclipses on your natal planets](/astrology/eclipses-on-your-natal-planets/)). The prenatal eclipse isn't part of my readings: a degree shared by a season's worth of births tells me less about you than the planets do.

An eclipse is weather, not a forecast, and that holds for the one before your birth as much as for the ones after. It doesn't mark a fate, a debt or a danger, and nobody can tell you otherwise from your chart. If you want to look at your prenatal eclipses for yourself, read them through the house they fall in and any planet close to their degree, and leave the old omens in the old books: what a chart shows are tendencies, never a fate ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Being born just after an eclipse is unlucky.”** Every birth follows an eclipse by weeks or months; there are four to seven a year.
- **“The prenatal eclipse is always a solar one.”** There is a last solar eclipse and a last lunar one before every birth, and some astrologers use both.
- **“It's yours alone.”** Everyone born before the next eclipse of the same kind shares it; the house is what's yours.
- **“A penumbral eclipse isn't an eclipse.”** It is: the Moon passes through the Earth's outer shadow. Leaving penumbral eclipses out can move a prenatal lunar eclipse back by more than a year.
