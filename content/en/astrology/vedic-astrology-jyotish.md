Jyotish is the astrology of India, and the one most often set beside mine. The two share ancestors and a few words, and they part company over where the zodiac begins and over most of the techniques built on it.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

It began as timekeeping. *Jyotiṣa*, from a word for light and the heavenly bodies, was one of the six Vedāṅgas, the auxiliary sciences that supported the Vedic rituals, and its early work was a calendar fixing the dates of sacrifices, with nothing written about the planets. Its oldest surviving text, the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa*, attributed to Lagadha, organises time in a five-year cycle of the Sun and Moon. Its date is disputed. Taking the solstice it describes at face value, T. K. S. Sastry and R. Kochhar place it between 1370 and 1150 BCE; Michael Witzel, pointing to its late language and its resemblance to Babylonian works, puts it in the last centuries BCE.

Horoscopy came from the Greek-speaking world. The *Yavanajātaka*, ‘the nativity according to the Greeks’, is among the earliest Sanskrit works on Greek horoscopy, and its vocabulary gives it away: the seventh place, the descendant, is *jāmitra*, after the Greek *diametros*, and the thirds of the signs are *drekāṇas*, after the Greek *dekanos* ([The Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)). In 1978 David Pingree dated it to a Greek prose text written in Alexandria in 149/150 CE and put into Sanskrit verse by Sphujidhvaja in 269/270. Later work by Bill Mak, using newly found manuscripts, found no evidence of a Greek original, and the date is still disputed, with proposals from after 22 CE to the early seventh century. Historians also disagree on whether Indian horoscopy was a Hellenistic import or an Indian growth that met Greek astrology on the way. What arrived with the transmission is clearer: the twelve signs counted from Aries, the twelve places counted from the ascendant, and the planets in the order of the seven-day week all appear in Indian texts after it.

The classical authorities followed. Varāhamihira, who worked at Ujjain in the sixth century, mastered all three branches of jyotiṣa, mathematical astronomy, natural sciences and horoscopy, and after his death his works superseded nearly all earlier Indian texts on the subject; his *Bṛhajjātaka* is his detailed work on nativities. The *Bṛhat Parāśara Horāśāstra* is ascribed to the sage Parāśara, but its origin is unknown and its versions differ: Pingree described a composite of 71 chapters written between the seventh and the late eighth centuries, while the version most read today, R. Santhanam's translation of 1984, runs to 97.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

A zodiac tied to the stars. Jyotish counts the signs from a point among the fixed stars and corrects for the slow drift of the equinox with an *ayanāṃśa*, so a planet often falls one sign earlier in a Vedic chart than in mine ([Sidereal zodiac](/astrology/sidereal-zodiac/)). I work in the [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/), counted from the March equinox; setting the two side by side is the subject of [Sidereal and Vedic charts](/astrology/sidereal-and-vedic-charts/).

A calendar of the Moon. The 27 *nakshatras*, lunar mansions of 13°20′ each, follow the Moon rather than the Sun, and the Moon's nakshatra at birth opens the *Viṁśottarī daśā*, the main system of planetary periods: the planet that rules that nakshatra governs the first period, and each period divides into smaller ones. The Moon crosses a nakshatra in about a day, so where it stood within one depends on the hour, and the error from an inexact birth time grows with every subdivision. Jyotish needs the time to build its chart, and so do I: no birth time, no reading ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Nine grahas, two of them points. Rahu and Ketu, the Moon's ascending and descending nodes, count among the nine bodies of Jyotish beside the Sun, the Moon and five planets; they move backwards and always stand opposite each other. In my charts they are the [North Node](/astrology/north-node/) and the [South Node](/astrology/south-node/), and I look at both the mean node and the true one.

Houses and aspects of its own. Each *bhāva*, or house, spans 30°, and aspects fall on whole houses, counted forward from the planet: Jupiter looks at the 5th, 7th and 9th houses from where it stands, Mars at the 4th, 7th and 8th. Where [cosmobiology](/astrology/cosmobiology/) keeps only the angles that divide the circle by eight, Jyotish gives each planet its own sightlines. I use Placidus, in which houses differ in size, and I measure aspects in degrees ([Whole Sign houses](/astrology/whole-sign-houses/)).

And knowledge that travelled on. In Baghdad, on the orders of the caliph al-Manṣūr, Brahmagupta's seventh-century *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* was translated into Arabic as the *Sindhind*, possibly the route by which Indian astronomers' mathematical methods and the Hindu numerals reached the Islamic world ([Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/)). Eastward, in 718, Aryabhata's table of sines went into Chinese, in the Tang *Treatise on Astrology of the Kaiyuan Era* ([Chinese astrology](/astrology/chinese-astrology/)).

I don't read sidereal signs, nakshatras or dashas, and I don't think Jyotish wrong for using them: it is a complete system. If you have a Vedic chart and a tropical one, read each by its own rules; a nakshatra from one and a sign from the other give an answer neither tradition would.
