Michel Gauquelin tested astrology with a psychologist's tools: thousands of births, counted against what chance would give. He threw out the zodiac signs and kept one narrow claim, which astrologers and sceptics then argued over for the rest of his life.

## Who {#who}

He was a French psychologist and statistician, sometimes called a neo-astrologer. His book *L'influence des astres: étude critique et expérimentale* (1955) made two claims at once. The signs and the aspects, the backbone of a horoscope, showed nothing in his figures. The daily position of some planets did: successful doctors, scientists, athletes, actors, writers and painters were born more often than expected with particular planets in particular parts of their daily round, and the planet in each case was the one astrology traditionally gives that profession.

More books followed, among them *The Scientific Basis for Astrology* (1969), in English, *C'est écrit dans les astres* and *Les Horloges cosmiques*. In 1979 he and Françoise Gauquelin answered a negative American test in the sceptics' own journal, under the title “Star U.S. Sportsmen Display the Mars Effect”. A French committee for the study of paranormal phenomena then tested the claim on 1,066 champions; its report, published as a book in 1996 with several of his letters to the committee, found no effect and put his results down to bias in the selection of data.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He made astrology a question of counting. Where a chart is read one life at a time, he set the planets of thousands of births against the frequencies chance would produce. Even the authors of the negative American test granted part of his method: in 1983 George Abell, Paul Kurtz and Marvin Zelen wrote that “Gauquelin adequately allowed for demographic and astronomical factors in predicting the expected distribution of Mars sectors for birth times in the general population”.

He pulled apart two things astrology reads together. The signs and aspects failed his tests; a planet's place in its daily round, measured in the twelve sectors he drew from its rising, did not ([Gauquelin sectors](/astrology/gauquelin-sectors/)). Since that round runs on the clock, every one of his findings depended on recorded birth times, and one of the later explanations turned on who had written those times down. He noticed himself that the effect faded in births after 1950, and put it down to the growing number of birth hours set by surgery rather than by nature.

His numbers became other people's work. The astrologer John Addey applied harmonic analysis to them, and a chapter by Kelly, Dean and Saklofske in a philosophy-of-science textbook set out the result ([Harmonic charts](/astrology/harmonic-charts/)). Suitbert Ertel argued that the effect showed up once athletes were graded by eminence. [Geoffrey Dean](/astrology/geoffrey-dean/) suggested that some parents had reported birth times to suit their beliefs. The independent tests themselves, and the quarrel inside the sceptics' committee, have a page of their own: [The Mars effect (Gauquelin)](/astrology/mars-effect-gauquelin/).

His question and my notebook's were different. He asked whether a planet marks out a profession across thousands of strangers; I asked whether each placement described the person it belonged to, a tick or a cross at a time. Mars was in my notebook like every placement and wasn't one of the three that held far more often, Saturn, Chiron and Lilith, at 75 to 90%; overall about a third held, 30 to 40%, the Sun sign among them ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). It's a notebook, not a study, and it doesn't weigh against research on his scale ([Does astrology work?](/astrology/does-astrology-work/)).

Where [Carl Jung](/astrology/carl-jung/) had looked for meaning in coincidence, Gauquelin looked for frequency. In 1968, a year before his *Scientific Basis for Astrology* came out in English, [Linda Goodman](/astrology/linda-goodman/) published *Sun Signs*, the first astrology book to reach *The New York Times* best-seller list.
