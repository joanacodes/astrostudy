Reinhold Ebertin took the arithmetic of one school of astrology, removed its most contested part, and gave what was left a name with a scientific ring: cosmobiology. His handbook of 1940 became that school's main reference.

## Who {#who}

He was a German astrologer and publisher, and astrology ran in the family. His mother, Elsbeth Ebertin, was a graphic artist, writer and astrologer who published astrological yearbooks under the title *Ein Blick in die Zukunft*, ‘a look into the future’. His own books came out from the Ebertin-Verlag of Aalen, the press that carried his name.

His method was built on another man's research. Alfred Witte's *Regelwerk für Planetenbilder*, the rulebook the Hamburg School published in 1928, gave him the midpoint and its combinations, and the school's 4th-harmonic dial of 90° gave him a way of seeing them at a glance ([Alfred Witte](/astrology/alfred-witte/)). Ebertin kept both and left out the eight hypothetical planets the school had placed beyond Neptune. He acknowledged the debt: his handbook records that he had used Witte's rulebook extensively in his research, and that some of Witte's ideas might yet prove useful in time, even where he had moved away from them.

The handbook appeared in German in 1940 as *Kombination der Gestirneinflüsse*. Its English translation, *The Combination of Stellar Influences*, was last revised in 1972, and its readers know it by its initials, the CSI or the COSI. It simplified Witte's [midpoint](/astrology/midpoints/) technique and added psychological meanings. Other books followed in English: *Applied Cosmobiology* (1972), *Transits: Forecasting using the 45 degree Graphic Ephemeris* (1973), *Directions: Co-Determinants of Fate* (1976), *Fixed Stars and Their Interpretation*, written with Georg Hoffmann (1971), and, late, *Astrological Healing* (1989). He wrote working tools too, such as *Rapid & Reliable Analysis* and *Auxiliary Tables for the Calculation of the Stellar Positions*.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He made Witte's arithmetic lighter to carry. Without the hypothetical planets, the method needed nothing an ordinary ephemeris doesn't give, and it was in that form, through the translations of his books, that the word cosmobiology became current in English ([cosmobiology](/astrology/cosmobiology/)).

The part of his work counted most original is his research in medical astrology. For it he used Fritz Brandau's *Organuhr der anatomischen Entsprechungen*, which matches the degrees of the zodiac to parts of the body. I don't follow him there. I never read a chart for health, and a degree mapped onto an organ is not something I would put in front of anyone worried about their body ([Can astrology predict health problems?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-health-problems/); [Medical astrology, in history](/astrology/medical-astrology-in-history/)).

His titles show how much of his work looked ahead. *Transits* came with a subtitle about forecasting, *Directions* with one about fate, and another book asked *Transits: What Days Favor You?* The ephemeris of *Transits* is a graph rather than a table: positions are counted round a scale of 45°, so a moving planet crosses the line of a natal point whenever it reaches the conjunction, [semi-square](/astrology/semi-square/), square, [sesquiquadrate](/astrology/sesquiquadrate/) or opposition to it. I read [transits](/astrology/transits/) as weather, not a forecast: they describe a season you move through, and no day in them is chosen for you. A title like *Directions: Co-Determinants of Fate* at least makes the sky one cause among several rather than the only one, which is closer to how I hold a chart ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

The fixed stars had a book of their own with Georg Hoffmann. I describe stars on this site without computing them in readings ([Fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)), and my notebook, which went through planets by sign and house, has nothing to say about them or about midpoints ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

He reached psychology by way of arithmetic. At the same time, in America, [Dane Rudhyar](/astrology/dane-rudhyar/) was arriving at a psychological astrology from the other end, by way of Jung, with a book first published in 1936.
