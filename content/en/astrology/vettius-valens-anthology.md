*Anthologia* is Greek for a gathering of blossoms, and [Vettius Valens](/astrology/vettius-valens/) chose an honest title. His *Anthology* is a second-century astrologer's collection: what he had learned, from whom, which tables he trusted and how he wanted it all kept. Where [Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/) argues, the *Anthology* shows its working.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A long treatise in Greek, of nine books or ten depending on who is counting. David Pingree's critical edition, published at Leipzig in 1986, settles on nine in its very title, *Anthologiarum libri IX*; Wilhelm Kroll had edited the Greek before him, at Berlin in 1908. Most modern readers meet it in English, through Robert Schmidt's translations of single books for Project Hindsight in the 1990s and the draft translation Mark Riley made available in 2010. In French, J.-F. Bara published the first book in 1989.

It had a life in Persia long before any of that. Middle Persian versions of Valens and of [Dorotheus’ Carmen Astrologicum](/astrology/dorotheus-carmen-astrologicum/) were among the Greek books that reached Sasanian Iran, and from there they fed the astrology later written in Arabic ([Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/)). The book was known there as the *Bizidaj*, and according to two Arabic historians, Ibn al-Qifti and Sa'id al-Andalusi, the Persian astrologer Zadanfarrukh Andarzgar particularly admired its ten books.

What makes it worth reading is its tone. The *Anthology* gives the methods astrologers of the Roman Empire used from day to day, with little of the physics Ptolemy built round his rules, and it lets you watch a practitioner choose between authorities rather than derive everything from first principles.

## What it says {#what-it-says}

Valens explains the week. The planets were thought to take the hours in turn, Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury, the Moon, and each day was named after the one ruling its first hour. Saturday is [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/)'s day because Saturn rules its first hour; the historian Dio Cassius gave the same account. It's the scheme of the [planetary hours](/astrology/planetary-hours/).

The zodiac's finer divisions are here too, among them the Egyptian terms, the five unequal stretches of every sign that belong to Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. They were Valens' standard, and the most widely accepted table of his day ([Terms (bounds)](/astrology/terms-bounds/)).

He names his sources. Valens quotes earlier astrologers, among them Critodemus, one of the earliest known authors on astrology, and he is just as open about the astronomy under his charts. In the ninth book, after comparing the lengths of the year used by the Chaldeans and by the Babylonians, he says whose tables he follows: “I thought it best to use Hipparchus for the sun, Sudines, Kidenas, and Apollonius for the moon, in addition to Apollinarius for both bodies.” The Babylonian astronomer Sudines was his authority for eclipses of the Moon.

Every chart rests on two things you can't see in the wheel: the tables behind the positions, and the time it was drawn for. Valens named his tables. I ask for the minute of birth, because the rising degree and the houses depend on it, and without it I don't read the chart at all ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Astrologers still choose between authorities, and I'd rather name my choices than hide them. Mine are [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/) for the houses; both rulers for Scorpio, Aquarius and Pisces, the traditional and the modern ([Traditional and modern rulers](/astrology/traditional-and-modern-rulers/)); and both lunar nodes, the mean and the true ([Mean node and true node](/astrology/mean-node-and-true-node/)).

Discretion mattered to him. Valens wrote that his teaching should not be handed to the ignorant or passed on through chance encounters, and he records that Critodemus had extracted “frightful oaths” from his students. Some astrologers of his time asked as much ([Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/)). Ptolemy, holding astrology to be a natural study, asked for no oath at all, and on this I'm with Ptolemy. There's nothing in a reading you couldn't learn yourself, with time, and the way I read is set out in full on [How I read](/how-i-read/).
