Psychological astrology asked what a chart says about the mind. Humanistic astrology asked something slightly different: what a person might become. Its author was a composer from Paris who learned astrology from mimeographed lessons in California.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

[Dane Rudhyar](/astrology/dane-rudhyar/) was born Daniel Chennevière in Paris in 1895 and arrived in New York in November 1916, brought there by his music; he became an American citizen in 1926. From his years in Paris he kept a conviction that all existence is cyclical in character. His association with Theosophy began in 1920, when he was asked to compose music for a production at the Society's headquarters in Los Angeles. The theosophist Will Levington Comfort introduced him to [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/), and Jones taught him astrology through mimeographed lessons.

He learned it while he was reading Carl Jung, and set out to bring the two together; he also cites Jan Smuts's *Holism and Evolution* as an influence. In his account, the planets did not cause what happens in a life: the chart was a picture, aligned with the person by synchronicity, of psychological forces that never overrode human freedom to respond to them. He first called this “harmonic astrology”, then “humanistic astrology”, and set it out in *[The Astrology of Personality](/astrology/dane-rudhyars-the-astrology-of-personality-1936/)* (1936), published by the theosophist Alice Bailey's press. The book also notes that the astrology of his day “originated almost entirely” in the work of [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/).

In 1969 Rudhyar founded the International Committee for Humanistic Astrology, a small professional society, and by the mid-1970s he had moved on to what he called transpersonal astrology. With his protégé Alexander Ruperti, he was one of the first astrologers to bring Jung's psychology into astrology. His first astrological writing appeared as regular articles in two popular magazines, Paul Clancy's *American Astrology* and Grant Lewi's *Horoscope*. He was not above a date, either: in *Astrological Timing* (1972) he placed the beginning of the Age of Aquarius in 2062, an age counted by the slow drift of the equinox ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)). He died in 1985.

His teacher left a mark of his own. Marc Edmund Jones, who lived from 1888 to 1980, created the Sabian symbols with the clairvoyant Elsie Wheeler in 1925, an image for each of the 360 degrees of the zodiac, and named seven shapes a chart can take before a single aspect is drawn: splay, splash, bundle, bowl, locomotive, bucket and seesaw.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

The chart as potential. For Rudhyar a birth chart was not a set of instructions from the sky but a picture of what a person could grow into, and the person stayed free to answer it. That is the sense in which I hold a reading too: a conversation with yourself, not an instruction ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

The whole before the parts. The holism he cites among his influences points that way, and Jones's shapes, such as the [bowl chart](/astrology/bowl-chart/), do the same thing graphically: they ask how the planets are spread round the wheel before any single placement is read. On this site each shape has its own page, with how often it turns up. Reading a chart whole is also why I need the birth time: without it the rising sign and the houses are missing, and the picture has holes in it ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

A bridge, finally. Rudhyar connected the theosophical astrology he met in California with Jung's psychology ([psychological astrology](/astrology/psychological-astrology/)), and his later “transpersonal” turn pointed beyond the personality altogether. The writers who went furthest in that direction, towards the soul and its past, called their approach [evolutionary astrology](/astrology/evolutionary-astrology/).
