"Does it work?" is really several questions: does a chart describe a person, does it predict what happens to them, does a reading help. The tests below asked the first two, and to those the answer is plain.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Not in the way the tests asked. When astrologers have had to match birth charts to people, or people have had to pick out their own reading from several, under conditions that hid the obvious clues, the results have been no better than chance, and controlled experiments have failed to show any effect. Whether a reading can still help someone think about their life is a different question from the one those tests asked. My answer to that is below, with what I found and what I don't claim.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

The best-known test is [Carlson's double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/), published in *Nature*. Astrologers nominated by the National Council for Geocosmic Research advised on the design, helped draw up the central claim of natal astrology to be tested, and agreed beforehand that it was a fair test. The personality profiles came from the California Psychological Inventory, a standard questionnaire that the astrologers themselves judged closest to what they believed a chart could tell. Then 28 astrologers tried to match more than a hundred birth charts to those profiles. They did no better than chance, and they were no more often right when they felt confident. Carlson concluded that the result "clearly refutes the astrological hypothesis". That the design had been agreed with astrologers matters: any test can be accused of being unfair to what it tests, and this one was agreed before it began.

Geoffrey Dean, a scientist and former astrologer, and the psychologist Ivan Kelly brought much of the later evidence together in 2003. A large test of theirs, covering more than a hundred cognitive, behavioural, physical and other variables, found no support for astrology. In another, 45 astrologers who rated themselves confident, with ten years' experience on average, used birth charts to judge 160 people who had scored at the extremes of the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire; they did much worse than simply going by people's ages, and much worse than 45 people who didn't use charts at all. Pooling 40 studies involving 700 astrologers and over 1,000 birth charts, Dean and Kelly found that in the ten tests where astrologers, 300 in all, had to pick the right chart interpretation from several, usually three to five others, no chart was preferred once the date and other obvious clues were removed. They also gathered 25 studies of how far astrologers agree about the same charts: the agreement was a low 0.1. A study of people born within five minutes of each other found no effect either: see [Time twins: Dean and Kelly](/astrology/time-twins-dean-and-kelly/).

People have been tested as well as astrologers. In ten studies where people picked the horoscope they felt described them, the right one was chosen no more often than chance would predict. And in 2024, Mohsen Joshanloo found zodiac signs no better than random numbers at predicting people's well-being and quality of life.

The one finding that looked like a hit came from Michel Gauquelin, an astrologer and psychologist. In 1955 he reported that, while signs and aspects showed nothing, the daily position of some planets lined up with success in the professions astrology links them with; the best known is [the Mars effect](/astrology/mars-effect-gauquelin/) in the charts of sports champions. A replication by a team of French scientists found no statistical evidence for it.

Whether astrology is a science is a separate question, with its own page, [Is astrology a science?](/astrology/is-astrology-a-science/); whether it's real in any other sense is on [Is astrology real?](/astrology/is-astrology-real/)

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My own test was the opposite of blind. I knew whose chart I was reading, and the people I tested knew it was theirs: my own chart first, then, with their consent, family, friends and strangers online, who marked each line themselves, a tick or a cross. Overall about a third held, 30 to 40%, which is close to what a flattering horoscope scores, so I hold those placements lightly; the Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury are among them. [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/), [Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/) and [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/) held 75 to 90% of the time. It's [a notebook, not a study](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), and it proves nothing about astrology in general.

So does it work, for me? It gives me a way of working I can stand behind. I spend the most time where [the three that held best](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/) sit in your chart, and when a reading rests on a placement that held less often, I tell you, so that you can weigh it. What it doesn't give me is anything to promise you. My readings are offered for reflection and entertainment, and no outcome is promised.
