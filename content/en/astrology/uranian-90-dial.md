The 90° dial is less a chart than an instrument: a disc that holds the whole zodiac in a quarter of a circle. It was made for one job, to bring the hard aspects and the midpoints of a chart into line, where the eye can catch them.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Cut the zodiac into four arcs of 90° and lay them on top of one another: that is the dial. Each of its degrees stands for four degrees of the zodiac, a square apart, so 10 on the dial is 10° Aries, 10° Cancer, 10° Libra and 10° Capricorn at once.

Cut that way, the signs sort themselves by modality ([The three modalities](/astrology/three-modalities/)). The first 30 degrees of the dial hold the cardinal signs, Aries, Cancer, Libra and Capricorn; the next 30 the fixed signs, Taurus, Leo, Scorpio and Aquarius; the last 30 the mutable signs, Gemini, Virgo, Sagittarius and Pisces. The dial's zero is the start of all four cardinal signs, which is why astrologers who use it treat them as one point ([Aries Point](/astrology/aries-point/)).

In arithmetic, the dial is the fourth harmonic: multiply any position on it by four and you have that point in the chart of the fourth harmonic ([Harmonic charts](/astrology/harmonic-charts/)). The disc is still drawn as a full circle, though, so every degree takes four times the room it has on a chart wheel, and small gaps are easier to see.

It came out of Hamburg. Alfred Witte did much of his work on midpoints with movable dials, and his school read its combinations on a dial of 90° ([The Hamburg School (Uranian astrology)](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/)). Reinhold Ebertin kept it when he dropped the school's hypothetical planets and made [cosmobiology](/astrology/cosmobiology/) of the rest.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

To put a chart on the dial by hand, keep a planet's degree as it is if it stands in a cardinal sign, add 30 if it is in a fixed sign, and 60 in a mutable one. A Sun at 10° Aries goes to 10. A Moon at 10° Capricorn goes to 10 as well: on the dial the two meet, because they are in [square](/astrology/square/). A Mars at 25° Taurus goes to 55, half the dial away from the Sun, because the two are 45° apart.

The dial itself is a disc numbered 0 to 90 round its edge, with a pointer pivoting at the centre. Wherever the pointer lies, its two ends mark spots half the dial apart, so one sweep of it finds:

- **On the same spot:** the conjunction, the square and the [opposition](/astrology/opposition/).
- **Directly across:** the [semi-square](/astrology/semi-square/) of 45° and the [sesquiquadrate](/astrology/sesquiquadrate/) of 135°.
- **Equal spacing:** two planets the same distance either side of the pointer, which puts their [midpoint](/astrology/midpoints/) under it.

The fast points, the Moon and the angles above all, need an exact birth time on the dial as on any chart ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

One family of aspects, all at once. The aspects that divide the circle by two, four and eight, which Witte and Ebertin found the most potent in a birth chart, collapse onto two positions, the same spot and the opposite side, and the midpoints between planets line up with them.

It shows nothing else as special. A semi-sextile, a sextile, a [trine](/astrology/trine/) and a quincunx all come out a third of the dial apart: 120° less a quarter turn is 30, and 150° less one is 60, which on a circle of 90 is 30 the other way. The dial can't tell those four apart, and isn't meant to.

I don't use the dial. My readings start from the planets by sign, house and aspect on the ordinary wheel, and the semi-square and the sesquiquadrate I read there, as minor aspects kept to a degree or two ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Its limits {#its-limits}

What isn't a multiple of 45° drops out of sight, as above, and so does part of the ordinary chart: a planet's place on the dial no longer says which of four signs it is in, only its modality.

Midpoints multiply fast. Ten bodies make 45 pairs, and each pair's midpoint marks two spots on the dial, its own and the one directly across: 90 spots on a dial of 90 degrees. In births from 1930 to 2009, by The Astro Study's count, each of the ten bodies stood within a degree of such a spot belonging to two others in about 8 births in 10, and in about 1 in 6 all ten did. On the dial, something nearly always lines up.

The Uranian form adds eight hypothetical points beyond Neptune, and more spots still, for points no telescope has found.

Other frames start from somewhere else: [declination charts](/astrology/declination-charts/) measure from the equator rather than along the zodiac, and [sidereal and Vedic charts](/astrology/sidereal-and-vedic-charts/) count the zodiac from the stars rather than from the equinox.
