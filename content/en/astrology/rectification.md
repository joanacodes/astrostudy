Most astrology runs forwards, from a birth to a life. Rectification runs backwards: it starts from a life and tries to recover the minute it began.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

When a birth time is missing, or known only roughly, a rectifier tries to recover it from the chart itself. The reasoning is that a chart's timing, its directions, progressions and transits, should line up with what has happened in a life. So the astrologer tries possible times until the timing fits the dated events best.

The problem is as old as the art. In the second century, Ptolemy discussed how hard it was to record the time of a birth precisely enough to fix the rising degree, and gave a method that works from the new or full moon before the birth (*Tetrabiblos*, book III, chapter 2; see [Prenatal lunation](/astrology/prenatal-lunation/)). Fifteen centuries later, [William Lilly’s Christian Astrology (1647)](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/) showed how the techniques he used to time a life, [primary directions](/astrology/primary-directions/) among them, could correct a nativity whose time wasn't exactly known.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

Modern rectification usually starts from a window of possible times, set by whatever was recorded or remembered, and a list of dated events: moves, marriages, the start or end of a job. For each candidate time, the astrologer works out when directions, progressions and transits would have reached the angles, and keeps the time whose dates fit the list best.

The angles carry the method, because they're the part of a chart that moves fast enough to tell one minute from the next. A change of four minutes in the birth time turns the Midheaven by roughly a degree, and in [solar arc directions](/astrology/solar-arc-directions/) each degree stands for about a year: each four minutes tried shifts a direction to the Midheaven by a year. That sensitivity is what makes rectification possible, and what makes a wrong rectified time costly, since every house and angle in the chart inherits it.

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

A rectified time is a hypothesis: the minute that, by one astrologer's methods, best fits the events they were given. It can be careful, honest work. It isn't a record. Nothing outside the method can check it, and two rectifiers working from the same life can arrive at two different minutes, each fitting its own reading of the events.

That's why I don't rectify charts, or read from a rectified time. The rising sign, the houses and the angles would all rest on the estimate, and a reading built on it would present a guess as a fact. The order form says it plainly: *We’re working on a project to find people’s birth data accurately. Unfortunately, for now I can only do a reading with the time and place of birth.* If you're still looking, your time may be written down somewhere ([How to find your birth time](/astrology/how-to-find-your-birth-time/)), and [what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) explains what hangs on it.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

The method checks itself. The events are chosen after the fact, the techniques that find the time are the ones then used to read it, and a fit is easy to find when you're looking for one ([Confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/)).

The events that would test a time most sharply are often the hardest moments of a life: losses, illnesses, accidents. I don't read charts for those, forwards or backwards.

A recorded time has its own weaknesses: rounded to the hour or the quarter hour, or written in a time zone or a summer time that has since been forgotten ([Time zones and daylight saving in charts](/astrology/time-zones-and-daylight-saving-in-charts/)). But it is evidence of the birth, not a fit to the life. Other ways of filling the gap, a chart for noon or for sunrise, describe a time of day rather than a birth ([Noon and sunrise charts](/astrology/noon-and-sunrise-charts/)).
