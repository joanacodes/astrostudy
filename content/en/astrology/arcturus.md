Arcturus is the orange-red star you reach by following the curve of the Plough's handle, and the brightest star north of the celestial equator. Its Greek name means guardian of the bear, and in the zodiac it stands at 24°36′ Libra in 2026, less than half a degree along from Spica.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Only three stars of the night sky outshine α Boötis, at magnitude −0.04. It is a red giant about 37 light-years away, roughly the Sun's mass but swollen to some twenty-five times its size, and Ptolemy already described it as slightly red. In 1635 the French mathematician Jean-Baptiste Morin saw it through a telescope in full daylight, the first recorded daylight sighting of a star other than the Sun, supernovae aside.

It is also visibly on the move. Arcturus crosses about 228 seconds of arc a century against the background stars, faster than any first-magnitude star except α Centauri, enough to carry it some six degrees in 10,000 years.

In 2000 it stood at 24°13′ [Libra](/astrology/libra/), in 2026 at 24°36′, never more than half a degree from [Spica](/astrology/spica/), at 24°12′. The two could hardly be further apart in the sky: Spica lies near the Sun's path, while Arcturus stands 30.7° north of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), high in the evening sky of late spring.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Arktouros joins the Greek *arktos*, bear, to *ouros*, guardian. In the myth told by Hyginus, Zeus set the boy Arcas in the sky as the Herdsman, Arctophylax, the bear-keeper, and his mother Callisto as the Great Bear beside him. Hesiod, around 700 BC, used its dawn rising as the signal for the grape harvest, and in Sophocles a shepherd grazes his flock "from spring until Arcturus".

In Arabic, Arcturus is one of the two simāk, the uplifted ones; Spica is the other. Arcturus is as-simāk ar-rāmiḥ, the uplifted one of the lancer, also called the keeper of heaven. Polynesian navigators knew it as Hōkūleʻa, the star of joy: it passes straight overhead in Hawaii, so they sailed north until it stood at the zenith, then west to landfall.

Cornelius Agrippa drew the sign of Alchameth, as he called it, among the fifteen Behenian stars of his magic, with [Mars](/astrology/mars/) and [Jupiter](/astrology/jupiter/) for its planets, jasper for its stone and plantain for its plant. The next Behenian star along the zodiac is [Alphecca](/astrology/alphecca/), the bright star of the Northern Crown, at 12° Scorpio.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

A day or so after it passes Spica, on about 17–18 October, the Sun reaches Arcturus's degree, a [Sun in Libra](/astrology/sun-in-libra/) near the end of the sign. By the tradition's rule, a planet within about a degree of that longitude conjoins the star, though here it passes thirty degrees south of it ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)).

Arcturus is a star I'd gladly point out to anyone on a late-spring evening, following the Plough's handle round; it has no part in my readings, which leave the fixed stars to others ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). What I keep from it is the calendar: a star that told farmers when to cut the grapes is telling you about the year, not about you.
