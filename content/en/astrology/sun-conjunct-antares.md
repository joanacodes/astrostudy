Sun conjunct Antares belongs to the first two days of December: a birth then puts the Sun within a degree of the red heart of the Scorpion, in the sign of Sagittarius. About 1 person in 185 born between 1930 and 2009 has it.

## What it means {#what-it-means}

I read the [Sun](/astrology/sun/) as what organises you: your purpose, your vitality, the direction you keep choosing. [Antares](/astrology/antares/) is known first by its name. In Greek it is the rival of Ares, the star so red it could pass for Mars, and laid over the Sun, that colour becomes, in the tradition's terms, heat and drive at the centre of who you are becoming. The medieval list of Behenian stars gives Antares Jupiter as well as Mars, and [Jupiter](/astrology/jupiter/) rules Sagittarius, the sign this Sun stands in: in that old scheme the star and the sign share a planet.

This is a [Sun in Sagittarius](/astrology/sun-in-sagittarius/), at around 9° or 10° of the sign, on a star of the constellation Scorpius. Antares stood at 8°46′ Sagittarius in 1930 and 9°45′ in 2000, so the birthday has stayed put across those decades. The sign is what describes you, its manner, its house, its aspects; the constellation is only the star's address on the sky's old maps ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).

On those birthdays the star itself is out of sight. From mid-northern latitudes Antares vanishes into the Sun's glare for two or three weeks either side of 30 November, and around 2 December the Sun passes about five degrees north of it, close in the zodiac and apart in the sky. Six months later the places swap: at the end of May the Sun reaches [Aldebaran](/astrology/aldebaran/), the opposite royal star, and Antares is up all night.

The meeting also lines up two very different stars. Antares is a red supergiant: set where the Sun is, it would reach out to somewhere in the asteroid belt, beyond the orbit of Mars. Astrology treats our Sun as one of the lights of a chart, like the Moon and the planets; on these birthdays it shares its degree with a star hundreds of times its size.

Royalty is the other half of the star's reputation: astrology books count Antares among the four royal stars of Persia, with Aldebaran, Regulus and Fomalhaut. I keep both, the rivalry with Mars and the crown, as the tradition's own story. A Sun on this star is still a Sun: it describes what you are trying to become, and nothing about Antares decides how that goes.

None of this enters my readings, because the charts I work from are drawn without stars; what I'd read in a chart with the Sun here is its sign, house and aspects ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). The star's other contacts follow other calendars: the [Moon conjunct Antares](/astrology/moon-conjunct-antares/), the most frequent of the five at about 1 in 171; [Venus conjunct Antares](/astrology/venus-conjunct-antares/), the rarest, about 1 in 212, for births between mid-October and mid-January; [Mars conjunct Antares](/astrology/mars-conjunct-antares/), about 1 in 210; and [Saturn conjunct Antares](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-antares/), limited from 1930 to 2009 to December 1956 to October 1957 and February to November 1986. [Conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/) explains the one-degree rule, and [fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/) the wider tradition.
