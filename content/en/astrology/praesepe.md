Praesepe, Latin for manger, is a cluster of about a thousand stars in Cancer that looks, to the naked eye, like a small patch of mist. Astrologers have long counted it among the fixed stars, although it isn't one star, and unlike a star it has no catalogue position of its own.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Its modern names are the Beehive Cluster and Messier 44. It shines at a combined magnitude of 3.7 and spreads about 1½° across the sky, three times the width of the full Moon, which fits it easily in a pair of binoculars. It lies some 600 light-years away, and its stars are about 600 million years old.

The degree given here is worked out from the centre astronomers give for the cluster: about 7°38′ [Leo](/astrology/leo/) in 2026, 7°16′ in 2000 and 6°17′ in 1930, with its stars spread about three-quarters of a degree either side. The cluster belongs to the constellation Cancer and sits in the sign of Leo ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).

What makes it unusual among the stars astrologers use is its latitude, only about 1.6° north of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/). The Moon and the planets that reach its degree pass close by in the sky, sometimes straight through it ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Hipparchus called it the Little Cloud and Aratus the Little Mist. [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/)'s star catalogue, the Almagest, lists it among seven "nebulous" objects as the nebulous mass in the breast of Cancer. Galileo, in 1609, was the first to see it as stars: forty of them in his telescope. Johann Bayer drew it as a hazy star on his atlas of 1603 and gave it the letter epsilon, which now belongs to its brightest star alone.

The manger comes from a farm scene. The Greeks and Romans saw two donkeys eating from it, the stars Asellus Borealis and Asellus Australis, the mounts that Dionysus and Silenus rode against the Titans; Eratosthenes named this part of the sky the Crab, the Asses and the Crib. Arabic had al-ma'laf, the stall.

Chinese astronomy placed it in the Ghost, the 23rd lunar mansion, and likened it to a cloud of pollen blown from willow catkins ([the 28 lunar mansions](/astrology/28-lunar-mansions/)).

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

With no single star to measure from, the one-degree rule of the tradition is applied here to the centre: roughly 6°38′ to 8°38′ Leo for a birth in 2026, and 5°17′ to 7°17′ for one in 1930 ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). On about 29 and 30 July the Sun stands on it, a [Sun in Leo](/astrology/sun-in-leo/) a week into the sign.

Here a conjunction can be a real meeting. When Mars reached Praesepe's degree in the spring of 2025, it passed through the cluster itself. The Moon, whose path tilts about 5° to the Sun's, comes this way every month, sometimes beside the cluster and sometimes across it.

[Castor](/astrology/castor/) lies 17° before it in the zodiac and [Acubens](/astrology/acubens/), the Crab's claw, 6° after. I describe Praesepe here; the system I use for readings doesn't place it, or any other star ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).
