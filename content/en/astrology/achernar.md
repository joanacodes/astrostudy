Achernar is the end of the river: the bright blue-white star at the southern tip of Eridanus. Ptolemy never saw it. From Alexandria it stayed below the horizon, and it is the only star of the first magnitude missing from his catalogue.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Achernar is α Eridani, about 139 light-years away and of magnitude 0.46 in the Yale catalogue, the ninth-brightest star of the night and the hottest and bluest of the ten brightest. It spins so fast that it has flattened: in 2015 it was the least round star known in the Milky Way, more than a third wider at the equator than from pole to pole.

It never rises north of about 33°N, so from Europe it is out of sight, and south of 33°S it never sets. Precession is slowly bringing it north: between the eighth and eleventh millennia it will be visible as far north as southern England.

On the zodiac it projects to 15°40′ [Pisces](/astrology/pisces/) for 2026 (15°18′ for 2000, 14°18′ for 1930), from 59.4° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/). However close a planet comes to its degree, the star stays some sixty degrees away in the sky ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

The Arabic ākhir an-nahr means the end of the river. The name seems first to have belonged to another star of the River, θ Eridani, now called Acamar, which has the same meaning.

Europe came to Achernar late. In 1592 the Englishman Robert Hues listed three bright stars "never seene here in England": one of them was "in the end of Eridanus". European knowledge of it is also credited to Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser, on the first Dutch voyage to the East Indies, and Johann Bayer's atlas of 1603 was the first to draw it in Eridanus.

So the old astrology that starts with [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/) had no Achernar to describe. If a book gives this star a nature "from Ptolemy", it is worth asking which star of the River he meant. In China, where the far southern stars were added from European charts, it is the first star of the Crooked Running Water.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The tradition's one-degree window runs from 14°40′ to 16°40′ Pisces for a 2026 birth and from 13°18′ to 15°18′ for a 1930 one ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)); those born around 5 and 6 March have the Sun there, a [Sun in Pisces](/astrology/sun-in-pisces/) at mid-sign.

With [Canopus](/astrology/canopus/) and the south pole of the sky, Achernar makes a rough equilateral triangle, one way of finding the pole from the southern hemisphere; both stars are far from the Sun's path, and both reach a chart only by projection. [Altair](/astrology/altair/), next on this site, is the reverse: a northern star, 29° above the ecliptic, that almost the whole world can see.

My readings leave Achernar out, as they leave out every fixed star ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). On a degree of Pisces it remains a fact about the sky, not a meaning for a chart.
