On a clear night the stars look like points of light on the inside of a dome, every one of them the same distance away. Astronomers know they aren't, and keep the picture anyway, because it works. They call it the celestial sphere, and every birth chart is drawn on it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The celestial sphere is an imaginary globe of unlimited size with the Earth at its centre. Everything in the sky is projected onto its inner surface: the Sun, the Moon, the planets and the stars. Because the sphere can be as large as you like, it keeps one thing about each body, the direction in which you'd look to see it, and drops how far away it is.

The Greeks took it literally. Eudoxus of Cnidus built his model of the heavens from 27 nested spheres, Aristotle his from 55, with the outermost carrying the fixed stars. Astronomy has long since let the shells go and kept the geometry: lines and planes drawn from the Earth, extended outwards, become circles on the sphere.

Four of those circles make a chart. The Earth's equator, extended, becomes the [celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/). The plane of the Earth's orbit becomes the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), the Sun's yearly path, along which the zodiac is laid. Your horizon at the moment of birth becomes the [horizon axis](/astrology/horizon-axis/) of your chart, and the circle through the celestial poles and the point overhead becomes its meridian ([meridian axis](/astrology/meridian-axis/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Because the Earth spins eastward, the sky appears to turn the other way: each star crosses from east to west and comes back to the same place 23 hours 56 minutes later, a little less than a day by the clock ([sidereal time](/astrology/sidereal-time/)). Against that daily turning, the Sun creeps east along the ecliptic by about a degree a day, and the Moon and the planets travel close to the same line.

Each of the sphere's circles carries a pair of coordinates, as latitude and longitude do on a map:

- **The horizon:** a body's altitude above it and its bearing round it. Both belong to one place and one moment, which is why the angles of a chart need the time and the town of birth.
- **The celestial equator:** [declination](/astrology/declination/), north or south of it, and [right ascension](/astrology/right-ascension/), along it.
- **The ecliptic:** [zodiacal longitude](/astrology/zodiacal-longitude/), along it, which gives a planet its sign and degree, and [celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/), north or south of it.

Leaving out distance costs almost nothing, except with the Moon. It is close enough that two observers far apart on the Earth see it against slightly different stars, up to about a degree apart, an effect called parallax. Almanacs therefore list positions as seen from the Earth's centre, and an observer who needs the view from one spot corrects for it.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

A chart wheel is this sphere flattened, at your minute and from your place ([Chart wheel, and how to read it](/astrology/chart-wheel-and-how-to-read-it/)). The horizontal line is your horizon. Everything drawn above it, in houses 7 to 12, was in the sky over you when you were born; everything below it, in houses 1 to 6, was hidden under your feet by the Earth itself. The zodiac runs round the rim because the ecliptic runs right round the sphere.

Because the sphere has no depth, a chart has none either. When two planets share a degree, I'm reading two bodies in the same direction, and one may be thousands of times further away than the other. What I read is the angle between their directions, which is all an aspect ever was.

The positions I work from are geocentric, as an ephemeris gives them. The horizon and the meridian, on the other hand, are yours alone: they come from the minute and the place of birth, and that is why the houses can't be drawn without the time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The celestial sphere is a real shell.”** It's a drawing tool. Ancient astronomers believed in solid spheres; today only their geometry survives.
- **“Planets in conjunction are close together in space.”** They lie in the same direction as seen from the Earth. Saturn is always thousands of times further away than the Moon, whatever degree they share.
- **“A geocentric chart means astrology puts the Earth at the centre of everything.”** It describes the sky from where you stood. The astronomers' almanacs list geocentric positions too.
- **“A chart shows only the sky you could see.”** Half of it, houses 1 to 6, was below the horizon when you were born.
