Algol is the star that winks. Every two days and almost twenty-one hours it fades from magnitude 2.1 to 3.4 and back, in an eclipse that lasts about ten hours, and the old sky-watchers gave it one of the darkest names in their catalogues: the Demon Star.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Algol is β Persei, the star that marks the head of Medusa, which the hero Perseus carries in the old star maps. It is really three stars, and two of them pass in front of each other as they orbit: that is the dimming. Geminiano Montanari noticed it in 1667, and John Goodricke worked out its regular rhythm in 1783 and suggested the cause. An Egyptian calendar of lucky and unlucky days, written some 3,200 years ago, has been read as the oldest record of Algol's blinking, though that reading is disputed.

Its zodiac degree, 26°31′ [Taurus](/astrology/taurus/) in 2026 (26°09′ in 2000, 25°11′ in 1930), sits four degrees short of the Taurus–Gemini border, where the Pleiades lie ([Alcyone and the Pleiades](/astrology/alcyone-and-the-pleiades/)). Algol itself lies 22.4° north of the ecliptic, too far from the Sun's path for the Moon or any planet ever to pass in front of it.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

The name is Arabic, raʾs al-ghūl, the head of the ghoul, and "Demon Star" comes from the same words. Hebrew folklore called it Satan's Head; a sixteenth-century Latin name was Caput Larvae, the spectre's head; and as the first star of Medusa's head it is also Gorgonea Prima. Ptolemy calls it the Gorgon of Perseus and, in book IV of the *Tetrabiblos*, ties it to death by beheading: the myth of Perseus and Medusa read back into the sky.

The astrology of fixed stars has long counted Algol among the unluckiest stars, in the tradition's own word, and [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/) in 1647 treated any planet within five degrees of it as afflicted, five times the orb these pages use. Medieval magic, all the same, set the ghoul's head among the fifteen Behenian stars, with the diamond and black hellebore.

A myth about a severed head belongs to Medusa. It carries no information about how anyone will live or die, and no chart holds that information about anyone ([Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)).

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Mid-May, on about the 16th or 17th, is when the Sun crosses Algol's degree, a late-Taurus Sun: the [Sun conjunct Algol](/astrology/sun-conjunct-algol/), about 1 person in 176 across the births of 1930 to 2009. The rarer contacts are [Venus conjunct Algol](/astrology/venus-conjunct-algol/), about 1 in 212, and [Mars conjunct Algol](/astrology/mars-conjunct-algol/), 1 in 207, with the [Moon conjunct Algol](/astrology/moon-conjunct-algol/) at 1 in 186. [Saturn conjunct Algol](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-algol/), 1 in 191, came in three spells: June 1941 to April 1942, May 1971, and June 2000 to March 2001.

If you found Algol on one of your planets and came here worried, start with what the contact actually is: a shared zodiac degree with a star twenty-two degrees off the Sun's path, inside a one-degree window, carrying a name from a myth. I don't place Algol in my readings, because no star appears in the charts I draw, and I would never read harm from the Demon Star. The planet underneath is what describes you, through its sign, its aspects and its house, and the house is one more thing that only an accurate birth time can give ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).
