*Carmen Astrologicum* is Latin for “the astrological poem”, and the title is now the last trace of its form. [Dorotheus of Sidon](/astrology/dorotheus-of-sidon/) wrote it in Greek verse in the first century, to teach horoscopic astrology in five books; what we read today is a translation of an Arabic translation, made around 800 and probably from a Persian one.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

In Greek it was the *Pentateuch*, the five books. The verse survives only in fragments, published with a German translation by Viktor Stegemann in 1939 and 1943, and in the use later writers made of it. Around 415, Hephaestion of Thebes wrote a treatise that set out to reconcile Dorotheus with [Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/), the practitioner's poem with the philosopher's system. The astrology I practise is still that kind of reconciliation: modern tropical astrology, with traditional techniques.

The whole text is the Arabic of ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān al-Ṭabarī. Before that, like [Vettius Valens’ Anthology](/astrology/vettius-valens-anthology/), it had been read in Middle Persian, and the Arabic has surprises of its own: it refers to two Hermeses, and cites a book of Hermes on the positions of the planets. In Arabic it stayed in use: around 950 the only known book of Ibn Hibinta, a Christian astrologer in Iraq, drew on it alongside Ptolemy, al-Khwarizmi and the Indian astrologer Kanaka. I rely on David Pingree's English, published with his edition of the Arabic in 1976, and on Benjamin Dykes's translation of 2017.

Its road also ran far to the east. The historian Jeffrey Kotyk argues that a significant part of Chinese horoscopy derives from material traced back to Dorotheus, carried by Indian and Iranian intermediaries, and one of the earliest Chinese texts to teach the birth chart, the *Duli Yusi Jing*, may come from the same tradition.

In the West, the poem was still cited by name more than two centuries after it was written: [Firmicus Maternus’ Mathesis](/astrology/firmicus-maternus-mathesis/), the great Latin handbook of the fourth century, names Dorotheus and the city he came from.

## What it says {#what-it-says}

It starts with practice. The classicist Mark Riley points out that Dorotheus begins “not with a proof of the validity of astrology or a description of the universe, but with instructions for calculating births”, then moves straight on to the native's status, the parents' and the brothers'. Ptolemy, a century later, spent the opening of his first book defending astrology before teaching any of it. I like Dorotheus's order. I don't claim to know how astrology works, and a reading that began with a proof would begin with something I can't give ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

It gives the elements their rulers. Each triplicity, the three signs of one element, has a planet for charts cast by day, another for charts cast by night, and a third that shares in both, a scheme that still carries Dorotheus's name ([Triplicity rulers](/astrology/triplicity-rulers/)).

It counts points as well as planets. The lots, measured from the Ascendant by the distance between two other parts of the chart, were already standard equipment for Dorotheus ([Doctrine of the lots](/astrology/doctrine-of-the-lots/)).

It ends with beginnings. The fifth book teaches how to choose the moment to start something, and it is the oldest surviving treatise on what is now called [electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/). The Greek word for such a beginning, *katarchē*, became the name of the whole branch, which also judged the chart of a venture already begun, to foretell how it would go. I practise neither. I don't choose dates, and whether a business, a lawsuit or an investment will succeed is a question I won't read from any chart ([Can astrology predict a court case?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-a-court-case/), [Can astrology pick investments?](/astrology/can-astrology-pick-investments/)).
