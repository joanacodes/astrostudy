Take a bowl chart and lift one planet out of it, over to the empty side: the bowl gets a handle. [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) called the result a bucket, and it's one of the commonest shapes a chart can have.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

Nine planets fit within half the circle; the tenth stands alone in the other half. The nine are the bucket, a [bowl](/astrology/bowl-chart/) with one planet missing, and the lone planet is the handle. In the descriptions that follow Jones, the handle is a singleton, one planet by itself in the hemisphere the others leave empty ([singletons](/astrology/singletons/)). The count behind this site keeps to that plain version: nine within 180°, the tenth outside.

Where the handle points changes the picture. A handle across from the middle of the group splits the chart into a crowd and a single counterweight. One close to the rim of the group almost rejoins it, and the chart begins to look like a long arc of ten planets with a gap in it. In both cases the handle is the planet that stands apart, and that's what draws the eye.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

About 1 person in 3 born from 1930 to 2009 has a bucket in the count behind this site: nine of the ten planets within 180°, the tenth outside them. Only the locomotive is more common among the shapes it checks; the [bundle](/astrology/bundle-chart/), with all ten inside a third of the circle, is the rarest. The rule is simple and catches a lot, and its edges are sharp: move the line a few degrees and charts change shape. In the count's terms, a chart can even be a bucket and a locomotive at once, when the handle sits near enough to the rim that all ten still fit within 240°. The Ascendant, the Midheaven, Chiron, Lilith and the nodes weren't counted, and any of them can sit on the handle's side.

Astrologers who use the shapes give the handle planet extra weight: the point the whole chart leans on, the function that pulls the group's energy towards one purpose. I give it a closer look, not the last word. The system I built for my readings holds that no single factor determines a chart's dominant theme, so I read the handle through its function, its sign and its house, and then see whether the rest of the chart repeats what it says. When Saturn is the handle, I take longer over it: Saturn was one of the three placements that held 75 to 90% of the time in [my notebook](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), for me and for the people I tested.

The handle's house needs the time of birth, and so does the question of which half the bucket fills. The Moon complicates things in its own way. It can cross from the group to the empty side within a day or two, so on some birthdays the hour decides whether the Moon is the handle or part of the bucket. That's one reason I [read nothing without the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/).

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Find the handle.** It's the planet everyone else in the chart seems to face. Name its function before anything else.
- **Ask what the handle carries.** Its sign and house often show where the energy of the nine is put to use, or where it keeps escaping to.
- **Don't let one planet eclipse nine.** The group still holds most of the chart. The handle is a focus, not the whole story.
- **Read the handle's aspects.** How it connects to the group, by opposition, trine or square, tells you how easily the two sides work together.

A bucket is easier to carry by its handle, and that's the use of the shape: one planet to pick the chart up by, and nine more to read once you have.
