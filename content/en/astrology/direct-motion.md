Most of the time, every planet in your chart is doing the ordinary thing: moving forward through the signs, Aries to Taurus to Gemini, in the zodiac’s own order. That movement is called direct motion. It only needs a name because, now and then, the planets seem to do the opposite.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Direct motion is a planet’s forward movement along the zodiac, seen from Earth. Seen from the Sun, every planet goes forward round its orbit all the time. It’s from here, on a planet that is moving too, that each of them appears now and then to slow down, stop and back up for a while. That backward stretch is retrograde motion, and [Retrograde, explained](/astrology/retrograde-explained/) shows why it happens. The rest of the time, a planet is direct.

So direct motion isn’t a special condition: it’s what a planet does when nothing special is happening. In the dates I work from, which run from 2000 to 2040, Mercury is direct about four days in five, Mars about ten days in eleven and Venus about thirteen days in fourteen. The slow planets turn back for months every year, so their share is smaller: Jupiter is direct about seven days in ten, Saturn nearly two in three, Pluto only a little more than half the time.

Three bodies never leave it, and one pair of points never enters it. The [Sun](/astrology/sun/) and the [Moon](/astrology/moon/) always move forward through the signs, and so does mean Lilith, the [Black Moon Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/) most astrologers read. The mean lunar nodes do the opposite: they always move backwards, round the zodiac in about 18.6 years. The true node, which I look at too, wobbles around them.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A direct planet doesn’t keep a steady pace. It is quickest when it’s on the far side of the Sun from us, roughly halfway between two retrogrades, and it slows as the next one approaches, until it seems to stop. That standstill has its own page, [Stations](/astrology/stations/). After the backward stretch comes a second standstill, the station direct, and the planet starts forward again, slowly at first. In an ephemeris, the table of daily positions astrologers work from, that day is marked with a D, as the day a planet turns back is marked with ℞.

The first days of direct motion aren’t quite like the others. The planet has to cover again the degrees it has just crossed backwards, and until it gets back to the point where it first turned, it is moving over ground it has already been over twice. Many astrologers call that stretch the post-shadow, and [the shadow period](/astrology/shadow-period/) explains how they count it. On the sky’s side, nothing marks its end: the planet is simply direct, picking up speed.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In a birth chart, I don’t read anything into direct motion as such. A direct Mars is simply Mars, and what it means comes from its sign, its house and its aspects, as for every planet. Most planets in most charts are direct; turning the ordinary state into a message would only add noise.

What I do notice is pace. A planet born in the days around its station direct had only just stopped moving backwards, and the system I built for my readings marks it as stationary (the stations page says where I draw that line). I read it as a planet close to its own moment of change, with the emphasis that brings. A planet born retrograde has a different rhythm again, inward first, the one I wrote about in [Natal retrograde planets](/blog/retrograde-planets/).

In transits, the day a planet turns direct is a change of pace in the sky. If Mercury, Venus or Mars has spent its retrograde backing over a point in your chart, that day begins its last pass forward over the point. I read it as a stretch of review coming to its close: weather, not a forecast, and never a date on which things are bound to go right.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Direct is good, retrograde is bad.”** Neither is a verdict. Retrograde changes the rhythm of a planet’s function; direct is its usual rhythm. A planet that asks for patience in your chart doesn’t ask for less of it because it was moving forward.
- **“When Mercury goes direct, everything is sorted on the day.”** On the day it turns, Mercury is at its slowest. It spends the following days and weeks going back over the degrees it has just retraced, so whatever those weeks were about tends to settle gradually, if it settles at all.
- **“The Sun and Moon can go retrograde too.”** Seen from Earth they always move forward. Only the planets and Chiron turn back, and the mean nodes always move backwards.
- **“A planet in direct motion moves at the same speed all the time.”** It speeds up and slows down along the way: fastest on the far side of the Sun, slowest near its stations.
