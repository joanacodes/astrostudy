When it was found in November 2003, Sedna was the most distant body anyone had seen in the Solar System, and its name came from far outside Greek myth: the Inuit goddess of the sea. Some astrologers have taken it up. I haven't, and this page explains what it is and why.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Sedna is a dwarf planet about 1,000 km across, one of the reddest bodies known, nearly as red as Mars. Its orbit is among the longest and most stretched ever measured: about 11,400 years, from 76 times the Earth's distance from the Sun at its closest to some 937 times at its farthest. It is near the close end now, 83 times the Earth's distance in 2026, and will be nearest in 2076. Even so, it sits two and a half times as far out as [Neptune](/astrology/neptune/), and at the far end of its orbit it goes about nineteen times farther than [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/) ever does.

Michael Brown, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz found it on 14 November 2003 at Palomar Observatory in California. Brown nicknamed it the Flying Dutchman, after the ghost ship, because its slow drift had kept it hidden. That slowness had a sequel: it made the team rerun their older images with a lower speed limit, and the rerun turned up [Eris](/astrology/eris/). [Makemake](/astrology/makemake/) followed from the same team in 2005.

Sedna's closest approach is too far out for Neptune to have thrown it there, so its discoverers described it as the first known body of the inner Oort cloud, the reservoir of icy bodies thought to surround the Solar System. Something else bent its orbit: a passing star, a young star in the cluster where the Sun was born, or a planet nobody has seen ([Planet X and Planet Nine](/astrology/planet-x-and-planet-nine/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The goddess has many names among Inuit communities, many of them ways of avoiding a name that commands fear and respect: Sanna, "the great one down below", on Baffin Island; Nuliajuk, "the great wife", among the Netsilik. Sedna is an older spelling, made familiar by the anthropologist Franz Boas. In the heart of her story, a young woman is thrown overboard and clings to the side of the boat, and someone in the boat cuts off her fingers so that she lets go. She sinks to the sea floor and becomes the mistress of the sea; the pieces of her fingers become the sea mammals, seals in every version, walruses and whales in some. When people break the taboos, she holds the animals back from the hunters, and a shaman travels down to her house to appease her, sometimes by combing and braiding her hair, which she can't do without fingers.

Brown chose the name because her home at the bottom of the Arctic Ocean suited a body so far from the Sun, and asked that future finds in that region take names from Arctic mythologies. The symbol astrologers use is a monogram of Sanna in Inuktitut syllabics.

Seen from Earth, Sedna barely moves. Near its closest point, where it moves fastest, it still takes decades to cross a sign; at the far end of its orbit, a single sign would hold it for thousands of years. People born decades apart share its sign, so the sign marks an era rather than a person, and only its house or a tight contact could make it personal.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read Sedna, and with Sedna I have one reason more than with the other distant bodies. Its story belongs to Inuit religion, told in many versions under many names, and it came into astrology second-hand, through an astronomer's choice of name. I'm wary of turning it into a line in someone's horoscope about the people who let them down.

The other reasons are the ones I give for every body I leave out. The system behind my readings doesn't compute Sedna, and my notebook never checked it. That notebook went through the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith [like an invoice](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), and what stood out were [the three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/) 75 to 90% of the time. I'd rather give a reading's time to those than to a body whose sign people born decades apart share ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Sedna is the farthest object in the Solar System.”** It was the farthest seen when it was found. Today, near its closest point, it is nearer the Sun than Eris, and some long-period comets travel farther out.
- **“Sedna is a goddess of Greek myth, like the planets' namesakes.”** She belongs to Inuit religion, and Sedna is only one of her names.
- **“Sedna proves there's a hidden ninth planet.”** Its orbit needs an explanation, and an unseen planet is one candidate among several, beside a passing star and the Sun's birth cluster.
