Spica is the ear of wheat in the hand of Virgo, the Maiden, and the star thought to have shown Hipparchus that the equinoxes move. Only two degrees south of the Sun's path, it stands at 24°12′ Libra in 2026.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Most of the light of Spica, α Virginis, magnitude 0.98 and among the twenty brightest stars of the night, comes from a pair of hot blue stars so close together that they are egg-shaped rather than round, about 250 light-years away.

Virgo's brightest star belongs, in the tropical zodiac, to [Libra](/astrology/libra/): 22°51′ in 1930, 23°50′ in 2000, 24°12′ in 2026 ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)). At 2.1° south of the ecliptic it lies close enough to the Sun's path for the Moon to pass in front of it, and now and then a planet: Venus covered it on 10 November 1783 and won't again until 2 September 2197.

[Arcturus](/astrology/arcturus/) has nearly the same zodiac degree, 24°36′ Libra, but stands thirty degrees north of the ecliptic. Sky-watchers join the two with an old rhyme: follow the curve of the Plough's handle, arc to Arcturus, then spike to Spica.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Its name is Latin, *spica virginis*, the Virgin's ear of grain, and older names point the same way. A Sumerian star list from Uruk calls it the Seed-Furrow; an Arabic name, Sumbalet, means an ear of grain; another, al-simāk al-aʽzal, calls it the unarmed simāk, a word whose meaning has been lost. In Hindu astronomy it is the lunar station Chitrā.

Spica has a place in the history of astronomy as well. It is believed to be the star whose positions led Hipparchus to discover that the equinoxes slowly slide against the stars ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)), and centuries later Copernicus measured it with a home-made triquetrum for the same question.

In the Behenian list the ear of wheat is a star of Venus and Mercury, its stone the emerald and its plant sage; [Algorab](/astrology/algorab/), the crow's star at 13° Libra, is the Behenian star just before it along the zodiac.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

About 1 person in 181 born between 1930 and 2009 has the [Sun conjunct Spica](/astrology/sun-conjunct-spica/), a late-Libra Sun of 16–17 October. Among its other contacts the odds spread widely, from about 1 in 155 for [Mars conjunct Spica](/astrology/mars-conjunct-spica/) through 1 in 187 for [Moon conjunct Spica](/astrology/moon-conjunct-spica/) and 1 in 218 for [Venus conjunct Spica](/astrology/venus-conjunct-spica/) to 1 in 332 for [Saturn conjunct Spica](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-spica/), which takes in only the births of November 1952 to August 1953, and of September and October 1982.

With Spica so near the Sun's path, the planets that share its degree really do pass close by it, within a few degrees, unlike Arcturus at the same degree thirty degrees up. Even so, the ear of wheat stays out of my readings, along with every other star ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). Its oldest image is still worth keeping: a furrow, a seed, an ear of grain, a meaning about growth rather than a promise of harvest.
