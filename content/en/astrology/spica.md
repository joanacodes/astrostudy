Spica is the ear of wheat in the hand of Virgo, the Maiden, and the star thought to have shown Hipparchus that the equinoxes move. In 2026 it stands at 24°12′ Libra, just two degrees south of the Sun's path.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Fewer than twenty stars of the night sky outshine Spica, α Virginis, at magnitude 0.98. Most of its light comes from a pair of hot blue stars so close together that they are egg-shaped rather than round, about 250 light-years away.

Virgo's brightest star belongs, in the tropical zodiac, to [Libra](/astrology/libra/): 22°51′ in 1930, 23°50′ in 2000, 24°12′ in 2026 ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)). At 2.1° south of the ecliptic it lies close enough to the Sun's path for the Moon to pass in front of it, and now and then a planet: Venus covered it on 10 November 1783 and won't again until 2 September 2197, and each year, around 16 October, the Sun slips past a little over two degrees north of it.

[Arcturus](/astrology/arcturus/) has nearly the same zodiac degree, 24°36′ Libra, but stands thirty degrees north of the ecliptic. Sky-watchers join the two with an old rhyme: follow the curve of the Plough's handle, arc to Arcturus, then spike to Spica.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Its name is Latin, *spica virginis*, the Virgin's ear of grain, and older names point the same way. A Sumerian star list from Uruk calls it the Seed-Furrow; an Arabic name, Sumbalet, means an ear of grain; another, al-simāk al-aʽzal, calls it the unarmed simāk, a word whose meaning has been lost. In Hindu astronomy it is the lunar station Chitrā.

Spica has a place in the history of astronomy as well. It is believed to be the star whose positions led Hipparchus to discover that the equinoxes slowly slide against the stars ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)), and centuries later Copernicus measured it with a home-made triquetrum for the same question.

The Behenian list of medieval magic places the ear of wheat under Venus and Mercury, with the emerald for its stone and sage for its plant; [Algorab](/astrology/algorab/), the crow's star at 13° Libra, is the Behenian star just before it along the zodiac.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The [Sun conjunct Spica](/astrology/sun-conjunct-spica/), a late-Libra Sun of 16–17 October, comes to about 1 person in 181 in the births counted from 1930 to 2009. [Mars conjunct Spica](/astrology/mars-conjunct-spica/) is its most common contact, about 1 in 155; [Moon conjunct Spica](/astrology/moon-conjunct-spica/) runs to about 1 in 187, [Venus conjunct Spica](/astrology/venus-conjunct-spica/) to 1 in 218. [Saturn conjunct Spica](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-spica/) is the rare one, about 1 in 332: Saturn sat on it only from November 1952 to August 1953, and in September and October 1982.

With Spica so near the Sun's path, the planets that share its degree really do pass close by it, within a few degrees, unlike Arcturus at the same degree thirty degrees up. Even so, the ear of wheat stays out of my readings, along with every other star ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). Its oldest image is worth keeping even so: a furrow, a seed, an ear of grain, a meaning about growth rather than a promise of harvest.
