Two things in astrology carry Fortune's name, and they're easy to mix up. One is the [Part of Fortune](/astrology/part-of-fortune/), the best known of the ancient lots, a point worked out from the Ascendant, the Sun and the Moon. The other is asteroid 19, Fortuna, a dark rock more than 200 km across. This page is about the rock.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Fortuna is one of the largest asteroids in the main belt, and nearly round, as the Hubble Space Telescope showed in 1993. It is also one of the darkest bodies of its size: a weathered surface rich in primitive organic compounds, rather like that of [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/). It goes round the Sun every 3.8 years, between 2.1 and 2.8 times the Earth's distance, on an orbit tilted less than 2° to the Sun's path, and it belongs to no known family of asteroids.

John Russell Hind found it on the evening of 22 August 1852, observing from George Bishop's observatory in London: a ninth-magnitude point with a yellowish tint, perhaps the atmosphere's doing. Bishop encouraged him to choose a name, and he chose the Roman goddess of fortune and misfortune. No symbol came with the announcement, but Hind's *An Astronomical Vocabulary*, published the same year, printed one without explaining it: a star over Fortune's wheel. Two years later Hind found [Urania](/astrology/urania/).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Fortuna was the Roman goddess of luck, good and bad, and of life's sudden turns; the Greeks called her Tyche. She was shown with a ship's rudder for steering fate, a ball or a wheel, and a horn of plenty, and sometimes veiled and blind. One of her titles was Automatia, she who does what she will, and her name may come from Vortumna, she who turns the year. Romans made dedications to Fortuna Mala, bad fortune, as well as to the kinder forms. The wheel outlasted Rome: the medieval image of Fortune's wheel, with one figure rising, one crowned at the top, one falling and one at the bottom without a kingdom, goes back to the second book of Boethius's *Consolation of Philosophy*.

Fortune also had a counterweight in another goddess. [Nemesis](/astrology/nemesis/), whose name first meant giving each what is due, came to be seen as the balancer of fortune's chance, and by the third century AD some worshipped a single, all-powerful Nemesis-Fortuna.

In a chart, the two Fortunes behave in opposite ways. The Part of Fortune is calculated and moves with the Ascendant, round the whole zodiac in about a day, so without an exact birth time there is no Part to speak of. The asteroid is observed: its degree comes from its orbit, changes slowly, and is much the same for everyone born that day; only its house needs the time ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). The Part is personal by construction; the asteroid could only be made personal by its house and by a tight contact with a planet or an angle.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I leave the asteroid Fortuna out, and with this one I want to be plain about why. A body called Fortune invites questions about luck and money, and no chart can answer those: I won't tell anyone that a placement promises a windfall or warns of a loss ([Can astrology pick investments?](/astrology/can-astrology-pick-investments/)).

The plainer reason is that it has never been tested. My notebook put a tick or a cross beside the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith; about a third held overall, and three placements, Saturn, Chiron and Lilith, held far more often than the rest ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). The asteroid had no line on that invoice, and the system behind my readings doesn't compute it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

If it's the old idea of fortune you're after, what befalls you rather than what you win, tradition put it in the Part of Fortune, and that page explains how it's worked out and why it needs your birth time.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Fortuna and the Part of Fortune are the same thing.”** One is an asteroid more than 200 km across; the other is a point calculated from three parts of your chart.
- **“Fortuna was the goddess of good luck.”** She brought both kinds. Romans made dedications to doubtful, fickle and bad fortune too.
- **“A strong Fortuna means money.”** No placement promises money, and this asteroid has never been put to any test.
