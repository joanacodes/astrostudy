Picture a train: carriages coupled one behind another along most of a loop of track, with an empty stretch of line ahead. That's the image behind the locomotive chart, the commonest of the shapes in the count behind this site.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

In a locomotive, the ten planets fit within 240°, two-thirds of the circle, and a whole third, about four signs, holds none of them. Within the train, the planets can bunch up or spread out; what defines the shape is that single empty stretch of at least a third of the circle. It's one of the seven shapes [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) described, and the name is his picture of it.

One planet leads. In the descriptions that follow Jones, it's the first planet after the empty third, counting clockwise, the direction in which the whole chart turns through a day: the planet that pulls the rest along behind it. The last planet of the group, on the other side of the gap, closes the train.

Gather the carriages into a third of the circle and the chart becomes a [bundle](/astrology/bundle-chart/), with two-thirds empty instead of one. Spread them further, round most of the wheel, and the empty third shrinks until no clear gap is left.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

In the count behind this site the locomotive beats every other shape: about 2 people in 5 born from 1930 to 2009 have all ten planets within 240° but not within 180°, which the count leaves to the bowl and the bundle. The rule is simple, and charts close to its edges switch shape with a few degrees of difference; the Ascendant, the Midheaven, Chiron, Lilith and the nodes weren't counted, and any of them may sit in the empty third.

Astrologers who use the shapes read a locomotive as drive: a chart that pushes towards what it lacks, with its leading planet setting the pace and much depending on it. I take the image, and keep it in proportion. The empty third is the direction the chart keeps moving into, the experience it goes looking for. The leading planet gets a closer look, through its function, sign and house, because it's where the train meets the open track. But one planet at the front doesn't decide a chart, and no shape fixes a character: a cluster or a spread describes emphasis, which the planets themselves have to confirm.

Where the empty third falls needs the birth time: above or below the horizon, east or west ([hemisphere emphasis](/astrology/hemisphere-emphasis/)), and across which houses. The Moon matters too, since it may be the planet that leads the train or the one that closes it, and its place changes by about a sign every two and a half days. Without the time I couldn't tell you which part of your life the train is heading into, so I [read it only with the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/).

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Find the leading planet.** Name its function: it often shows how you set off, and what you set off for.
- **Look at the gap.** The signs and houses of the empty third describe what the chart reaches for. Going there on purpose is easier than being pulled.
- **Pace the train.** A chart that keeps moving can forget to stop. Rest is part of the journey, not a breakdown.
- **Remember the carriages.** Every planet in the group still has its own work; the shape only shows that they travel together.

When the planets split into two groups facing each other, with empty space on both sides, the train breaks into a [seesaw](/astrology/seesaw-chart/) instead. A locomotive reads best with an eye on both its engine and its open line.
