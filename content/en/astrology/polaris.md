Polaris is the North Star, the one point of the northern sky that seems to stay put while everything else turns around it. In the zodiac it has a degree like any star, 28°55′ Gemini, and that degree says almost nothing about where it is: 66° from the Sun's path.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Polaris is α Ursae Minoris, marking the tail of the Little Bear. Its magnitude is 2.02 in the Yale catalogue, about the 47th-brightest star, not the brightest, as is often believed. It lay 0.66° from the north celestial pole in 2018 and will come closest, about 0.45°, soon after 2100. From the southern hemisphere it can't be seen at all.

Its zodiac degree, 28°55′ [Gemini](/astrology/gemini/) for 2026 and 28°33′ for 2000, will tip over into [Cancer](/astrology/cancer/) early in the 2100s. Its latitude is 66.1° north of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), so the star is far closer to the pole of the ecliptic, 24° away, than to any planet ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Polaris hasn't always marked the pole. In antiquity the pole lay in an empty patch of sky about 10° from it, and the Greek sailor Pytheas, around 320 BCE, described it as starless. Ptolemy recorded Polaris as a star of the third magnitude; research reported in *Science* in 2004 suggests it is about two and a half times brighter now.

The name stella polaris, polar star, dates from the Renaissance and first appeared in print in the Alfonsine Tables of 1492. Old English called it the ship-star, later English the lodestar; Arabic astronomy knew it as al-jady, the kid; the Hindu Puranas personify it as Dhruva, the immovable. Shakespeare gave Julius Caesar the line "as constant as the northern star", though in Caesar's day no star stood at the pole.

The Bedouin of the Negev and Sinai steered by two stars and set them against each other: Polaris, always visible and so steadfast, and [Canopus](/astrology/canopus/), which dips below their horizon and so stood for a changeable nature. Steadiness runs through nearly all these names, and it belongs to the sky, to the pole, rather than to a degree of Gemini.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Polaris shares its zodiac degree with [Betelgeuse](/astrology/betelgeuse/): 28°55′ against 29°07′ Gemini, while the two stars are 82° apart in the sky. A planet at 29° Gemini is "conjunct" both by the tradition's one-degree rule, which shows plainly what a conjunction to a star this far north is: a shared number, not a shared place ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). The Sun stands on that number for births on about 19 and 20 June.

Astrologers who want the real sky look instead at which stars rise, culminate or set with a planet ([parans](/astrology/parans/)). Polaris never rises or sets for anyone well north of the equator, so for it even that comes down to the moment it crosses the meridian.

[Acubens](/astrology/acubens/), the faint claw of the Crab, is the opposite case: close enough to the Sun's path for the Moon to hide it. I describe both here and read neither, since the stars aren't part of the system I built for my readings ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).
