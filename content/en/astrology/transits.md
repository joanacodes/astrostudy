Your birth chart is a still picture: the sky at one minute. The sky itself kept moving, and it's still moving now. Transits are what happens when you lay the moving sky over the still one and watch where they meet.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A transit is a planet in the sky today making an aspect to a point in your birth chart: *transiting Saturn square natal Sun* means Saturn, where it is now, stands 90° from where your Sun was when you were born. A transit can also be a planet crossing one of your houses: [Saturn transiting the 7th house](/astrology/saturn-transit-7th-house/) usually takes two to three years.

What a transit does is set by the two parts involved: the moving planet's function, the natal point's function, and the aspect between them. It doesn't bring anything into the chart that wasn't there. It brings out, for a while, what was. Robert Hand's *Planets in Transit* (1976) describes them one transit at a time.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

You need your birth chart and the planets' positions for the period you're looking at, from an ephemeris or a program. Then it's a matter of noticing when a moving planet comes within orb of an aspect to a natal point, and for how long.

The orbs I use for timing run from 1½° before exact to 1° after, and the planets I follow are the slow ones: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, Pluto, Chiron and the true node. With those orbs, here is how long one transit to one point lasts, counted from the planets' real motion:

- **Jupiter:** about twelve days when it passes straight over; about eight months when a retrograde loop takes it over the point three times, which happens about three times in ten.
- **Saturn:** usually about nine months, often with three exact passes; as little as three weeks when it crosses once.
- **Uranus:** about twenty months.
- **Neptune:** usually about two and a half years, sometimes closer to two.
- **Pluto:** usually between two and three years, sometimes nearly four.
- **Chiron:** anywhere from three weeks to nearly two years, ten months typically, because its orbit is so stretched.
- **The true node:** about a month, now and then three.

Transits to planets need only your birth date, except the Moon's exact degree. Transits to the Ascendant and Midheaven, and through the houses, need your birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

Transits show seasons: when a part of the chart is under emphasis, in what manner, and for how long. A Saturn transit tends to ask for structure and patience around whatever it touches; a Jupiter transit for growth and perspective; Uranus for change and room to move; Neptune for imagination and letting go; Pluto for deep reworking. The natal point says where.

Some come to everyone at about the same age, because a slow planet comes back to its own place: the [Saturn return](/astrology/saturn-return/) first between 28.4 and 29.9, the [Uranus opposition](/astrology/uranus-opposition/) between about 39 and 44½, the [Neptune square](/astrology/neptune-square/) between about 39½ and 42. The [Pluto square](/astrology/pluto-square/) comes at very different ages depending on the birth year.

Others are rare, and that's what makes them personal. By 80, people born between 1930 and 2009 meet [transiting Saturn square natal Sun](/astrology/transiting-saturn-square-natal-sun/) five or six times, and Jupiter conjunct their Sun six or seven times, but more than half never see [transiting Pluto conjunct natal Sun](/astrology/transiting-pluto-conjunct-natal-sun/) at all.

I read transits alongside [secondary progressions](/astrology/secondary-progressions/), solar arcs, the [solar return](/astrology/solar-return/), profections and zodiacal releasing. A theme that several of them point to at once is worth attention; a transit on its own is a lighter accent.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

A transit is weather, not a forecast. It describes the conditions, and you decide what to do in them. Several transits at once change one another, the same transit lands differently in two lives, and the birth chart always comes first: no transit can make a chart into something else.

What a transit can't do matters just as much. It can't tell you whether you'll fall ill, die, become pregnant, win a case, make money or break up, and I never read it for those ([how I read](/how-i-read/)). A hard transit from Saturn or Pluto is a demanding season, never a sentence. And an app that lists every transit of the Moon and Mercury, hour by hour, is listing moods, not turning points.
