Some patterns look dramatic on paper. The yod is a long, thin triangle with its point aimed at a single planet, and it has been called the finger of God, a name that has frightened more people than any aspect deserves. It's a shape of constant adjustment, and a fairly common one.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

A yod has three planets. Two sit about 60° apart, in [sextile](/astrology/sextile/), at the base of the triangle; the third sits about 150° from each, a [quincunx](/astrology/quincunx/) to both, at the far point. The two long sides are equal, the base is short, and the apex lies opposite the midpoint of the base. In signs, the base pair usually falls two signs apart, in complementary elements, and the apex in the sign opposite the one between them: base planets in Aries and Gemini point to Scorpio, across the chart from Taurus. The apex sign then shares no element, modality or polarity with either base sign. That's the character of the figure: a pair that cooperates easily, and a third planet with no common ground with either of them.

*Yod* is the tenth letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and the pattern's other name, finger of God, comes from its look: a long finger pointing at one planet. They're names for a shape. In my reading, they promise nothing beyond it. Its neighbour in this family, the [T-square](/astrology/t-square/), also gathers its tension at one planet; there the apex pushes back, here it bends to fit.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

About 1 person in 8 born from 1930 to 2009 has a yod among the ten planets, in the count behind this site, with each quincunx held within 3° and the sextile within 8° when the Sun or Moon is involved, 6° otherwise. For a pattern with such a reputation, that's common. The tight quincunx orb matters: allow a degree more and many more yods appear, a degree less and many disappear. The count also leaves out the angles, Chiron, Lilith and the nodes, any of which can stand at a point of the triangle.

The system I built for my readings describes the quincunx as a relation of adjustment and recalibration: not conflict, not failure, and not something that settles by itself. The sextile at the base is cooperation that needs taking up to become anything. Put together, I read a yod as two functions that work well as a pair and a third that has to keep re-tuning itself to both, because there's no shared ground where they could meet halfway. Over the years, that re-tuning tends to turn into precision: the apex learns, by sheer repetition, a way of working that suits two very different neighbours.

None of this is a destiny. A chart describes how you're inclined to move, not where you must end up ([how I read](/how-i-read/)), and a yod is no exception, whatever its name.

The houses of the base and the apex need the time of birth: the apex's house is the area of life where the adjusting goes on, the base's houses the areas it adjusts to. A yod with the Moon at one of its points is the most time-sensitive of all: with a 3° orb on the quincunxes and the Moon moving about a sign every two and a half days, it can be there in the morning and gone by the evening. That's why I [don't read a chart without the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/).

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Learn what the apex needs.** Its function, in its sign, has needs the base doesn't share. Meeting them on purpose is easier than waiting to be thrown off balance.
- **Adjust in small steps.** Recalibration works best as a habit: a timetable changed a little, a plan reworked, rather than one big overhaul.
- **Use the base pair.** The sextile at the bottom of the triangle is real help, and it only works when you take it up.
- **Look across from the apex.** The point opposite it, at the base's midpoint, is where a fourth planet would turn the figure into a [boomerang yod](/astrology/boomerang-yod/); its sign and house are worth a look even when it's empty.

A finger pointing at one planet is a good place to start a reading, and a poor place to stop.
