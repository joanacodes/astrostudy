Linda Goodman made astrology a best-seller. *Sun Signs*, in 1968, was the first astrology book to reach *The New York Times* best-seller list, and a profile in *The New Yorker* in 2026 presented her as the astrologer who brought astrology to the masses.

## Who {#who}

She was born Mary Alice Kemery. She started out writing for newspapers in West Virginia and Kentucky and made her name in radio: in 1957 she hosted a show on WJAS in Pittsburgh called *Love Letters from Linda*. She also wrote speeches for Whitney Young, the civil rights leader who served for several years as president of the National Urban League.

*Linda Goodman's Sun Signs* came out from Taplinger in 1968 ([Linda Goodman's Sun Signs (1968)](/astrology/linda-goodmans-sun-signs-1968/)), and *Linda Goodman's Love Signs* from Harper & Row in 1978. Both reached the best-seller list, and the paperback rights to *Love Signs* sold for $2.25 million, at the time a record for a non-fiction book. Between and after them came *Venus Trines at Midnight* (1970), *Linda Goodman's Love Poems* (1980), *Linda Goodman's Star Signs* (1987), subtitled *The Secret Codes of the Universe: A Practical Guide for the New Age*, and *Gooberz* (1989).

She spent her last years in Colorado and died in 1995. *Relationship Signs* appeared in 1998, brought out by Crystal Bush, who had obtained the publicity rights to her name. A biography by Karen Christino, *Dazzled by the Light: Linda Goodman, Pop Astrology and the New Age*, came out in 2026.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

She took astrology to the mass market. Her books arrived in the 1960s and 1970s, as astrology was crossing into the mainstream, and the writer Rachel Syme credits her with converting it from “a niche interest into the center of the Zeitgeist”.

She made the Sun sign the front door. It's the one placement almost anyone can find from a birthday, and any page about it speaks to about 1 person in 12 at once, so it has to speak broadly; a broad sketch can still feel written for you, as the psychologist Bertram Forer showed ([The Barnum (Forer) effect](/astrology/barnum-forer-effect/)). A chart, by contrast, is drawn for one minute and one place. In my own notebook, the Sun sign was among the placements everyone reads first, and it held about a third of the time, 30 to 40%, much as a flattering horoscope would; Saturn, Chiron and Lilith, at 75 to 90%, were the ones that stood out ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). So I read the [Sun](/astrology/sun/) with the rest of the chart, rising sign and houses included, and those need the birth time ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). The research on Sun signs, and on people who come to describe themselves in their sign's terms, has a page of its own ([Sun signs and personality: the studies](/astrology/sun-signs-and-personality-the-studies/)).

She brought the signs into love: *Love Signs*, as its title says, set them to work on couples. Set side by side, two Sun signs are one placement from each chart. How two people comfort each other, what draws them and how they argue sit in other placements, and the houses need both birth times ([Sun-sign compatibility, and why it isn't enough](/astrology/sun-sign-compatibility-and-why-it-isnt-enough/)).

She had a theory of her own about the sky. In *Sun Signs* she proposed that a planet or other body has no effect at all until it is discovered, in “its proper time in the universal plan”. On that view Uranus counts from 1781, Neptune from 1846 and Pluto from 1930 ([How the outer planets got their names](/astrology/how-the-outer-planets-got-their-names/)).

Thirteen years before *Sun Signs*, in 1955, [Michel Gauquelin](/astrology/michel-gauquelin/) had reported that his statistics found no evidence for the signs. Eight years after it, [Liz Greene](/astrology/liz-greene/)'s *Saturn* turned astrology inward.
