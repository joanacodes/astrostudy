Every chart I read is written in a shorthand older than most of the techniques in it. The glyphs began as scribes' abbreviations and the attributes of painted gods, and knowing that is the best protection against reading too much into their strokes.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

The oldest forms are Greek, on papyrus. In the original Greek horoscopes, the Sun is a circle with a single ray and the Moon a crescent. The symbols for Mercury, Venus, Jupiter and Saturn can be traced to forms in some of the latest horoscopes found at Oxyrhynchus. Jupiter's is a monogram of zeta, for Zeus. Saturn's began as kappa-rho, for Kronos, with a bar marking the abbreviation: the bar sits on the rho in a papyrus of 376 and is gone in one of 481. Mercury's is a stylised caduceus. The ideal form of the Mars symbol is uncertain; Alexander Jones, who published those papyri, thinks it perhaps unrelated to the later circle with an arrow.

Some shapes are older than the abbreviations. In 1934 Annie Maunder pointed to Bianchini's planisphere, made in the second century, on which the planetary gods carry early versions of the symbols as attributes: Mercury a caduceus, Venus a cord joining two necklaces, Mars a spear, Jupiter a staff, Saturn a scythe, the Sun a circlet of rays, the Moon a crescent on her headdress.

Byzantine scribes kept the system and widened it. The medieval codices in which many ancient horoscopes survive carry symbols for the planets, the signs, the aspects, the lots and the lunar nodes. In the twelfth-century *Compendium of Astrology* of Johannes Kamateros, the Sun is still a circle with a ray, Jupiter a zeta and Mars a shield crossed by a spear, and Mercury and Venus have no cross at the foot.

The shapes in my charts were settled in print. The crosses under Mercury and Venus appear around the sixteenth century, and woodcuts of 1506 and 1550 still disagree about them; Maunder read them as “an attempt to give a savour of Christianity to the symbols of the old pagan gods”. The Sun's circle with a dot first appears in the Renaissance too. Even the retrograde mark is a borrowing: ℞, an R with a stroke across its tail, abbreviated words beginning with R, *recipe* on a prescription among them, and William Lilly's *Christian Astrology*, in an edition of 1659, prints it beside a retrograde Jupiter ([Natal retrograde planets](/blog/retrograde-planets/)).

Every discovery needed a new sign. For [Uranus](/astrology/uranus/), found in 1781, Johann Gottfried Köhler offered the symbol of platinum, gold's circle joined to iron's arrow; in 1784 Jérôme Lalande proposed instead “a globe surmounted by the first letter of your name”, as he wrote to William Herschel. That H over a circle became the astrologers' Uranus, while astronomers lean to Köhler's. Neptune took a trident, though older French books mark it with an L and a V, for Le Verrier. Pluto's discoverers announced its name and its PL monogram on 1 May 1930, for Pluto and for Percival Lowell; astrology mostly uses another symbol, an orb over Pluto's bident, popularised by Paul Clancy's *American Astrology* magazine. The names have their own story ([How the outer planets got their names](/astrology/how-the-outer-planets-got-their-names/)).

The asteroids got symbols as they were found: a sickle for [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/) in 1801, the spear of Pallas in 1802, a sceptre topped by a star for Juno, and for Vesta an altar with its sacred fire, designed by Carl Friedrich Gauss. From 1851 astronomers replaced such symbols with numbers in circles, though the first four kept theirs for some years. Astrologers took them up again in the 1970s, Vesta's in a simplified form attributed to Eleanor Bach ([Vesta](/astrology/vesta/)). [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/)'s key, proposed in 1977 by the astrologer Al Morrison, is a monogram as well: O and K, for ‘Object Kowal’, after its discoverer Charles Kowal.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

A shared alphabet. A chart drawn with these signs can be read by anyone who knows them, whatever language they speak, and the same marks long served astronomers. Almanacs wrote a planet's longitude as a sign and degrees, giving the sign by its glyph or by a number from 0 to 11, and ♈ still marks the March equinox, the point the [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/) counts from.

A habit of signing. The modern glyphs often carry a person's initials, H for Herschel, L and V for Le Verrier, P and L for Lowell, O and K for Kowal, where the ancient ones carry a god's name or attribute. Both kinds are labels, not portraits.

And a caution I keep. Since most planet symbols began as abbreviations, and the crosses came late, I don't read meaning into their strokes: the weight [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/) carries in a chart comes from what the planet does there, in its sign, its house and its aspects, not from the cross on its glyph. The sign glyphs stand for the old figures of the zodiac, and the stories behind those figures are told in [The myths behind the signs and planets](/astrology/myths-behind-the-signs-and-planets/). To learn them all at a glance, [Glyphs: the cheat sheet](/astrology/glyphs-the-cheat-sheet/) puts them on one page.
