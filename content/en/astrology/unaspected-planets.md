Most planets in a chart are tied to others by aspects, the set distances that connect them across the wheel. Now and then one isn't: no conjunction, no sextile, no square, no trine, no opposition. Astrologers call it unaspected.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

An aspect is a set distance between two planets: 0° for a [conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/), 60° for a [sextile](/astrology/sextile/), 90° for a [square](/astrology/square/), 120° for a [trine](/astrology/trine/), 180° for an [opposition](/astrology/opposition/). An unaspected planet makes none of these five to any other planet, within the orbs in use. The orb is the leeway allowed either side of the exact distance, and it varies: some astrologers are generous with it, others strict.

So “unaspected” is never a property a planet carries by itself. It's a result of method: which aspects count, how wide the orbs are, whether the angles and the nodes join in. A planet that stands alone in one table can carry a wide trine in another. Before I read anything into it, I check which rules produced it.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Minor aspects complicate the picture. In 1619 Johannes Kepler proposed the quintile (72°), the biquintile (144°) and the sesquiquadrate (135°), and many astrologers today use other minor aspects as well. A planet with no major aspect may still be linked to the rest by one of these, more quietly.

The birth time matters more here than it seems. The Moon goes round the zodiac in about 27⅓ days, covering some 13° a day, so over a few hours it can move into orb with another planet or out of it: a Moon unaspected at dawn may be in a square by the evening. Aspects to the Ascendant and the Midheaven need the time as well. With a planet this solitary, a few hours decide whether it's alone at all, which is one more reason I don't read a chart without the birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read an unaspected planet as a function running on its own track. Aspects are how planets talk to each other; without them, a planet misses the constant negotiation the others go through. It still works, through its sign and its house, but it can feel separate from the rest of the person: fully on or fully off, slow to blend with everything else, sometimes the trait people notice precisely because it doesn't match the pattern around it.

Where the planet stands changes a great deal. On an angle, an unaspected planet still shows plainly, because the angle puts it on view ([Angular planets](/astrology/angular-planets/)). Away from the angles, in a cadent house, it may work quietly enough to be overlooked for a long time.

Take an unaspected Mercury. I'd look at how thinking and talking happen for that person: perhaps in their own way and at their own moment, not easily fed into the feelings or the drive of the rest of the chart. I describe that, and I leave room for the person to tell me whether it fits.

Being unaspected isn't the same as being alone in one part of the chart. A planet can make several aspects and still be the only one in its half of the wheel or in its element; that's a different kind of solitude, and it has its own page ([Singletons](/astrology/singletons/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“An unaspected planet doesn't work.”** It works through its sign and its house. What it lacks is constant contact with the other planets.
- **“It's the strongest planet in the chart.”** Some astrologers say so, some say the opposite. I'd rather call it distinct than rank it.
- **“Every program shows the same unaspected planets.”** Orbs and the list of aspects vary, and so does the answer.
- **“Unaspected means unlucky.”** Aspects don't hand out luck. It describes a way of working, not a fortune.
