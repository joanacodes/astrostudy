The horizon and the meridian cross at the centre of a chart, and the cross leaves four quarters. Astrologers call them quadrants, and counting the planets in each is one step finer than counting them by half.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Each quadrant runs from one angle to the next, in the order the houses are numbered. The first goes from the Ascendant down to the IC and holds houses 1 to 3. The second runs from the IC to the Descendant: houses 4 to 6. The third climbs from the Descendant to the Midheaven: houses 7 to 9. The fourth comes back from the Midheaven to the Ascendant: houses 10 to 12. Every quadrant belongs to two halves of the chart at once; the first, for instance, is both below the horizon and in the east ([Hemisphere emphasis](/astrology/hemisphere-emphasis/)).

The word has a second use, which confuses people. Placidus, Koch, Porphyry and the other quadrant house systems are named after the way they work: they take these four quarters, fixed by the angles, and divide each one into three houses. The quarters are identical in all of them; only the cusps inside differ ([Which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The quarters are rarely equal. The Ascendant and the Midheaven are seldom exactly 90° apart: the distance between them along the zodiac changes as the sky turns, and with the latitude of the birthplace. So one quarter of your wheel may span a long stretch of the zodiac and the next a short one. A wide quarter catches more planets by sheer size, and that's the first thing I check when a quadrant looks crowded.

All four boundaries are angles, and the angles come from the minute and place of birth. The sky carries every planet clockwise round the houses once a day, so a planet in the first quadrant, just below the eastern horizon, has risen into the fourth a few hours later. Get the hour wrong and the whole frame moves, not just a label. I need the time before I read anything ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read each quarter through what its three houses share. The first, houses 1 to 3, gathers the self, its resources and its way of learning and talking: energy there tends to build a person from the inside. The second, houses 4 to 6, gathers home and roots, creativity and daily craft: energy there tends to show close to home and in the work of every day. The third, houses 7 to 9, gathers partnership, what is shared, and wider horizons of study and belief: energy there tends to grow through other people and other ways of seeing. The fourth, houses 10 to 12, gathers public role, friendship and community, and the inner life: energy there tends to turn towards the world at large, and to what lies beyond it.

A crowded quadrant tells me where the chart's energy gathers; it doesn't hollow out the others. An empty quarter isn't an empty part of life. I follow the rulers of its three cusps to wherever they stand, and the trail shows how that ground gets worked from elsewhere in the chart.

When three quarters are busy and one holds a single planet, that planet draws my eye. It's the only voice in its part of the chart, which is the idea behind [singletons](/astrology/singletons/).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The first quadrant is the most important.”** The numbering follows the houses, not a ranking. Each quarter covers its own ground.
- **“An empty quadrant means that part of life won't happen.”** Empty means no planet there, not no life there. Its houses still have signs, and their rulers are at work somewhere else.
- **“Quadrants are equal slices of 90°.”** Rarely. The angles set the boundaries, and they're seldom square to one another.
- **“Quadrants and quadrant houses are the same thing.”** One is a quarter of the chart; the other is a way of dividing that quarter into houses.
