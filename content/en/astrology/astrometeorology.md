My motto for transits is that they're weather, not a forecast. Astrometeorology took the sky at its word and tried to forecast the weather itself: rain, frost, wind and harvests, read from the planets. For centuries, learned people thought it could be done.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Astrometeorology, from the Greek words for a star, for what is high in the sky and for a branch of knowledge, is the belief that the positions and motions of the Sun, Moon and planets can be used to predict both the weather and the climate of a season. Through most of its history it was a scholarly tradition, common in learned circles and close to astronomy, alchemy, meteorology and medicine.

Its roots are among the oldest in astrology. Babylonian records set weather beside planetary configurations, and a history of meteorology credits the Babylonian astronomer-priests with founding astrometeorology by connecting what happened in the air with the movements of the heavens. Historians of ancient science count weather forecasting as the oldest branch of astronomy and astrology in the Greek tradition, with traces as early as the poet Hesiod, around 700 BC. In the second century, Ptolemy gave much of the second book of the *Tetrabiblos* to it ([Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)).

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

Ptolemy's method starts from the seasons. The new or full moon just before the Sun enters Aries sets the tone of the year's weather, and the lunations before its entries into Cancer, Libra and Capricorn refine each season; monthly charts, the planets' conjunctions and the fixed stars add detail, and shooting stars, clouds and rainbows add local colour ([Planetary ingresses](/astrology/planetary-ingresses/)). Behind it lay the physics of his day: the cycles of the sky were thought to heat, cool, moisten or dry the air.

Later astrologers built on that frame:

- **Counting testimonies.** In his *De impressionibus aeris*, the medieval scholar Robert Grosseteste had planets gain strength from signs of a similar nature and lose it in opposing ones; the planet with the most testimony decided the weather.
- **Testing by observation.** From 1593, [Johannes Kepler](/astrology/johannes-kepler/) recorded the weather to support his belief that a conjunction of the Sun and [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/) brought cold, and from 1598 he produced calendars with forecasts based on astrology. He held that the Earth itself responded to the sky, much as people were thought to.
- **Keeping journals.** In the mid-seventeenth century, reformers tried to make astrology more empirical, with weather diaries and instrument readings; John Goad's large *Astro-Meteorologica* (1686) came out of that movement.

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

That astrology once claimed the whole world as its subject, the air and the fields as much as kingdoms and bodies. The weather was read with the same tools as [mundane astrology](/astrology/mundane-astrology/), the seasonal charts and the eclipses, and the economy was often forecast in the same breath ([Financial astrology, in history](/astrology/financial-astrology-in-history/)).

The surviving medieval texts are telling. They dwell on strange weather and on events that weren't meteorological at all, which suggests a practice of scholars more than of farmers.

Some of what it noticed was real. In the ninth century, [Abu Ma'shar](/astrology/abu-mashar/) recorded that the tides rose with the position of the Moon, though he put it down to an astrological virtue that drew the sea. Gravity explains the tides; nothing comparable ties the planets to next week's rain.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

It didn't work, and science moved on. Astrometeorology stayed in mainstream scientific discussion through the seventeenth century and had fallen out of favour by about 1704. Societies devoted to it lasted in Britain until the mid-nineteenth century, without being taken seriously by scientists. In the twenty-first century, some farmers in India still time their work by the nakshatras, the lunar mansions of Vedic astrology ([Vedic astrology (Jyotish)](/astrology/vedic-astrology-jyotish/)), but a study of the rainfall found it too variable within those periods to rely on.

I forecast no weather, in the sky or in a life ([how I read](/how-i-read/)). For the weather outside, the forecast to trust is the meteorologists'.
