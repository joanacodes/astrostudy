Mars conjunct Antares puts the red planet on the red star named after it: Antares means rival of Ares, the star that could be taken for Mars. About 1 person in 210 born between 1930 and 2009 has Mars within a degree of it.

## What it means {#what-it-means}

I read [Mars](/astrology/mars/) as effort and initiative: the part of you that starts, competes, protects and keeps going. [Antares](/astrology/antares/) was named for its likeness to that planet. In Greek it is the rival of Ares, or more bluntly not-Ares, and Egyptian astronomers called it the red one of the prow. In South Australia the Ngarrindjeri people knew it as Waiyungari, the red man, and noticed its slow changes of brightness. With Mars on it, the tradition sees the same colour twice. I don't read a doubled red as a doubled temper. Mars describes how you act, and courage is not recklessness any more than assertion is aggression; the star's old heat adds a tone, not an outcome.

The star is the heart of the constellation Scorpius, and Mars is the traditional ruler of [Scorpio](/astrology/scorpio/), which modern astrologers give to Pluto; I read both. But the sign that counts in a chart is the one the star now stands in, Sagittarius, at around 9° or 10° for births from 1930 to 2009. This is a [Mars in Sagittarius](/astrology/mars-in-sagittarius/), with its own sign, house and aspects to read ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).

Mars comes to Antares about every two years and is usually past it within a few days. Once in these eighty years it stayed. In 1969 Mars crossed the star in March, turned retrograde further on in Sagittarius, came back over it at the start of June and crossed it a third time in August. Around 31 May that year, Mars, moving backwards, stood on Antares while the Sun stood on [Aldebaran](/astrology/aldebaran/), directly opposite: both red royal stars occupied at once ([Mars retrograde in Sagittarius](/astrology/mars-retrograde-in-sagittarius/), [natal retrograde planets](/blog/retrograde-planets/)). The tradition counts conjunctions only, so even a chart like that is read one star at a time: Mars with Antares, the Sun with Aldebaran.

In the sky they keep their distance. Antares lies 4.6° south of the ecliptic, and Mars, like most planets, goes by to the north of it: in these eighty years, by between about one and five and a half degrees. On the closest passes, as in August 1969, the two stood near enough to compare their reds by eye. Calculations show that Mars has not covered the star in the last thousand years and will not in the next.

The system behind my readings doesn't place stars, so nothing on this page comes from my own practice: it is the tradition, set beside the sky. The Scorpion's heart has four other contacts: the [Sun conjunct Antares](/astrology/sun-conjunct-antares/), fixed to the first two days of December; the [Moon conjunct Antares](/astrology/moon-conjunct-antares/), the most frequent; [Venus conjunct Antares](/astrology/venus-conjunct-antares/), rarer even than Mars's; and [Saturn conjunct Antares](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-antares/), which held the degree for months in 1956–57 and 1986. [Conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/) sets out the one-degree rule, and [fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/) where it comes from.
