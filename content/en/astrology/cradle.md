Some patterns pull, and some hold. The cradle does both at once: an opposition stretched across the chart, and beneath it, on one side, two planets that carry it the way a cradle's curve carries its weight.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

Start with an [opposition](/astrology/opposition/), two planets 180° apart. Put two more planets between them on one side, so that the four stand about 60° apart: at 0°, 60°, 120° and 180°. Each planet is then [sextile](/astrology/sextile/) its neighbour along the curve, three sextiles in a row; the planets two steps apart are in [trine](/astrology/trine/), two trines crossing the figure; and the ends face each other in the opposition. Six aspects, four planets, half a hexagon.

Inside it, two smaller figures overlap. The first three planets make a [minor grand trine](/astrology/minor-grand-trine/), two in trine with a third sextile both, and so do the last three. In signs, the four usually share a polarity, because every aspect in the cradle joins signs an even number apart: planets in Aries, Gemini, Leo and Libra make one, all in fire and air, with the Aries–Libra opposition across the top.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The cradle is about as common as the grand trine: roughly 1 person in 10 born from 1930 to 2009 has one among the ten planets, in the count behind this site, its sextiles, trines and opposition each allowed 8° when the Sun or Moon is in them and 6° otherwise. That leaves out the cradles an angle, Chiron, Lilith or a node completes. A cradle needs four planets spaced evenly, so a degree or two of orb either way changes the count a great deal.

The system I built for my readings describes the opposition as polarity and integration, the sextile as cooperation that needs taking up, and the trine as flow. I read the cradle as a tension held from below. The opposition sets two functions face to face; the middle planets give you a way to travel between them in easy steps, so the tension can be visited from several sides instead of swung between two. That makes the two middle planets the most useful in the figure: go-betweens, the functions to reach for when the opposition feels stuck. A pattern is still read through the planets, signs and houses it joins, never on its shape alone.

Its catch is its comfort. Support all round an opposition makes it easy to live with, and so easy never to settle: the cradle can rock for years without going anywhere. The other half of the circle, on the far side of the opposition, holds nothing of the figure, and I look there for what it doesn't supply.

The opposition usually lies across two houses facing each other, and the middle planets in the houses between them: which ones depends on the time of birth. A cradle with the Moon in it can form or dissolve in a day. Where it rocks, and whether it's there at all, are both questions for [the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/), and I wait for it before reading.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Use the steps.** When the opposition feels like a choice you can't make, go through the middle planets: what do they offer each end?
- **Give the opposition a decision.** Comfort is a good base. Use it to decide something the two ends have been circling.
- **Look at the empty half.** What's missing from the figure often lives on the other side of the chart, in planets the cradle doesn't touch.
- **Rest without apology.** A held tension is still a tension. The cradle's support is there to be leaned on.

Its neighbours in this family show its range: [Thor’s hammer](/astrology/thors-hammer/) passes friction on to a single planet, while the cradle shares tension out along a curve. A cradle is a good place to start from, as long as you remember that it was made for a beginning, not for staying in.
