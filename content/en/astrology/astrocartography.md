Draw your birth chart onto a map of the world and you get astrocartography: a web of lines showing where, at the minute you were born, each planet stood on the horizon or on the meridian.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The angles of a chart depend on where you stand: one birth minute gives a different [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) and [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) in every town ([Relocation charts](/astrology/relocation-charts/)). Astrocartography turns that round. For each planet, it finds every place on Earth where, at your birth minute, that planet was rising, setting, culminating at the top of the sky or anti-culminating at the bottom, and draws those places as lines.

Astrologers have tied the sky to places since late antiquity. This way of doing it was made popular in the 1970s and 1980s by the American astrologer [Jim Lewis](/astrology/jim-lewis/), who called it Astro*Carto*Graphy. Other ways exist: [local space astrology](/astrology/local-space-astrology/), developed by the German astrologer Friedrich Sieggrün and later popularised in the United States by Steve Cozzi and Michael Erlewine, maps a chart differently.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

The geometry gives the map its look. A planet is at the top of the sky for everyone on the same meridian at the same instant, so its MC and IC lines run straight from pole to pole. Rising and setting depend on latitude too, so the AC and DC lines curve, crossing the MC and IC lines and bending sharply towards the poles, where some planets never rise or set at all. Your Sun's MC line runs through every place where it was noon by the Sun at the moment you were born.

Given your birth data, a program draws forty lines: four for each of the ten planets, more if it adds Chiron, the nodes or lines of aspect. Some maps also mark [parans](/astrology/parans/), the places where two planets were on angles at once. How far either side of a line still counts is a matter of convention, and astrocartographers don't all draw it alike.

The birth time matters here as much as anywhere. The Earth turns a degree every four minutes, so four minutes of error move every line by a degree of longitude, about 70 km at the latitude of London or Paris. A time known only to the hour can shift the whole web by 15°, more than a thousand kilometres there ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

Astrocartographers read a line as a place where that planet's function comes forward, the way a planet close to an angle stands out in a birth chart ([Angular planets](/astrology/angular-planets/)): a Saturn line for structure and responsibility, a Venus line for what you value and are drawn to, a Mars line for drive and effort. Where two lines cross, the two themes meet.

Read gently, that's a description of emphasis: the planets you carry everywhere, given more room in one place than another. Nothing in your chart changes when you cross a line. The same planets go with you, in the same signs, making the same aspects.

I don't draw astrocartography maps in my readings, and the use that worries me most is advice about where to live. Writing about astrology in 1988, the physicist Shawn Carlson, known for a double-blind test of astrologers ([Carlson’s double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/)), noted that astro-cartographers often counsel clients to move to another part of their country, or even to emigrate. A map of lines is a thin reason to uproot a life.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

A line can't promise anything. A Jupiter line isn't luck and a Venus line isn't love: Jupiter describes growth and meaning, Venus what you value, and neither guarantees an outcome in a city. No line says where you'll stay well, earn more or meet someone, and none should be read that way.

The map also leaves out most of a chart. It shows where each planet would sit on an angle, but not the houses of a relocated chart, the aspects between your planets, or the signs they're in, all of which go with you unchanged.

And it is only as good as the birth time behind it. With a time rounded to the hour, the lines on a map are drawn with a precision the time doesn't have.
