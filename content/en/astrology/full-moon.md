Once a month the Moon comes up as the Sun goes down, round and bright, and stays up all night. It is the most watched moment of the lunar cycle, and the one with the most stories attached to it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A full moon is the moment the Moon is opposite the Sun, 180° away in longitude, so that the whole of the half facing us is lit. Astronomers time it to the minute. In the dates I use for 2026 to 2030 it comes between about 14 and 15½ days after the new moon, and it closes the [waxing gibbous moon](/astrology/waxing-gibbous-moon/) and opens the [waning gibbous (disseminating) moon](/astrology/waning-gibbous-disseminating-moon/).

Astrologers name a full moon for the Moon’s sign, with the Sun in the sign opposite. The full moon of 24 November 2026 is at 2°20′ Gemini, with the Sun in Sagittarius, a [Full moon in Gemini](/astrology/full-moon-in-gemini/); a month later, on 24 December, comes a full moon in Cancer. So while the Sun is in Scorpio the full moon falls in Taurus, and a full moon in Scorpio comes while the Sun is in Taurus.

In Dane Rudhyar’s eight phases, from *The Lunation Cycle* (1967), the full moon is a phase rather than an instant: the Moon 180° to 225° ahead of the Sun, the three or four days after the exact full moon.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The full Moon is up all night: it rises around sunset, is highest around midnight, and sets around sunrise. Between 2026 and 2030 there are twelve or thirteen a year. 2026 and 2028 have thirteen, which is why each of them also has a month with two full moons, the popular kind of [blue moon](/astrology/blue-moon/).

Most months the full Moon passes a little above or below Earth’s shadow, because its path is tilted against the Sun’s. When a full moon falls close to the lunar nodes, the Moon moves into the shadow: a lunar eclipse. Between 2026 and 2030 that happens twelve times, two or three a year, beginning with a total eclipse in Virgo on 3 March 2026 and a partial one in Pisces on 28 August 2026. They have their own page, [Lunar eclipses](/astrology/lunar-eclipses/).

The full moon’s size in the sky depends on its distance, not on its phase. The full moon nearest the Moon’s closest point to Earth looks a little larger, the [supermoon](/astrology/supermoon/), and the one nearest its farthest point a little smaller, the [micromoon](/astrology/micromoon/).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Born at a full moon, you have the Sun and Moon opposite each other: [Sun opposite Moon](/astrology/sun-opposite-moon/). The system I built for my readings reads an opposition as polarity, two functions that become aware of each other by facing each other, never as conflict. So I read the full moon birth as purpose and need across the chart from each other, asking to be balanced rather than settled by choosing a side, and often worked out through the people closest to you. Near the exact full moon, a few hours decide whether you were born just before it or just after ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)).

Against a birth chart, a full moon gets a narrow role in my reading. It counts within a degree of one of your natal points, or of the point opposite, since a full moon lies along an axis, and only when something slower is already active there: a transit, a progression, a solar arc or an eclipse. Then it can light up that season for a moment. On its own it is weather, not a forecast, and the houses it falls across need your birth time.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Full moons make people behave strangely.”** That’s a question for research rather than for a chart, and it has its own page: [Do full moons change behaviour?](/astrology/do-full-moons-change-behaviour/)
- **“The full moon in Scorpio season is a Scorpio full moon.”** While the Sun is in Scorpio, the full moon is in Taurus, opposite. A full moon in Scorpio comes six months later, while the Sun is in Taurus.
- **“The full moon is bigger than the other phases.”** The Moon is the same size at every phase. How large it looks depends on its distance from us, which changes through the month on a cycle of its own.
- **“A full moon is a time to let go, as a rule.”** Many people like to use it that way, and that’s their choice. The sky makes no rules: a full moon describes a moment of the month, never a duty.
