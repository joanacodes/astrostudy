Horary reads a moment that has arrived. Electional astrology picks one that hasn't come yet: a time to marry, to sign, to open the doors, chosen because its chart looks like the beginning you want.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

An election treats a beginning like a birth. The chart for the moment something starts is read as that thing's own chart ([Event charts](/astrology/event-charts/)), so an electional astrologer looks ahead for a moment whose chart describes the venture well begun, and advises starting then.

Its oldest surviving treatise is the fifth book of the *Carmen Astrologicum* of Dorotheus of Sidon, a Greek astrological poem of the first century ([Dorotheus’ Carmen Astrologicum](/astrology/dorotheus-carmen-astrologicum/)). Elections were long used above all to time battles; their users have since applied them to weddings and travel.

Vivian Robson's *Electional Astrology* sorts them into three kinds. Radical elections are chosen from the birth chart of the person they're for. Mundane elections take account of the charts in force for the wider world ([Mundane astrology](/astrology/mundane-astrology/)). Ephemeral elections are chosen from the sky alone, and they're what most people mean by the word: in the historian James Holden's phrase, "an horary chart set for a certain time in the future".

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

The astrologer starts from what's being begun and the window in which it can happen: a wedding that has to fall on a Saturday in June, a shop that opens in the spring. Within that window they cast charts for candidate dates and times, and judge them by the rules of their tradition: the planets and houses that stand for the matter, the condition of the Moon, and moments to avoid, such as a [void-of-course Moon](/astrology/void-of-course-moon/). Some add [planetary hours](/astrology/planetary-hours/), the old scheme that gives each hour of the day to a planet. A radical election then checks the chosen chart against the birth chart, or against both, for a couple.

Popular advice adds rules of thumb, the best known being never to sign anything while [Mercury is retrograde](/astrology/mercury-retrograde/). A rule like that turns a few weeks of the year into a ban, and I don't give advice of that kind.

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

An elected chart describes a beginning: the conditions a thing starts in, as the tradition reads them. That is all it can describe. A marriage, a company or a trip is lived by people, and their own charts, choices and circumstances weigh far more than the minute it started.

I don't choose dates. Electional astrology isn't part of the timing I use, and many of the dates people want chosen sit on my list of refusals: an operation, a court hearing, an investment, a conception ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). A chart can't pick the moment that makes money grow, either ([Can astrology pick investments?](/astrology/can-astrology-pick-investments/)). For a wedding or a launch, choose the day that suits the people involved. If what you'd like to understand is how you meet beginnings yourself, that's a question your birth chart can hold.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

Every elected chart is a compromise. Within a practical window, the moment that satisfies one rule breaks another, and something has to give. The chart that ticks every box often falls at an hour nobody would choose.

A chosen moment also slips. The ceremony starts late; the contract is signed after lunch. Which moment is the beginning: the vows, the signature, the first customer through the door? The answer changes the chart, and the reasoning behind it.

And a date can weigh on people more than it helps them. Postponing something good because a planet is retrograde, or dreading a day because its chart looks hard, gives the calendar a power over a life that it doesn't have. Electional astrology and [horary astrology](/astrology/horary-astrology/) both read moments; a person is more than any of them.
