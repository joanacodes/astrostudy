Before astrology read people, it read kingdoms. Mundane astrology, from the Latin *mundus*, the world, is the branch that looks furthest out: at countries, cities, eras, and the sky that everyone alive shares.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Historians of astrology generally see it as the oldest branch. The astrology of Babylon was concerned almost entirely with the state and its king ([Babylonian astrology](/astrology/babylonian-astrology/)), and when Ptolemy wrote the *Tetrabiblos* in the second century, he set out the astrology of peoples and cities in book II, before any chart of a person, because "the particular always falls under the general" ([Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)).

He tied regions to signs, and wrote that the moment a city was founded could be read like a birth. When that moment wasn't known, the chart of whoever ruled could stand in for it, with its Midheaven given particular weight. Much later, in fifteenth-century England, the astrologer Richard Trewythian cast the chart of the Sun's entry into Aries for some thirty years, and wrote general predictions for twelve of them between 1430 and 1458.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

The tools are moments the whole world shares:

- **Founding charts.** The chart of the moment a country, a city or an institution began, read like a birth chart, when that moment can be agreed on ([Event charts](/astrology/event-charts/)).
- **Ingresses.** The chart for the moment the Sun enters Aries, at the March equinox, or another cardinal sign, cast for a capital and read for the season or the year ([Planetary ingresses](/astrology/planetary-ingresses/)).
- **Eclipses**, which Ptolemy called the first and most potent cause of change, read for the regions where they were seen.
- **Great conjunctions.** Jupiter and Saturn meet about every twenty years: on 21 December 2020 at 0°29′ Aquarius, and next on 31 October 2040 at 17°55′ Libra ([Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/)). Slower pairs meet more rarely: Saturn and Neptune, about every 36 years, last on 20 February 2026 at 0°45′ Aries ([The Saturn–Neptune conjunction of 2026](/astrology/saturn-neptune-conjunction-of-2026/)).

Choosing the moment for a public act by these charts is a branch of its own, the mundane election ([Electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

As I'd read it, mundane astrology describes the sky of a time. Pluto's long stay in Aquarius, Neptune's entry into Aries, the conjunction a whole year's births share: these form the setting in which every personal chart is lived ([Neptune in Aries, 2026–2039](/astrology/neptune-in-aries-2026-2039/)). When I describe them, I describe an era's mood, shared by everyone in it, and leave the personal part to each chart's houses ([Generational placements by birth year](/astrology/generational-placements-by-birth-year/)).

What I don't do is predict. The branch's history is full of forecasts of war, plague and the deaths of rulers; Trewythian, for one, expected the king to fall ill in 1433 because Saturn ruled the tenth house of the year's chart. That's precisely the kind of statement I won't make, about a country, a leader or anyone else ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). A leader's chart is a person's chart, and without their agreement I leave it closed ([Reading public figures](/astrology/reading-public-figures/)).

## Its limits {#its-limits}

A country has no single minute of birth. Is it the declaration, the treaty, the constitution, the first sitting of a parliament? Each gives a different chart, and the choice is often made with the answer already in mind.

The charts it reads are shared by millions of people at once. A great conjunction or an ingress says nothing about any one person living under it; the same sky meets very different lives.

And read after the fact, a mundane chart can be made to fit almost anything that happened, which is the easiest way for any astrology to look right ([Confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/)). A sky described honestly, as a mood, asks less of the facts and promises less in return.
