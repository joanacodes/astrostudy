Marsilio Ficino translated Plato for the Medici, became a priest, and wrote a book on how a scholar might stay well under the stars. Its chief patient was the melancholy scholar; its chief planet, Saturn.

## Who {#who}

He was born in 1433 at Figline Valdarno, in the Republic of Florence. His father, Diotifeci d'Agnolo, was a physician under the patronage of Cosimo de' Medici, and Cosimo took the young man into his household and stayed his patron for life; Ficino in turn tutored Cosimo's grandson Lorenzo, and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola was among his students. When Cosimo decided to refound Plato's Academy in Florence, he chose Ficino to lead it, and in 1462 gave him Greek manuscripts of Plato. Ficino translated the whole corpus into Latin, the first to do so; the draft of the dialogues was finished in 1468 or 1469 and published in 1484. He also put the Hermetic writings into Latin, and his commentary on Plato's *Symposium*, *De amore* (1484), spread the idea of Platonic love through Western Europe.

Trained as a physician, he was ordained a priest in 1473. His astrology brought him trouble: in 1489 he was accused of heresy before Pope Innocent VIII, and acquitted. He died at Careggi on 1 October 1499, and in 1521 a bust of him by Andrea Ferrucci was placed in Florence Cathedral.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He wrote a medicine for thinkers. The *De vita libri tres*, the *Three Books on Life*, written over the 1480s and published in December 1489, is a book of advice, medical and astrological, for scholars who wanted to keep their health and vigour. Its concern is the spirit, which physicians of his day described as a fine vapour of the blood, the instrument the soul uses to feel and to think.

He made Saturn the scholar's planet. In his account, people who live by study are naturally prone to extremes of melancholy, and so to the ambivalent influence of [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/), which the gentler planets, the Sun, Jupiter, Venus and Mercury, can help to balance ([benefics and malefics](/astrology/benefics-and-malefics/)). His remedies are things you can do: wine and fragrant substances, good smells and clean air, and above all music, which he seems to have valued most.

Melancholy, for Ficino, was a matter of the body and its spirit; low mood today is a matter for a doctor or a therapist, and a chart is no substitute ([astrology and mental health](/astrology/astrology-and-mental-health/)). His medical astrology belongs to its history ([medical astrology, in history](/astrology/medical-astrology-in-history/)), and no chart can tell anyone what illness to expect ([Can astrology predict health problems?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-health-problems/)).

He also changed how the claims of astrology were held. The world, he wrote in the book's defence, is itself alive, and only the blind see life in the meanest plants but not in the heavens. Yet in 1494, in *De Sole*, a treatise on the Sun addressed to Piero de' Medici, he described his own astrology as allegorical and anagogic rather than dogmatic.

The heresy charge followed the third book above all, with its instructions for living well in a world of spirits. Ficino's own student, Pico, would turn against predictive astrology in print ([Renaissance astrology](/astrology/renaissance-astrology/)).

Two centuries earlier, Florence had paid [Guido Bonatti](/astrology/guido-bonatti/) for advice on the stars. A century after Ficino, in Denmark, [Tycho Brahe](/astrology/tycho-brahe/) would measure the sky more precisely than anyone before him, and still cast horoscopes for princes.
