*The Astrology of Personality* is the book in which [Dane Rudhyar](/astrology/dane-rudhyar/) set out to restate astrology in the language of the psychology and philosophy of his time. It appeared in New York in 1936, and its subtitle announces the programme: *A Reformulation of Astrological Concepts and Ideals, in Terms of Contemporary Psychology and Philosophy*.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Its publisher was the Lucis Publishing Company, the New York press of the theosophist Alice A. Bailey, who had encouraged Rudhyar's thinking. The book went on to a long life in print: Servire brought it out in the Netherlands in 1963, Doubleday in New York in 1970 as a third edition with a new preface, Aurora Press in 1991, and Kessinger again in 2004.

The 1970 preface traces the book to two things Rudhyar was studying at once: the astrology he learned from the mimeographed lessons of [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/), and the psychology of Carl Jung.

What came out of that meeting is a book about the person rather than the body. Where many works on astrology spoke in terms of physical constitution and the four elements, this one takes a psychological, person-centred view of the chart. The shift sounds small and changes a great deal: read as psychology, a chart asks what a person is working with, not what will befall them. Rudhyar named the approach humanistic astrology, after first calling it harmonic ([Humanistic astrology](/astrology/humanistic-astrology/)), and the book became one of the roots of the astrology that reads a chart through depth psychology ([Psychological astrology](/astrology/psychological-astrology/)).

## What it says {#what-it-says}

It begins by looking back. Early in the book, Rudhyar traces the astrology of his day almost entirely to the work of Ptolemy ([Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)), which is the tradition he sets out to restate.

It changes what a chart is for. In the book, the chart causes nothing. It corresponds to a person by synchronicity rather than by any force the planets exert, and what it shows are psychological tendencies that leave the person free to answer them in their own way. That takes it a long way from [William Lilly’s Christian Astrology (1647)](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/), which judged questions and foretold events.

It takes in the newest planet. Pluto had been found only six years before, in 1930, and the book credits the symbol most astrologers now use for it, an orb above a bident, to Paul Clancy Publications, founded in 1933 ([Pluto](/astrology/pluto/)). Paul Clancy's magazine, *American Astrology*, was where Rudhyar's first astrological articles had appeared. Today I read Pluto beside Mars as a ruler of Scorpio: I read both, the modern ruler and the traditional one ([Traditional and modern rulers](/astrology/traditional-and-modern-rulers/)).

Where I stand with it is simple. A chart describes tendencies, not fate, and you stay in charge of your choices; on that, Rudhyar and I agree. Why a chart should describe anything at all is another matter. Synchronicity is his answer. I don't claim to know, and my own testing, a tick or a cross beside each placement of my chart and of other people's, is a notebook, not a study: it proves nothing about astrology in general ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

Psychology in a reading also has a limit, and I keep to it. A chart can describe a temperament; it can't treat anything. When what someone carries is psychological, the right door is a therapist's, not mine ([Astrology and mental health](/astrology/astrology-and-mental-health/)).

The same decade, astrology reached the general public by a very different road: by 1937 R. H. Naylor was writing a regular column of horoscopes for the twelve signs, *Your Stars*, in the *Sunday Express*. Thirty-two years after Rudhyar's book, that kind of astrology had its first best-seller, built on a single placement: [Linda Goodman’s Sun Signs (1968)](/astrology/linda-goodmans-sun-signs-1968/).
