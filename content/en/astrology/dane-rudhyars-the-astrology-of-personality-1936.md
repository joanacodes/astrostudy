*The Astrology of Personality* is the book in which [Dane Rudhyar](/astrology/dane-rudhyar/) set out to restate astrology in the language of the psychology and philosophy of his time. It appeared in New York in 1936, and its subtitle announces the programme: *A Reformulation of Astrological Concepts and Ideals, in Terms of Contemporary Psychology and Philosophy*. The book is still in copyright, so I describe it here and quote none of it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Its publisher was the Lucis Publishing Company, the New York press of the theosophist Alice A. Bailey, who had encouraged Rudhyar's thinking. The book went on to a long life in print: a Dutch edition at Wassenaar in 1963, a Doubleday edition in New York in 1970 with a new preface, an Aurora Press edition in 1991 and another in 2004.

That 1970 preface tells where the book came from. Rudhyar had learned astrology from the mimeographed lessons of [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) while he was reading the psychological writings of Carl Jung, and he began to think of the two together. He also names Jan Smuts's *Holism and Evolution* among his influences.

What came out of it is a book about the person rather than the body. Where many older works on astrology spoke of the physical constitution and the four elements, this one takes a psychological, person-centred view of the chart. Rudhyar first called his approach harmonic astrology, then humanistic astrology ([Humanistic astrology](/astrology/humanistic-astrology/)), and with his protégé Alexander Ruperti he became one of the first astrologers to bring Jung's psychology into the field ([Psychological astrology](/astrology/psychological-astrology/)).

## What it says {#what-it-says}

It begins by looking back. Early in the book Rudhyar traces the astrology of his own day almost entirely to the second-century Alexandrian [Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/), the tradition his book sets out to restate.

It changes what a chart is for. For Rudhyar the planets don't cause what happens in a life. The chart is a picture, aligned with the person by synchronicity, of psychological forces at work in them, and those forces never take away the person's freedom to respond. That moves the book a long way from [William Lilly’s Christian Astrology (1647)](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/), which judged questions and foretold events.

It takes in the newest planet. Pluto had been found only six years before, in 1930, and the book credits the symbol most astrologers now use for it, an orb above a bident, to Paul Clancy Publications, an astrology publisher founded in 1933 ([Pluto](/astrology/pluto/)). Today I read Pluto beside Mars as a ruler of Scorpio: I read both, the modern ruler and the traditional one ([Traditional and modern rulers](/astrology/traditional-and-modern-rulers/)).

Where I stand with it is simple. A chart describes tendencies, not fate, and you stay in charge of your choices; on that, Rudhyar and I agree. Why a chart should describe anything is another matter. Synchronicity is his answer; I don't claim to know, and my own testing, a tick or a cross beside each placement of my chart and other people's, is a notebook, not a study, and proves nothing about astrology in general ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

Thirty-two years later, astrology about the person reached a far larger public through a book about one placement only: [Linda Goodman’s Sun Signs (1968)](/astrology/linda-goodmans-sun-signs-1968/).
