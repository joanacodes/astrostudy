The word horoscope has changed jobs. It began as the name of one precise point in a birth chart, and it now mostly means a short weekly paragraph addressed to everyone born in the same month. Both are worth understanding, and neither needs sneering at.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

*Horoscope* comes from the Greek *hōroskopos*, from *hōra*, hour, and *skopos*, watcher: the hour-watcher. In Greek astrology it named the rising degree, the point of the zodiac coming up over the eastern horizon at a birth, which we now call the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/). From there it came to mean the whole chart cast for that moment, and for centuries a horoscope was simply a [birth chart](/astrology/birth-chart/).

Today, most people mean something else: the sun-sign column. Twelve short paragraphs, one for each sign of the zodiac, in a newspaper, a magazine or an app, each addressed to everyone born while the Sun was in that sign. Columns like these are younger than they look. The first regular newspaper horoscope appeared in Britain in the 1930s, and the format spread from there.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A column knows one thing about its reader: the sign the Sun was in at their birth, which follows the birth date. Everything else comes from the current sky. The writer looks at where the planets are now and where they're heading, usually from an ephemeris, a table of their daily positions ([Ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)).

A common way of turning that into twelve paragraphs is to treat each Sun sign as if it were the rising sign. For Taurus, Taurus becomes the first house, Gemini the second, and so on round the circle; when Mars moves into Leo, it's read as crossing the fourth house of every Taurus reader, and the paragraph talks about home. It's a neat shortcut, and it gives each sign a week of its own. But the houses are borrowed: your real houses are drawn from your birth time and place, and they rarely line up with the shortcut.

So the difference from reading a chart is one of scale. A column speaks to a twelfth of everyone at once, and has to choose words that will fit most of them, which is why psychologists study how general statements come to feel personal ([The Barnum (Forer) effect](/astrology/barnum-forer-effect/)). A chart reading starts from your own minute and place, with the Moon, the rising sign, every planet's house and the aspects between them: things no column can know. Pairing people by their Sun signs alone runs into the same limit ([Sun-sign compatibility, and why it isn't enough](/astrology/sun-sign-compatibility-and-why-it-isnt-enough/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read your sign, I read your chart, and I'm open about why. In [my notebook](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), the Sun sign held about a third of the time, like the rising sign, Venus and Mercury, the placements everyone reads first. That isn't nothing, but it's close to what a flattering horoscope scores, so I hold the Sun sign lightly and spend the most time where Saturn, Chiron and Lilith sit, the three that held 75 to 90% of the time.

What a reading shares with a good column is the sky in motion. When the planets cross your chart, I read their movements as [transits](/astrology/transits/): weather, a mood to dress for, never a forecast of events. The difference is that I read them against your chart, through your houses, with your birth time. And some subjects stay out of any reading of mine, however the sky looks: health, death, pregnancy, money and legal outcomes ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Horoscopes are all there is to astrology.”** A column is one popular corner of it. A birth chart reading uses the whole sky of one birth.
- **“My daily horoscope was worked out for me.”** It was written for everyone who shares your Sun sign, about one person in twelve.
- **“If my horoscope was wrong, astrology is wrong.”** A column can only speak in general terms, so it's a poor test of a chart, which says far more particular things.
- **“A horoscope tells you what will happen.”** At its best, a column describes the week's weather in broad strokes. Neither a column nor a chart can promise events.
