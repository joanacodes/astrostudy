Twice, astronomers have calculated that a planet should exist beyond Neptune before anyone had seen it. The first, Planet X, sent an observer searching photographic plates and turned up Pluto instead; the second, Planet Nine, is still being looked for. Neither has a measured position, and a planet needs one before it can go into a birth chart.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Planet X was Percival Lowell's planet. After the discovery of [Neptune](/astrology/neptune/) in 1846, small discrepancies seemed to remain in the orbits of the giant planets, [Uranus](/astrology/uranus/) above all. Lowell, a wealthy Bostonian with his own observatory at Flagstaff, Arizona, began searching in 1906 for the body he believed was pulling on them. He called it Planet X, X for unknown, and in 1915 he published his estimate: about seven times the Earth's mass, about 43 times the Earth's distance from the Sun.

Lowell died in 1916 without finding it. In February 1930 Clyde Tombaugh, working at the same observatory, spotted a faint moving point on plates taken that January, about six degrees from one of the places Lowell had suggested. It was named [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), its first two letters Lowell's initials. But Pluto was far too small to move Uranus: once its moon Charon was found, in 1978, it could be weighed, at about a five-hundredth of the Earth. In the early 1990s E. Myles Standish recalculated the orbits with the mass of Neptune measured by Voyager 2 in 1989, and the discrepancies, with them the need for Planet X, vanished.

Planet Nine rests on different evidence. Far beyond Neptune, small icy bodies travel on long, stretched orbits, [Sedna](/astrology/sedna/) among them. In January 2016 Konstantin Batygin and Michael Brown, of the California Institute of Technology, showed that six of the most distant make their closest approach to the Sun on roughly the same side and tilt their orbits alike. A planet of five to ten Earth masses, on a wide, elongated orbit, could herd them that way. Since Pluto was reclassified in 2006, such a planet would be the ninth.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Both predictions follow the method that found Neptune: when known bodies move in a way the known planets can't explain, work out the mass that would explain it and look where it ought to be. In 1846 Johann Galle found Neptune the night after receiving Urbain Le Verrier's prediction. For Planet X the method failed, because the evidence itself was an error.

For Planet Nine the question is open. Searches have covered much of its likely path, and the Subaru Telescope is working on the rest; so far out, it would be faint and slow, since every estimate of its orbit runs to thousands of years. Some astronomers think the clustering may come from where surveys happened to look rather than from a planet, and two bodies found since, 2017 OF201 and 2023 KQ14, have orbits that don't fit it. Unless someone sees it, it stays a hypothesis.

Even its name is provisional. Brown keeps it apart from Planet X, which he calls Lowell's own prediction and unrelated to theirs, and if the planet is ever imaged the International Astronomical Union will name it: not Persephone, the favourite of science fiction, already taken by the asteroid 399 Persephone.

Astrology keeps its own list of unobserved planets, worked out for its own reasons: [Persephone (hypothetical planet)](/astrology/persephone-hypothetical/), [Transpluto (Isis)](/astrology/transpluto-isis/), and the eight hypothetical points of [The Hamburg School (Uranian astrology)](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/). None is the astronomers' Planet X or Planet Nine, and all are hypothetical by their own description.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

A planet earns its place in a chart by having a position: a degree of the zodiac at the moment of birth, read from tables built on observation ([Ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)). Planet Nine has possible orbits and rough regions of sky, but no degree to write beside anyone's name. Whatever degree is offered for it comes from a hypothesis, not from the sky, and I don't read it.

If it is found, it will go round the Sun once in thousands of years and stay in one sign for centuries, so everyone alive would share its sign. What set one chart apart from another would be its house and its contacts with the angles, both of which need the birth time ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Until then, Planet X and Planet Nine are astronomy to me, and a fine story about how planets are found. My notebook covered the Sun, Moon, rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)); a planet nobody has seen was never in it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Planet X is the tenth planet.”** The X stands for an unknown. When Lowell named it, Neptune was the eighth planet and Planet X would have been the ninth.
- **“Pluto is Planet X.”** Pluto turned up in the search, but it is far too small to disturb Uranus, and the discrepancies it was meant to explain didn't exist.
- **“Planet Nine has been found.”** No telescope has seen it. It is still a proposal built on the orbits of other bodies.
- **“Planet X is on its way to hit the Earth.”** That doomsday story began on a website in 1995 and has missed every date set for it. Every planet astronomers have proposed out there stays beyond Neptune all the way round.
