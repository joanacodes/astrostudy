For about a week before the full moon, the Moon is more than half lit, rounding out night after night but not yet round. It rises in the afternoon and lights the evening: the waxing gibbous moon, the phase most people take for full a day or two early.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

In the sky, the waxing gibbous is everything between the [first quarter moon](/astrology/first-quarter-moon/) and the [full moon](/astrology/full-moon/): the Moon 90° to 180° ahead of the Sun, more than half lit and still growing. Gibbous comes from the Latin *gibbus*, a hump, for the bulge on its lit side. Seen from the northern hemisphere the right side is lit; from the southern, the left. It rises between midday and sunset, is high in the evening and sets after midnight, a little later each night.

In Dane Rudhyar’s eight phases, from *The Lunation Cycle* (1967), the gibbous phase is narrower: only the last 45° before full, from 135° to 180°, about three and three-quarter days. The stretch from 90° to 135° belongs to his first quarter phase. So a Moon three days past the first quarter is gibbous to an astronomer and still first quarter to an astrologer who follows Rudhyar.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Two aspects mark the stretch. The trine, at 120°, falls in the sky’s gibbous moon, though Rudhyar counts it in his first quarter phase; the sesquiquadrate, at 135°, is where his gibbous phase begins. The system I built for my readings describes the sesquiquadrate as compound tension that restructures and refines, and that is a fair picture of the phase: the Moon is close to full, and what remains is adjustment.

The light changes quickly now. Each night the Moon stays up longer after dark and the lit part swells, until, for a day or so before the full moon, it looks round to most eyes. The Moon’s sign moves too: with the Sun in Aries, a waxing gibbous Moon runs from about Cancer to Libra, and in Rudhyar’s narrower sense it is usually in Virgo or Libra.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Born under a waxing gibbous moon, your Moon was well ahead of your Sun but not yet opposite it. If it was near 120° ahead, your chart has a [Sun trine Moon](/astrology/sun-trine-moon/): purpose and need keeping the same pace. I read the gibbous birth, in Rudhyar’s narrower sense, as needs adjusting to purpose: nearly there, fine-tuning, inclined to improve on what is already good before letting it be seen. That describes a rhythm, not a promise of perfection or a warning of perfectionism, and the signs and houses of your Sun and Moon give it its colour.

The edges of the phase are where the birth time counts. The Moon gains about 12° a day on the Sun, so near 135° or 180° a few hours decide which phase is yours ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)). [Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/) explains how to find it.

In the sky, I leave these days to the rhythm of the month and look only at the full moon that ends them, under the conditions its page describes. After it comes the [waning gibbous (disseminating) moon](/astrology/waning-gibbous-disseminating-moon/), the same shape turned the other way.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“It looks full, so it is full.”** For a day or so before the full moon, the gibbous Moon passes for round. Astronomers date the full moon to the minute, and until then the Moon is still gibbous.
- **“Gibbous is an astrologers’ word.”** It is astronomy’s, from the Latin for a hump. Astrologers borrowed it, and Rudhyar narrowed it to the last 45° before full.
- **“A gibbous birth makes you a perfectionist.”** No phase hands out a trait. It describes a rhythm of refining, which each chart lives in its own way.
- **“Whatever you’re working on should be finished by the full moon.”** No phase of the Moon is a due date. What needs finishing keeps its own timetable, not the Moon’s.
