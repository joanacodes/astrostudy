They sit side by side on the same shelves, in the same apps, sometimes on the same table. Astrology and tarot are still two different practices, with different histories, and it helps to know which one you're being offered.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Astrology reads a chart: the positions of the Sun, the Moon and the planets calculated for a moment and a place, usually a birth. Anyone who draws your chart from the same details gets the same chart, which is why it needs the exact time ([Birth chart](/astrology/birth-chart/)). Tarot reads cards: a deck of 78, shuffled, drawn and laid out for a question, different every time. One describes a pattern fixed at a moment; the other opens a conversation about now.

On this site I read birth charts, and I don't predict the future from them ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). I'd be equally wary of anyone who claims to predict it from cards. Used honestly, both practices are ways of reflecting, and both deserve the same caution about anyone who sells certainty.

## Why it matters {#why-it-matters}

- **Their histories differ.** Tarot cards were invented in northern Italy in the mid-15th century, for card games, and historians have found no evidence of significant use for divination before the late 18th century. The earliest known divinatory meanings appear in a Bolognese manuscript from around 1750, and occult tarot was popularised in Paris in the 1780s by Antoine Court de Gébelin and Etteilla. The claim that the cards came from ancient Egypt dates from that time and has no historical basis. Astrology is far older, with roots in Mesopotamia ([Babylonian astrology](/astrology/babylonian-astrology/)).
- **They met through occultism.** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn taught astrological correspondences for the cards. A. E. Waite, one of its members, followed them in the deck that Pamela Colman Smith illustrated and William Rider & Son first published in 1909: he swapped two trumps so that Strength matched [Leo](/astrology/leo/) and Justice matched [Libra](/astrology/libra/). That's a link drawn between the two practices, not a single practice.
- **They ask different things of you.** A chart needs your date, time and place of birth, and describes tendencies that come back across a life ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). A spread needs a question, and speaks to the moment it's asked. Knowing which you're getting tells you what it can reasonably be asked.
- **The same lines apply to both.** A reader who frightens you and then sells the cure, keeps a meter running, or reads someone behind their back is doing the same thing with cards as with stars ([Fake psychics](/astrology/fake-psychics/)).

## What to do instead {#what-to-do-instead}

- **Choose by your question.** If you want to understand a pattern that keeps coming back in your life, a birth chart read in full can hold that. If you want someone to make a decision for you, neither cards nor stars should.
- **Ask any reader what they won't read.** Health, death, pregnancy, court cases and investments are where a reading can do harm, whatever the tool. A careful reader tells you their limits and their price before you start ([How to tell a careful astrologer from a scam](/astrology/how-to-tell-a-careful-astrologer-from-a-scam/)).
- **Don't read people who aren't there.** A chart or a spread about someone who doesn't know is a reading behind their back ([why I won’t read a synastry behind someone’s back](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)).
- **Test what you're told.** Write it down, date it, and come back later with a pen, the way I tested my own chart ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

If your hesitation is about faith rather than accuracy, that's a different question, with a page of its own ([Is astrology a sin?](/astrology/is-astrology-a-sin/)).
