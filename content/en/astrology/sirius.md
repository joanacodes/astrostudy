Sirius is the brightest star of the night sky, the Dog Star of Canis Major, whose return to the dawn sky once warned Egypt that the Nile was about to flood. In the zodiac it sits at 14°26′ Cancer in 2026; in the sky it lies nearly forty degrees south of the Sun's path.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Sirius is α Canis Majoris, magnitude −1.46, almost twice as bright as Canopus, the next brightest star. Part of that is closeness: at 8.6 light-years it is one of the Sun's nearest neighbours, with a white dwarf, Sirius B, circling it every fifty years. In 1717 Edmond Halley compared its place with the one recorded in Ptolemy's *Almagest* and found it had moved about half a degree on its own, as had Aldebaran and Arcturus: the "fixed" stars drift too, if far more slowly than precession moves them along the zodiac.

[Cancer](/astrology/cancer/) will hold it for centuries more: it stood at 13°06′ for a 1930 birth and 14°04′ for one in 2000, and has reached 14°26′ in 2026. Along the zodiac it follows [Capella](/astrology/capella/), the goat star at 22° Gemini, and precedes [Procyon](/astrology/procyon/), the little dog's star, which shares its sign at 26° Cancer. Its latitude, 39.6° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), means that when the Sun reaches its degree in early July, the star is lost in the daylight forty degrees away.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Its Greek name, Seirios, means glowing or scorching. Egypt worshipped it as the goddess Sopdet, and its first dawn rising, which kept almost exactly to a year of 365¼ days, came just before the Nile's yearly flood. The Greeks blamed its summer rising for the heat that wilted plants, and the season after it became the dog days; the Romans called it Canicula, the little dog. In Persia it was Tishtrya, the bringer of rain, and Chaucer's *Treatise on the Astrolabe* calls it Alhabor and draws it as a hound's head.

Of the fifteen Behenian stars of medieval magic, Sirius is the only one given a single planet, Venus, and Cornelius Agrippa listed beryl and juniper as its stone and plant. For astrologers who would rather follow the real sky than the zodiac, Sirius is the usual example of a paran: a star rising while a planet culminates, Sirius coming up over the horizon as Jupiter crosses the Midheaven, say ([parans](/astrology/parans/)).

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The brightest star of the night shares its degree with the Sun on about 5 or 6 July, a [Sun in Cancer](/astrology/sun-in-cancer/) in mid-sign, and shares nothing more physical: the star is forty degrees away. Any planet near 14° Cancer is in the same position, linked to Sirius by a line drawn from the zodiac, never by closeness in the sky ([fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)).

Bright as it is, Sirius has never appeared in a reading of mine: I read planets, angles and houses, and a degree of Cancer forty degrees from the star is a thin thread to hang a meaning on ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). What it offers instead is a calendar and a story, the Dog Star whose rising marked the flood and the hottest days of the year, and those I'm glad to pass on as they are.
