Below the sign, the tradition drew a finer map. It cut every sign into five strips of unequal width and gave each strip to a planet, so two planets in the same sign, at 3° and at 9° Aries, can stand on ground held by different rulers. Those strips are the terms, also called bounds.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Each sign holds five terms, one for each of the five planets from Mercury to Saturn: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. The Sun and Moon get none. The widths vary, and so does the order, which changes from sign to sign. In the Egyptian table, the oldest in common use, the first 6° of Aries belong to Jupiter; [Vettius Valens](/astrology/vettius-valens/) describes them as "temperate, robust, abundant in seed, benefic" (*Anthology*).

Dignity by term comes fourth among the five essential dignities, after domicile, exaltation and the [triplicity rulers](/astrology/triplicity-rulers/), and a planet has it in any term it rules. [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/) scored it 2 points in *Christian Astrology* (1647), but the ancients didn't rank the dignities that way. [Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/) went as far as saying that a planet in its own terms is "just as if located in its own sign" (*Mathesis*).

Terms appear on papyrus horoscopes from the middle of the first century. Despite the name, the Egyptian terms seem to have come from Babylonia: Alexander Jones and John Steele identified them in Babylonian tablets (*ISAW Papers 1*, 2011).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Two tables matter. The Egyptian terms were the standard for Valens, Firmicus and Paulus Alexandrinus. [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/) knew they were the most widely accepted, and he complained that they kept no consistency "either of the order or of the individual quantity". He preferred another arrangement, which he said he had found in "an ancient manuscript, much damaged" (*Tetrabiblos*, book I). That second table spread in the Middle Ages, and Lilly used it. The two agree in places and part company in others, so a planet can be in its own term by one table and not by the other.

To read a term, you look at whose strip a planet occupies and how that planet is placed. Rhetorius of Egypt gave the classic rule: a planet in the sign of a benefic and the term of a benefic was thought to do well for the native's fortune; in a benefic's sign but a malefic's term, less so; in a malefic's sign and term, it "hurts and darkens his luck" (*Compendium*). Benefic and malefic are the tradition's words, and I don't read them as luck ([Benefics and malefics](/astrology/benefics-and-malefics/)).

Terms also served elsewhere in the old techniques: in reception between planets, in primary directions, and in methods for estimating the length of a life. That last use is one I never touch. No chart can tell anyone how long they will live ([Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

If the sign is the country a planet stands in, its term is the street. The sign's ruler describes the manner the function works in; the term's ruler adds a second voice for that particular stretch. Take Saturn at 4° Aries. Aries is its fall, an uncomfortable sign for a function that builds slowly, but by the Egyptian table those degrees belong to Jupiter. An astrologer working with terms would read Saturn there as a slow builder given some room to grow.

Terms are fine-grained, and that makes them hungry for precision. The Ascendant moves through the zodiac at about a degree every four minutes, so its term can change within minutes of a birth ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). A time rounded to the quarter hour can put it in another planet's stretch.

Terms are not the [faces](/astrology/faces/): faces cut each sign into three equal parts and run through all seven planets in a fixed order. The two are often confused because both divide a sign into smaller pieces.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Terms and decans are the same thing.”** Terms are five unequal parts ruled by five planets; decans, or faces, are three equal parts ruled by seven.
- **“There is one table of terms.”** There are at least two in wide use, the Egyptian and Ptolemy's, and they differ.
- **“Terms are a medieval invention.”** They appear on first-century papyri, and their roots seem to be Babylonian.
- **“The Sun and Moon have terms of their own.”** They have none: the terms belong only to the five planets.
