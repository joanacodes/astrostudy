Celebrity charts are everywhere: a singer's Venus, a politician's Mars, a royal birth time passed around like a rumour. You won't find one on this site, and that's deliberate.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

I don't read the charts of real people who haven't asked me to. Not celebrities, not politicians, not royals, not someone who went viral this week, and not historical figures either: the dead can't say yes, and their birth time is rarely known. You won't find a famous person used as an example anywhere here.

It's the same principle that keeps children's charts out of my readings ([Children’s charts](/astrology/childrens-charts/)) and keeps synastry for people who know it's happening ([why I won’t read a synastry behind someone’s back](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)): a chart belongs to the person whose birth it records, and they should have a say in who reads it.

## Why it matters {#why-it-matters}

- **A chart goes deep.** The three placements that held best in my notebook, Saturn, Chiron and Lilith, describe what people usually keep to themselves ([The three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/)). A stranger's chart read in public puts exactly those on show.
- **Fame isn't consent.** A public role makes someone's work and words public. It doesn't hand over their inner life, their fears or their family to anyone with an ephemeris.
- **The data is shaky.** Famous birth times come from birth certificates, but also from memoirs, interviews, rumour and other astrologers' guesses. Lois Rodden, who spent decades collecting the birth data of public figures, applied a rating system to what she gathered and asked astrologers to give the source of their data, and to put it on every chart.
- **Hindsight fits everything.** Once you know a life, its chart seems to describe it: the scandal, the comeback, the breakdown. That's the easiest way for astrology to look right, and it proves nothing ([Confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/)).
- **People in the news are still people.** A chart drawn the week someone dies, falls ill or is accused of something adds a stranger's guess to a family's worst week.
- **It slides into what I won't read.** Public charts are read for illness, death, divorce and court cases more than for anything else, and those are subjects I don't read for anyone ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

None of this is about being precious. A chart read about someone, rather than with them, turns a person into a specimen, and the people around them, partners, children, parents, into supporting cast.

## What to do instead {#what-to-do-instead}

- **Learn on your own chart.** Go through it placement by placement, with a tick or a cross beside each line, the way I tested mine ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). You're the one person whose inner life you can check it against.
- **Practise with people who say yes.** Friends and family who agree, told what you'll do with what you find, and free to tell you where you're wrong. That's how my notebook was built: family, friends and strangers online who agreed, and who marked each line themselves.
- **For history, read the history.** The pages on astrologers of the past in this encyclopaedia describe what they wrote and changed, never their charts.
- **For the times you live in,** the sky everyone shares is a subject of its own: the slow planets' long stays, the great conjunctions, the mood of an era ([Mundane astrology](/astrology/mundane-astrology/)). It describes a period, not a person.
- **If someone you know intrigues you, ask them.** People usually enjoy being asked about their chart, and the conversation that follows will teach you more than anything you'd have read about them alone. Whatever they share is theirs: birth details are personal data, theirs as much as yours ([Your birth details are personal data](/astrology/your-birth-details-are-personal-data/)).
