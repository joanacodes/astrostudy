Before railways and telegraphs, every town set its clocks by its own Sun, and noon in Bristol came about ten minutes after noon in London. A birth recorded in those years was recorded in that local time, and a chart has to know it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Local mean time is the time of the average Sun on a place's own meridian. *Mean*, because the real Sun keeps uneven hours: a sundial can run up to about sixteen minutes fast, in early November, or fourteen slow, in mid-February, and clocks averaged that out once they were accurate enough to notice. Sundial time, the older standard, held on in national almanacs into the early nineteenth century; the British Nautical Almanac changed to mean time only in 1834.

The Earth turns 15° an hour, so local mean time changes by four minutes for every degree of longitude, and towns a degree apart kept clocks four minutes apart. Paris, about 2°20′ east of Greenwich, ran 9 minutes 21 seconds ahead of it; Dublin, 25 minutes 21 seconds behind.

Standard time replaced it country by country ([time zones and daylight saving in charts](/astrology/time-zones-and-daylight-saving-in-charts/)). The railways of Great Britain went over to London time in the 1840s, and Greenwich time became the law in 1880. France kept town time until 1891, then Paris time for the whole country until 1911. Some countries used one city's mean time as their national time well into the twentieth century: Ireland kept Dublin's until 1916, the Netherlands Amsterdam's until 1940, Liberia Monrovia's until 7 January 1972.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

For a birth before standard time, the chart needs the offset of the place where the time was recorded, not of today's zone. The arithmetic is simple: multiply the longitude by four minutes, then take the result away from the recorded time for a place east of Greenwich, or add it for a place to the west, and you have Universal Time. From there the chart is built like any other, through [sidereal time](/astrology/sidereal-time/). Atlases of time changes, such as Thomas Shanks's *International Atlas*, record when each place gave up its local time, and chart software works from records of the same kind; check that the offset it shows matches the town's longitude.

The difference matters. The [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) moves about a degree every four minutes on average, so the ten minutes between Bristol and London move it by about two and a half degrees, Dublin's twenty-five by about six, Monrovia's three-quarters of an hour by about eleven. Near the edge of a sign, any of those can change the rising sign, and all of them move the [house cusps](/astrology/house-cusps/).

On an old record, three questions are worth asking:

- **Which time was it written in?** The town's own mean time, a national mean time like Paris's or Dublin's, or an early zone time.
- **Had the town changed yet?** Railways often switched years before the law did, and some towns kept two times at once: Bristol's Exchange clock was given a second minute hand for London time.
- **Could it be sundial time?** Before mean time became the norm, a time read from the Sun could be a quarter of an hour away from the mean.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

By about 1900, almost every inhabited place had a standard time, so local mean time touches few living people's charts: mostly births where one city's mean time served as the standard into the twentieth century, as in the Netherlands until 1940 or Liberia until 1972, and the charts of earlier generations. When an older relative agrees to have their chart read, this is where the work starts ([Charts for friends, colleagues and family](/astrology/charts-for-friends-colleagues-and-family/)).

The rule doesn't change with the century: the time as written, the place, and the clock it was written by, which family papers can sometimes settle ([How to find your birth time](/astrology/how-to-find-your-birth-time/)). If no one knows the hour, there's no chart I can read, and I won't guess one, any more than for a birth last year ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Local mean time is sundial time.”** Sundial time follows the real Sun and wanders up to about a quarter of an hour either way through the year; mean time is its average, the time a good clock keeps.
- **“Before time zones, nobody knew the exact time.”** Towns kept careful mean time. What they didn't share was the same time as the next town.
- **“Once a country had standard time, every record used it.”** Railways, telegraphs and town halls didn't always change together, and Bristol's clocks showed two times for years.
- **“GMT has always meant the same thing.”** Until 1925 astronomers counted the Greenwich day from noon, so in an older almanac, 0 hours on a date means noon of that civil day ([Ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)).
