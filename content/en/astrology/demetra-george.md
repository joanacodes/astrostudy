Demetra George wrote, with Douglas Bloch, the book after which astrologers' use of the asteroids spread: *Asteroid Goddesses*, in 1986. She has also looked back on how that change began, and written *Finding Our Way Through the Dark*, which astrologers cite on the balsamic moon.

## Who {#who}

She is an astrologer and author. *Asteroid Goddesses: The Mythology, Psychology and Astrology of the Re-Emerging Feminine*, written with Douglas Bloch, came out in 1986. Her *Finding Our Way Through the Dark* was published in a second edition by the American Federation of Astrologers, in Tempe, Arizona, in 2008. In the winter of 2006–2007, twenty years on, she looked back on her first subject in an article for *The Beltane Papers*, “Revisiting the Asteroid Goddesses”. The United Astrology Congress has given her its Regulus Award for Theory and Understanding, a prize it describes as recognising work that uses astrology with other disciplines and so encourages its acceptance as a discipline in its own right.

The story behind it is a long one. The first asteroids were found at the start of the nineteenth century, Ceres on 1 January 1801, then Pallas in 1802, Juno in 1804 and Vesta in 1807, and each was counted a planet before astronomers set the asteroids apart. Astrology left them aside for more than a century and a half. In George's account its use of them began in 1973, when Eleanor Bach published the first asteroid ephemeris, the positions of Ceres, Pallas, Juno and Vesta from 1900 to 2000. Their old astronomical symbols came back with them: the sceptre of Juno, long replaced in astronomy by a numbered disc, was resurrected for astrological use that same year ([The origin of the glyphs](/astrology/origin-of-the-glyphs/)).

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

She put the asteroids into astrologers' charts. An ephemeris made them possible to place; *Asteroid Goddesses* gave astrologers a reason to, and their use spread after it. Each of the four now has a page on this site: [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/), [Pallas](/astrology/pallas/), [Juno](/astrology/juno/) and [Vesta](/astrology/vesta/).

She read them as goddesses. The subtitle names the angle, the re-emerging feminine, and the names did the rest: Ceres, the largest, holding about a third of the asteroid belt's mass, named for the Roman goddess of agriculture; Pallas, bound up with Athena; Juno, the Roman Hera, goddess of marriage; Vesta, the virgin goddess of home and hearth. Myth and psychology together turned four small bodies of the asteroid belt into figures a chart could hold, much as the planets are read through their gods.

I describe them rather than read them. The charts I prepare leave the asteroids out, and they weren't in the notebook where I put a tick or a cross beside each placement, so I can't tell you how often they held ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). Anyone who does read them will find a few rules for keeping them in proportion here: [How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/).

She has also written on the balsamic moon: *Finding Our Way Through the Dark* is a source on it, the last days of the Moon's cycle before the new moon ([Balsamic moon](/astrology/balsamic-moon/)). I read the phase a person is born in lightly, as one theme beside the rest of the chart ([Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/)).

[Robert Hand](/astrology/robert-hand/) spent the following decade on the oldest texts; her book had turned to bodies the old authors never knew. Her Regulus Award has a sister prize for discovery, innovation and research, which the United Astrology Congress gave in 1992 to [Jim Lewis](/astrology/jim-lewis/), the astrologer who drew birth charts onto maps of the world.
