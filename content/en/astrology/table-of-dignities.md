Seven planets, twelve signs, five ways of being at home and two ways of being out of place: the tradition fitted all of it into one grid. Read along a row and you see which planets are comfortable in a sign; read down a column and you see the whole range of one planet.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Each row of the table is a sign. Its columns give the sign's ruler, the planet exalted there and the degree of exaltation, the [triplicity rulers](/astrology/triplicity-rulers/) by day and by night, the five terms with their degrees, the three faces, and then the planets in detriment and in fall. [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/) printed the version most people know in *Christian Astrology* (1647). The same table, give or take the terms and the triplicities, can be found in Guido Bonatti, in Vettius Valens and in the *Tetrabiblos* of [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/).

Lilly also put numbers on it: 5 points for [domicile](/astrology/domicile/), 4 for exaltation, 3 for triplicity, 2 for a term and 1 for a face. That scoring belongs to the later tradition. Ancient authors don't appear to have ranked the dignities at all, and [Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/) held that a planet in its own terms was as strong as one in its own sign.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Take the Aries row. Mars rules the sign. The Sun is exalted there, at its 19th degree. The fire triplicity belongs to the Sun by day and Jupiter by night, with Saturn sharing in the oldest scheme. In the Egyptian [terms](/astrology/terms-bounds/), the first 6° belong to Jupiter. The faces run Mars, the Sun, Venus. Venus is in detriment, opposite its own Libra, and Saturn is in fall, opposite its exaltation in Libra. So a Sun in Aries in a day chart holds two dignities at once, exaltation and triplicity, and between 10° and 20° it adds its own face. It never holds a term: the luminaries have none.

Read down a column instead, and one planet's whole range appears. Venus rules Taurus and Libra, is exalted in Pisces, rules earth and water by day in Dorotheus's scheme, holds the faces of Aries, Cancer, Virgo, Scorpio and Aquarius, and is weak in Aries, Scorpio and Virgo.

A planet that turns up in no column of its row, at its degree, is [peregrine](/astrology/peregrine-planets/): it holds no dignity there at all. Totting up the points also answers another old question, which planet has the most say over a given degree, and that question has its own page, the [almuten](/astrology/almuten/). How the points add up, and where scoring goes wrong, is the subject of [dignity scores](/astrology/dignity-scores/).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Many modern astrologers use only the first column, the rulers of the signs, and I can see why: the finer columns change from one table to another, and a score can look more exact than it is. I read the rulers in every chart, both of them for Scorpio, Aquarius and Pisces. The rest of the table I treat as the tradition's commentary on fit: how comfortably a function sits in a given manner.

That word, fit, is the one I hold on to. No column can make a planet good or bad, because in my readings a sign only ever colours the way a function operates. A high score doesn't promise an easy life, and a low one doesn't sentence anyone to a hard one; the houses, the aspects and the person decide far more ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

The outer planets show the table's age. Uranus, Neptune and Pluto have no exaltation, triplicity, term or face in it, only the domiciles modern tropical astrology later gave them, and with those, their detriments.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“There is one correct table.”** The terms and the triplicity rulers differ between authors, so every table is someone's table.
- **“A planet's score is its strength in your life.”** It measures fit with a sign at a degree. Houses, aspects and the rest of the chart weigh at least as much.
- **“The points are as old as astrology.”** The ranking is medieval and later. Ancient authors treated the dignities as more nearly equal.
- **“The outer planets have their place in the table.”** They arrived long after it was drawn, and only their modern domiciles were added.
