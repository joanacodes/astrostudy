Claudius Ptolemy wrote the astronomy that Europe, the Middle East and North Africa followed for more than a thousand years, and then a second book on what the movements of the sky were thought to do on earth. That second book, the Tetrabiblos, is why he has a page here. The man behind both is hard to see.

## Who {#who}

Almost everything about his life is inferred from his name and his books. He wrote in Greek and lived in or around Alexandria, in the Roman province of Egypt; the Roman name Claudius suggests he was a Roman citizen. He was born around the year 100, place unknown. A fourteenth-century astronomer, Theodore Meliteniotes, put his birth at Ptolemais Hermiou, a Greek city in the south of Egypt, but the claim arrives more than a thousand years late with nothing to support it. He died in Alexandria in a year no ancient source records: modern estimates run from about 165 to about 175.

He wrote about a dozen treatises, on astronomy, geography, music, optics and the stars' effects. The *Almagest*, his astronomy, could not have been finished before about 145. The *[Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)*, “four books”, says in its opening lines that it comes after the astronomical work, which places it in the later part of his life. Its original title is unknown.

Even his authorship was once in doubt. In ninth-century Baghdad, [Abu Ma’shar](/astrology/abu-mashar/) took him for a member of Egypt's royal family, recorded a belief that another of that line had written the astrology book under his name, and concluded: “The correct answer is not known.” Modern scholars judge that account mistaken, and no longer doubt that the author of the *Almagest* wrote the *Tetrabiblos* as its companion.

The doubt now runs the other way. The *Centiloquium*, a hundred aphorisms often bound after the *Tetrabiblos* in medieval manuscripts, was read as his for centuries. The Italian scholar Cardano judged it spurious in the 1550s, and one of its aphorisms rests on a doctrine of Jupiter–Saturn conjunctions that Arabic astrologers developed long after him.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

Not the techniques. The historian Jim Tester notes that Ptolemy originated none of the methods he describes. What he brought was order: he arranged what he had inherited so that it rested on consistent principles, and explained it through the physics of his time. He was also selective. He set aside the numerology of names, and left out the choosing of auspicious moments and medical astrology, judging them without a sound basis. Two of those omissions are mine too: I don't choose dates ([electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/)), and a medical question belongs with a doctor ([medical astrology, in history](/astrology/medical-astrology-in-history/)).

The frame of the zodiac is his best-known legacy, and a disputed one. In the *Tetrabiblos* he writes that “it is reasonable to reckon the beginnings of the signs also from the equinoxes and solstices”, because the signs take their natures from the turning of the seasons. Some historians credit him with that zodiac, or with breaking from the practice of his day. Others point out that Geminos had already explained it in the first century BC, and that Ptolemy says he is following earlier writers. Either way, it is the frame I work in. Every chart I read is drawn in the [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/), tied to the seasons rather than to the stars, which is why its signs have slowly drifted away from the constellations that share their names ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)).

He also worried about the birth time. In his third book he insists on the exact degree of the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) at birth, admits how hard it is to note the local time precisely enough, and offers a way to correct it from the new or full Moon before the birth ([prenatal lunation](/astrology/prenatal-lunation/)). His worry is mine; his remedy isn't. I don't correct a time by technique ([rectification](/astrology/rectification/)): there's always a birth time, or no reading, and [what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) says why.

His order didn't convince everyone. The historian of science E. J. Dijksterhuis found it puzzling that the author of the *Almagest* could put together “such a system of superficial analogies and unfounded assertions”. Lynn Thorndike noticed the opposite problem in astrology's critics: they seemed the only readers unaware of the book, attacking positions it didn't hold or had already answered.

In the same century, [Vettius Valens](/astrology/vettius-valens/) was filling his *Anthology* with the working methods of older astrologers, the kind of material Ptolemy pruned. The world both men wrote in is [Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/).
