Haumea turns once every 3.9 hours, so fast that it has pulled itself out of round, and two teams on two continents still claim to have found it. Its name, given in 2008, belongs to the Hawaiian goddess of childbirth, chosen for an island, a rock and a family of fragments.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Haumea is one of the five bodies most often listed as dwarf planets, beside [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), [Eris](/astrology/eris/), [Makemake](/astrology/makemake/) and [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/), and it lies in the Kuiper belt, beyond [Neptune](/astrology/neptune/). Its spin has stretched it into an elongated ellipsoid: when it passed in front of a star in 2017, it proved about as long as Pluto is wide, and roughly half that from pole to pole. Its surface is crystalline water ice, as bright as snow. [Varuna](/astrology/varuna/), another fast spinner, is drawn out the same way.

Astronomers think a giant collision, at least a billion years ago, set Haumea spinning and scattered pieces of it that still travel on similar orbits, the first such family found beyond Neptune. Its two moons, Hiʻiaka and the smaller Namaka, are thought to be fragments too, and the 2017 occultation also revealed a ring.

The orbit takes 284 to 285 years and is tilted 28° to the Sun's path. Haumea never comes closer than 35 times the Earth's distance from the Sun; it was at its farthest in early 1992 and will be nearest in 2133.

Mike Brown, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz found it on 28 December 2004, in images taken at Palomar that May, and nicknamed it Santa. On 27 July 2005, José Luis Ortiz Moreno's team at the Sierra Nevada Observatory in Spain announced it first, from images of March 2003. Brown came to suspect that the Spanish team had used his team's observation logs; Ortiz admitted looking at them and denied any wrongdoing. When the IAU named the body in 2008, it credited no discoverer, gave the Spanish observatory as the place of discovery, and took the Caltech team's name.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

In Hawaiian tradition, Haumea is the goddess of fertility and childbirth, and her worship is among the oldest on the islands. She is the mother of many deities, Pele the volcano goddess among them, and apart from Pele her children were born from different parts of her body. With a magic stick, the Mākālei, she turns from an old woman into a young girl, again and again. In one story she visits Muleiula, a chief's daughter in a painful labour, and finds that people then gave birth only by cutting the mother open; she makes a potion from the kani-ka-wī tree, and the child is born naturally.

David Rabinowitz proposed the name, and it fitted three ways. Haumea is the goddess of the island of Hawaiʻi, where the moons were found from the telescopes on Mauna Kea. She is identified with Papa, the earth, and in 2006 the body was thought to be almost solid rock. And her children, born from her body, matched the fragments of the collision, so the moons took the names of two daughters: Hiʻiaka, born from her mouth, and Namaka, a water spirit born from her body. The Spanish team had proposed Ataecina, an Iberian goddess of spring, but as an underworld deity she suited bodies in step with Neptune, as Pluto is, and Haumea is not one.

Its symbol, by Denis Moskowitz, simplifies two Hawaiian petroglyphs, for woman and for childbirth, and is used mostly by astrologers. Seen from Earth, Haumea crawls: a sign holds it for decades, so its sign is something a whole age group shares ([Generational placements by birth year](/astrology/generational-placements-by-birth-year/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Astrology has known Haumea for about twenty years, and everything said about it rests on the name. Taken from the goddess, a reading would speak of what one body brings into being, and of beginning again, old turning young. I offer that only as a proposal: no tradition stands behind it, and there has been no time to test it.

One reading I won't give is the one its myth seems to invite. A goddess of childbirth in your chart says nothing about whether or when you will have a child; no placement does, and I don't read charts for that question ([Can astrology predict pregnancy?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-pregnancy/)).

Nor does Haumea come into my own readings. The system I built to prepare them doesn't place it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)), and my notebook never weighed it: the ticks and crosses went to the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, the planets, Chiron and Lilith ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Every dwarf planet is round.”** Haumea's spin has stretched it out of round, and much faster it would pull itself into a dumbbell and split in two.
- **“Rings belong to the giant planets.”** Haumea's, found in 2017, was the first seen around a body beyond Neptune; [Quaoar](/astrology/quaoar/) has since turned out to have two.
- **“Haumea is a ball of rock.”** That was the view in 2006, and part of why it was named after the earth goddess. The 2017 occultation gave a density like that of other large bodies out there, and a 2019 model puts a rocky core under an icy mantle up to 170 km thick.
