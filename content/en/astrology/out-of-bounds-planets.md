The Sun never stands more than 23°26′ north or south of the celestial equator: that's how far it gets at the solstices. Some planets go further. Astrologers call them out of bounds, beyond the range the Sun keeps.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Out of bounds is a matter of [declination](/astrology/declination/), not of the zodiac. A planet's sign says where it is along the Sun's path; its declination says how far north or south of the [celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/) it stands. The Sun's declination swings between about 23°26′ north at the June solstice and 23°26′ south at the December solstice, the tilt of the Earth's axis. Any planet whose declination passes either figure is out of bounds until it comes back inside.

Nothing else about the planet changes. An out-of-bounds Venus in Gemini is still in Gemini, still in its house, still making its aspects. Being out of bounds adds a note to it, the way being retrograde does, without moving it anywhere ([natal retrograde planets](/blog/retrograde-planets/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The zodiac's path, the ecliptic, reaches its furthest north at the start of Cancer and its furthest south at the start of Capricorn. The Sun never leaves the ecliptic, so it can go no further. The planets travel a little above or below it, and a planet near the start of Cancer or Capricorn, on the far side of the ecliptic, passes the Sun's limit.

The [Moon](/astrology/moon/) does it most often, and in waves. Its orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic, and over 18.6 years that tilt alternately adds to the Earth's and takes away from it. At the major lunar standstill of January 2025 the Moon reached 28.7°; around the minor one, in 2034, its monthly extremes stay under 19°. In 2026 it leaves bounds 27 times, twice a month, reaching 28.4°; from 2031 to 2037 it doesn't leave them once.

Mercury and Venus never stray far from the Sun, so they go out of bounds around the solstices, when the Sun is at its own limit. From 2026 to 2030, Mercury does so eleven times, for about ten days to a month each time, and Venus eight times, furthest from 2 April to 30 May 2028, up to 27.8° north. Mars does so six times in the same years, for up to seven weeks.

Further out, it's rare and slight. Jupiter passes the line by a few hundredths of a degree, for a few weeks in some years. Uranus passes it by up to 0.3° for a few years roughly every four decades, as in 1987–1991 and again in 2031–2035. [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), about 4° south of the ecliptic in early Aquarius, has been out of bounds for a few months each year since 2025, as it will be until 2035, for the first time since 1953. Saturn and Neptune don't leave bounds at all between 1900 and 2100.

In births from 1930 to 2009, by The Astro Study's count, the Moon was out of bounds about one time in six, Mars one time in six, Mercury and Venus each about one time in eight. Nearly half of those births had at least one of the four out of bounds.

Declination also has its own aspects, the [parallel](/astrology/parallel/) and the [contra-parallel](/astrology/contra-parallel/). A planet far out of bounds can't make either with the Sun, which never reaches that far; it can only pair with another body near or beyond the line.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read an out-of-bounds planet through its sign, house and aspects first, like any other. What astrologers who use the idea agree on is distance from the norm. The planet goes further than the Sun's range, so it tends to act further from what's expected, for better or worse. An out-of-bounds Moon can show feelings that don't follow the family script; Mercury, a mind that takes its own route; Venus, tastes and loves outside the usual; Mars, a drive that's hard to pace.

I hold this more lightly than a placement. My notebook checked each planet by sign and house; declination wasn't part of it, so I have no hit rate to give you for this ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). When a planet is out of bounds, I say so as a nuance, and I never build a reading on it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Out-of-bounds planets are rare.”** Nearly half of births from 1930 to 2009 had the Moon, Mercury, Venus or Mars out of bounds.
- **“An out-of-bounds planet has left its sign.”** It keeps its sign, house and aspects. Out of bounds is measured from the celestial equator, not along the zodiac.
- **“The Sun can be out of bounds too.”** The Sun's own greatest declination is the bound.
