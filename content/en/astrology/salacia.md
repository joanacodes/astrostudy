Salacia is the darkest of the large bodies known beyond Neptune, and with its moon it makes only the third pair out there found to have locked face to face, as Pluto and Charon have. Its name keeps it in the family of the planet it lies beyond: the Romans made Neptune's wife the goddess of salt water.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Salacia is about 838 km across, slightly smaller than [Orcus](/astrology/orcus/), and nearly round: its brightness barely changes as it turns. Its dark grey surface reflects about one part in twenty-five of the sunlight that reaches it, the lowest share of any known large body beyond [Neptune](/astrology/neptune/), and a surface that dark suggests nothing has renewed it in a very long time. In 2023 the James Webb telescope found water ice and carbon dioxide ice there, but none of the methane that larger worlds keep.

It goes round the Sun in 274 years, between 37.7 and 46.7 times the Earth's distance, on an orbit tilted 23.9° to the Sun's path and in no rhythm with Neptune. That orbit resembles [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/)'s, except that it is turned almost the opposite way, and Salacia is now close to its most northerly point above the Sun's path.

Its moon, Actaea, about 393 km across, nearly half Salacia's width, was found with Hubble on 21 July 2006 by Keith Noll, Harold Levison, Denise Stephens and William Grundy. It orbits every 5.49 days, and Salacia turns in exactly that time: the two are tidally locked to each other, each always showing the other the same face. Only Pluto and Charon, and Eris and Dysnomia, were known to do this before. From 2067 the pair will begin a rare season in which, seen from Earth, each passes in front of the other.

Henry Roe, Michael Brown and Kristina Barkume found it on 22 September 2004 with the Hale telescope at Palomar, and it was announced in May 2005 in a batch of seven new bodies beyond Neptune; older images go back to 1982.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Salacia was the Roman goddess of salt water, the wife of Neptune, identified with the Greek Amphitrite and with Tethys. Her name comes from sāl, salt, and stood for the wide, open sea. Her marriage to Neptune is implied by Varro and stated outright by Seneca, Augustine and Servius, and she bore him three children, the best known being Triton, half man and half fish. Roman priests invoked her in a formula that paired gods with their powers, Salacia Neptuni, which one historian of early Rome renders “effervescence of Neptune”. Apuleius pictures her “weighed down with her lapful of fish”, and she is shown crowned with seaweed, beside Neptune in a pearl-shell chariot drawn by dolphins or sea-horses.

The body was numbered 120347 in November 2005 and named on 18 February 2011. Its moon took the name of Actaea, one of the fifty Nereids, “the dweller on coasts”, to keep the pair at sea. For its symbol, Denis Moskowitz put forward a stylised hippocamp, the sea-horse that draws her chariot; it is not widely used, which says much about Salacia's place in astrology.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Salacia is the kind of name that tempts a reading on sight: a sea goddess, a wife, a moon that never turns its face away. I'd keep those images for what they are. A proposal drawn from the myth would be about the open sea, and the companion who stands beside a ruler rather than the ruler himself; nothing has tested it, and two bodies locked face to face describe nobody's marriage. Comparing two people's charts, with both their consent, is how I look at a relationship ([Synastry and consent](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)).

Neptune itself is in every chart I read. Salacia isn't: my readings are prepared without it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)), and my tick-by-tick notebook passed it by ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). Like [Gonggong](/astrology/gonggong/), another water name, it came to astrologers with a name given this century and no tradition behind it.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Salacia is one of Haumea's fragments.”** Its orbit runs near those of [Haumea](/astrology/haumea/)'s collision family by coincidence; tracking and its spectrum show it is unrelated, despite the water ice on its surface.
- **“Salacia spins in a few hours.”** Studies in 2014 put its day at about six hours. A study of sixteen years of data, published in 2025, showed that gaps in the observations had produced that figure, and that Salacia turns once every 5.49 days, in step with Actaea.
- **“A sea goddess must be a stormy one.”** Salacia stood for the calm, sunlit face of the sea.
