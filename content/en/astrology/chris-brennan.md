Chris Brennan explains the oldest horoscopic astrology to the astrologers practising now: in a book, *Hellenistic Astrology: The Study of Fate and Fortune* (2017), in papers on the lots and the houses, and in conversation, as host of *The Astrology Podcast*.

## Who {#who}

He is an astrologer and author. His paper “The Theoretical Rationale Underlying the Seven Hermetic Lots” appeared in the *Tradition Journal* in spring 2009, and “The Planetary Joys and the Origins of the Significations of the Houses and Triplicities” in 2013. *Hellenistic Astrology: The Study of Fate and Fortune* came out from Amor Fati Publications in 2017.

He also hosts *The Astrology Podcast*, where astrologers and scholars talk through the subject's techniques and history. Its guests have included Benjamin Dykes, on mundane astrology, and Martin Gansten, in 2020, on Tajika, the Indian system that reads a year of life from the chart cast for the solar return, the moment the Sun comes back to the longitude it held at birth ([Solar return](/astrology/solar-return/)).

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He wrote the Hellenistic tradition up for working astrologers. That tradition took shape in and around the Mediterranean, especially in Egypt, from around the late second or early first century BCE ([Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/)). His book treats it whole, and its research travels: encyclopedia articles cite it for the dating of Teucer of Babylon, an astrologer of uncertain date whom [Vettius Valens](/astrology/vettius-valens/) and Rhetorius used, and for Valens's own birth, on 8 February 120. On Teucer it is one of the sources for a debate: David Pingree placed him in or before the first century AD, and Wolfgang Hübner has argued that some of his material was already used by the Roman poet Manilius and by [Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/). His book came after three decades of [the traditional revival](/astrology/traditional-revival/), which had put the Greek and Latin texts back into English.

He traced meanings back to their sources. His paper on the joys connects the old names of the houses with the planets said to rejoice in them: the Moon in the 3rd, the house called the Goddess; the Sun in the 9th, the God; Jupiter in the 11th, the Good Spirit ([Planetary joys](/astrology/planetary-joys/)). The pattern carries the tradition's moral map of the chart: the benefics rejoice in the houses of Good Fortune and Good Spirit, the malefics, the tradition's word, in Bad Fortune and Bad Spirit, the 6th and the 12th. Those are old names, not forecasts; I read the 6th and 12th in an adult's life and never for illness or confinement ([Can astrology predict health problems?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-health-problems/)).

He explained a set of lots. His 2009 paper deals with the seven Hermetic lots, one for each traditional planet, which Paulus Alexandrinus recorded in the fourth century: Fortune for the Moon and Spirit for the Sun, the others counted from those two ([The Hermetic lots](/astrology/hermetic-lots/)). Of the seven, my readings use two, the Lot of Fortune and the Lot of Spirit, and both rest on the birth time, since every lot is counted from the Ascendant.

His podcast has become a source in its own right. Episodes of *The Astrology Podcast* are cited by encyclopedia articles, on the Persian astrologers who read history in the cycle of [Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/), and on Tajika, whose very name, the episode explains, means a Persian. Half of my own method, the traditional techniques, comes out of that history, beside the modern tropical astrology of the other half ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

Where [Bernadette Brady](/astrology/bernadette-brady/) brought old star lore into modern charts, Brennan wrote about a whole system. [Geoffrey Dean](/astrology/geoffrey-dean/) asked the question a history doesn't: whether any of it works.
