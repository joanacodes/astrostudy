The hundredth asteroid was found on 11 July 1868, and its name did two jobs at once. Hekate honours the Greek goddess of crossroads and magic, and it echoes *hekaton*, the Greek for a hundred. Her reputation for witchcraft makes this asteroid an easy tool for fear, which is the last thing a chart should be.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Canadian-American astronomer J. C. Watson found it, his fourth asteroid; ten years earlier, [Pandora](/astrology/pandora/) had been the fifty-fifth. Hekate is a stony asteroid about 87 km across, turning once in about 27 hours, and it circles the Sun every 5.4 years or so, a little under half the time [Jupiter](/astrology/jupiter/) takes. That near two-to-one rhythm puts it in the Hecuba group, asteroids that orbit close to that resonance with Jupiter. On 14 July 2003, observers in New Zealand watched it pass in front of a star.

It also has awkward neighbours. Hekate travels in the same part of the belt as the family of asteroids broken from [Hygiea](/astrology/hygiea/), but it isn't one of them: it's too bright and of the wrong kind for that dark, carbon-rich family, an interloper that only shares the road.

The goddess is old. In Hesiod's *Theogony*, from around 700 BC, she is a goddess of great honour, with a share of the sky, the earth and the sea. Later she became the goddess of crossroads, of night and of magic, shown with a pair of torches, a key, snakes or dogs, and later still in three forms; the Romans called her Trivia, from the three roads where her images stood. In Athens she watched over the household and its doors, and once a lunar month, at the dark of the moon, a meal was set out for her.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Those who place Hekate in a chart borrow her attributes: crossroads and choices, thresholds and keys, the torch carried into the dark. Her best-known moment is a kind one. In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, she helps Demeter look for her daughter Persephone, taken down to the underworld; in the tradition that follows, she becomes Persephone's companion on her yearly journeys there and back. Persephone has an asteroid too, number 399, found in 1895 ([Persephone (asteroid 399)](/astrology/persephone-asteroid/)), and Demeter's Roman double is [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/).

Like every body, Hekate needs the birth time for its house, and its sign is shared by everyone born within the same stretch of its slow circuit.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Hekate stays out of my readings, as every asteroid does, and out of my notebook too: the placements I [ticked or crossed](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/) never included it.

Because of her reputation, one thing needs saying plainly. No asteroid shows a curse, a spell or a fate, and no one can lift a curse for money: whoever offers to, for a fee, is running a scam ([curse removal scams](/astrology/curse-removal-scams/)). "Curses to lift" are on my list of what I won't read ([how I read](/how-i-read/)). Hecate herself protected doorways and travellers as well as presiding over magic, and a placement named after her describes nothing more frightening than a tendency. If a reading of Hekate elsewhere has left you afraid, take that fear to someone you trust, not to another reader.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Hekate in your chart means witchcraft.”** A placement makes no one a witch, or the victim of one.
- **“A Hekate contact means you've been cursed.”** No chart can show a curse, and anyone selling a cure is selling a scam.
- **“Hekate belongs to the Hygiea family.”** It orbits among them but differs in brightness and make-up: a neighbour, not a relative.
- **“Hecate was only a dark goddess.”** In the oldest sources she's honoured across sky, earth and sea, and in the Hymn she helps a grieving mother.
