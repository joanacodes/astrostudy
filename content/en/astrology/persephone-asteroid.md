Three things in astrology answer to Persephone's name. One is a small asteroid numbered 399; one is her Roman twin, the asteroid [Proserpina (26)](/astrology/proserpina-26/); the third is a planet once proposed beyond [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), which has a page of its own. This one is about the asteroid.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Asteroid 399 Persephone is about 49 km across, a small body in the outer part of the main belt between Mars and Jupiter. Max Wolf found it at Heidelberg on 23 February 1895. Its orbit is nearly round and tilted about 13° to the Sun's path, and one circuit takes about 5.3 years, at an average of three times the Earth's distance from the Sun.

The belt holds the goddess twice. Her Latin name had already gone to 26 Proserpina, found by Robert Luther in 1853, so Persephone arrived forty-two years after her own Roman double.

The third Persephone isn't a body anyone has observed. Planets that existed only in calculation were given names too: Vulcan for one inside Mercury's orbit, and Persephone, among several others, for one beyond Pluto ([Persephone (hypothetical planet)](/astrology/persephone-hypothetical/)). A chart program may list either under the same word.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The name comes with one of the best-known stories in Greek myth. Persephone was the daughter of Demeter, goddess of grain, whom the Romans called [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/). In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, she is gathering flowers in a meadow when Hades bursts through a cleft in the earth and carries her down to be his queen. Her mother searches the whole earth with torches borrowed from [Hekate](/astrology/hekate/), and while she grieves, nothing grows. Zeus orders the girl's return, but Hades has given her pomegranate seeds, and whoever eats the food of the dead has to go back. In the hymn she spends a third of every year below; in Ovid and Hyginus, writing later, it becomes half. On an Athenian vase of about 440 BC, it is Hekate, a torch in each hand, who leads her up to her mother.

The Greeks told it to explain the seasons: the months she spends above are the growing ones, the months below are the bare ones. She was also queen of the dead, and her name was handled with care: in cult she was often simply Kore, the Maiden.

An asteroid found in 1895 has no centuries of commentary behind it. Placed in a chart, it keeps a plainer rhythm than its goddess, about five and a third years round the zodiac rather than a season below and a season above, so many people born around the same time as you share its sign; its house waits on the birth time ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)), and only a contact within a degree or two of a planet or an angle would make it stand out. What it adds to all that is the story, and a story isn't evidence.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Persephone isn't one of the bodies I read. The system I built for my readings computes the planets, Chiron, Lilith and the lunar nodes; no asteroid has a place in it. In the myth Persephone spends part of every year out of sight; in my notebook she was out of sight the whole time. The [notebook where I tested charts](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/) took the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith in turn, and about a third of those placements held overall; there's no figure for her, not even a rough one.

The myth itself gives me a second reason. It's the story of a young woman taken without her consent and of a mother who loses her for part of every year, and it would be easy to turn it into a warning about someone you love. No placement can say that anyone will be taken from you, and I don't read a chart for what will happen in a family ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

If you meet her on a printout, first check which Persephone the program means, the asteroid or the hypothetical planet. A wheel that shows Persephone and Proserpina side by side is telling one story twice, which is exactly how a chart drowns ([How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Persephone and Proserpina are the same asteroid.”** Same goddess, two bodies: 26 Proserpina was found in 1853, 399 Persephone in 1895, and they follow different orbits.
- **“The asteroid Persephone is the planet beyond Pluto.”** That Persephone is hypothetical and has never been seen. Asteroid 399 is a small rock in the main belt.
- **“She spent half of every year below.”** In the oldest full telling, the Homeric Hymn, it's a third; half comes from later poets.
- **“Persephone is only a victim.”** She was also queen of the underworld, honoured with her mother in the Eleusinian Mysteries, which promised initiates a happy afterlife.
