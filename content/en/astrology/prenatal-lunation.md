Every birth has a new or full moon just behind it, a few days back and never more than about two weeks. Astrologers call it the prenatal lunation, and Ptolemy was already working from it in the second century.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Twice a month the Sun, the Earth and the Moon fall into line: at the [new moon](/astrology/new-moon/), with the Moon between us and the Sun, and at the [full moon](/astrology/full-moon/), with the Earth in the middle. The old word for that lining up is syzygy, and the last syzygy before your birth is your prenatal lunation. Some astrologers keep the name for the last new moon alone, which can lie up to a month back, so it's worth checking which one a writer means.

Which of the two came last depends on the Moon's phase when you were born. If the Moon was waxing, between new and full, the last lunation was a new moon; if it was waning, between full and new, it was a full moon. [Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/) tells you which half of the cycle you arrived in, and so which kind of lunation stands behind your chart.

The prenatal lunation is a fixed point. Its degree was set by the lunation itself, days before you arrived, and it is the same for everyone born between that lunation and the next: what differs from one chart to another is the house it falls in and the planets it lands near. Like a planet at [0° of a sign](/astrology/0-of-a-sign/), which has only just crossed into its sign, it belongs to the moments before the birth rather than to the birth. It isn't the [progressed new moon](/astrology/progressed-new-moon/) either, which comes round during a life; the prenatal lunation never moves.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

New and full moons take turns about every fifteen days. Between 1900 and 2100 the gap from one to the next runs from about 13.9 to 15.6 days, so a prenatal lunation in Ptolemy's sense is never more than about 15½ days old at birth. Counting new moons only, it can be up to about 30 days old.

Finding yours takes a list of lunations and your date of birth: take the last new or full moon before it. When a lunation falls on the birthday itself, the birth time decides whether it came before you or after. The house it occupies in your chart needs the time as well.

Its oldest job was a practical one. Ptolemy wanted the degree of the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) exact, knew how rarely a recorded time was good enough to give it, and in the third book of the *Tetrabiblos* he settles it from the new or full moon before the birth ([Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/)). Medieval and Renaissance astrologers took the method up as a standard way of correcting birth times, under the name animodar ([rectification](/astrology/rectification/)).

When the last lunation before a birth was also an eclipse, it does double duty, as the [prenatal eclipse](/astrology/prenatal-eclipse/) too.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The prenatal lunation isn't part of my readings. Its oldest use was to correct a birth time, and I don't correct times by technique: there's always a birth time, or no reading, and the time comes from a record, a certificate or a hospital file, not from the Moon ([how to find your birth time](/astrology/how-to-find-your-birth-time/)).

The lunar cycle still has its place in how I read. The phase you were born in, the angle between your Sun and your Moon, says which half of the month you arrived in, waxing or waning, and that's the part of the story I take from the Moon's rhythm. The lunation before it adds a degree you share with everyone born that fortnight.

If you meet the prenatal lunation in someone else's reading, two questions help. Which definition, new and full moons or new moons only? And which house, since the degree itself is shared? Those answers make it yours, as far as it can be. I'd rather spend a reading on the placements that held best in my tests than on a degree shared by a fortnight of births ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The prenatal lunation is your Moon sign.”** Your Moon is where the Moon stood at your birth. The lunation is a separate point, days earlier, often in another sign.
- **“It's always a new moon.”** In Ptolemy's sense it's the last new or full moon, whichever came later; some astrologers use the new moon only.
- **“It can stand in for a birth time.”** Its oldest use was to correct a recorded time, and I don't use it even for that. A chart needs the time itself.
- **“It's different for everyone born the same week.”** Everyone born between two lunations shares the same one.
