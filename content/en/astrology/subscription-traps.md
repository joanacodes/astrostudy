Most subscription traps don't look like traps when you walk in. They look like a free horoscope.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

A subscription trap is a sign-up built to keep charging after you meant to stop. In astrology and psychic services it tends to run the same way: a free birth chart, compatibility report or reading; a request for your card "to see the rest"; a trial at a small price that becomes, a few days later, a weekly or monthly payment; and a way to cancel that's hard to find. You can stop it, and being caught by one says nothing about your judgement.

Here, ordering a reading takes two steps, your birth details and then the payment, and a [birth chart reading](/birth-chart/) arrives within a week by email, in a private online space and as a PDF. The price is on the page before you start.

## Why it matters {#why-it-matters}

- **Small sums hide well.** A weekly charge for a "premium horoscope" rarely looks like much on its own. Over a year it can cost more than a reading, and it often runs for months before anyone checks a statement.
- **The free part is the hook.** Offering a free trial that turns into a paid subscription unless you cancel is an established way of selling online services, well beyond astrology. What's particular to this field is what you hand over first: your birth date, time and place, and sometimes someone else's ([Your birth details are personal data](/astrology/your-birth-details-are-personal-data/)).
- **The reader sets the clock.** On a chat or a phone line billed by the minute, the person answering decides how long the answer takes. The same business that sells a frightening "vision" often sells the minutes to hear the rest ([Fake psychics](/astrology/fake-psychics/)).
- **Leaving is made harder than joining.** Cancelling buried in settings, a subscription that survives deleting the app, emails that keep coming. Each step is small, and that's the point.

Nothing in a birth chart needs a subscription. A chart doesn't change from week to week, and a daily message written for everyone born in your month is a [horoscope](/astrology/horoscope/) column, whatever it costs. Whether a person wrote it at all is a fair question to ask ([Are horoscopes written by AI?](/astrology/are-horoscopes-written-by-ai/)).

## What to do instead {#what-to-do-instead}

- **Before you sign up,** find three things: the price, the date it renews, and the way out. If you can't find all three before typing your card number, don't start.
- **If you're already caught,** cancel through the route you signed up by: in the app, on the website, or in your app store's subscriptions page if you paid through the store. Keep the confirmation and check the next statement.
- **Call your bank or card issuer** about payments you didn't intend, and ask what it can do about the next ones.
- **Complain to a consumer body.** In the UK, Citizens Advice runs a consumer helpline. In the European Union, a European Consumer Centre in each member state, and in Iceland and Norway, helps with disputes involving a trader based in another of those countries. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission takes reports from consumers, about false advertising and fraud among other things. The protection you have depends on where you live: a federal rule meant to make cancelling as easy as signing up was struck down by a US appeals court in July 2025.
- **If you were charged for something you never agreed to,** treat it as possible fraud: [How to report a scam](/astrology/how-to-report-a-scam/).
- **Ask for your data to be deleted** once you've left, birth details included.

And before you pay anyone in this field, for anything, it helps to know what a careful practice looks like: the price stated up front, the limits stated too ([How to tell a careful astrologer from a scam](/astrology/how-to-tell-a-careful-astrologer-from-a-scam/)).
