Most aspect patterns are drawn with the aspects Ptolemy described. The grand quintile is drawn with Kepler's: five planets, each a fifth of the circle from the next, the outline of a five-pointed star.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

The name follows the other grand figures, which space planets evenly round the whole circle with one aspect. The [grand trine](/astrology/grand-trine/) does it with three planets a third apart, the [grand cross](/astrology/grand-cross/) with four a quarter apart, the [grand sextile](/astrology/grand-sextile/) with six a sixth apart. Five planets a fifth apart make the grand quintile.

The geometry then decides everything. Each planet sits 72° from its two neighbours, a [quintile](/astrology/quintile/), and 144° from the two planets across the figure, a [biquintile](/astrology/biquintile/). Five planets make ten pairs, and here every pair is one or the other: five quintiles round the edge, drawing a pentagon, and five biquintiles across it, drawing a star. No major aspect appears anywhere, since no multiple of 72° short of a full turn lands on 60°, 90°, 120° or 180°.

The signs give no shortcut. Seventy-two degrees is two signs and twelve degrees, so the corners fall two or three signs apart and never in one element, unlike the grand trine's. Spotting a grand quintile means measuring degrees, not counting signs.

The planets have limits of their own. Mercury is never more than about 28° from the Sun and Venus never more than about 47°, so the Sun can't hold a corner with either of them at another. Mercury and Venus can be 72° apart only near their greatest distances on opposite sides of the Sun, and then the Sun sits between them, off the figure. So at least three of the corners belong to the Moon or to Mars and the planets beyond. In a [harmonic chart](/astrology/harmonic-charts/) of the fifth harmonic, where every position is multiplied by five, all five corners gather on or near the same degree.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The count behind this site tests each chart from 1930 to 2009 against the figures listed in its notes, from the grand trine to the stellium, and the grand quintile isn't among them. So I have no share to give you. What I can say is how much the figure asks: five of the ten planets, each within a degree or two of its point, at the same moment. Those who use the quintile keep it that tight, and every extra degree of [orb](/astrology/orbs/) lets a program flag many more charts. If yours shows a grand quintile, check how wide each of its ten aspects is allowed to be before you give it weight.

In the system I built for my readings, the quintile relates two functions through creative organisation and pattern-making, and the biquintile through a more complex synthesis; neither is talent, genius or mastery handed over. A grand quintile repeats that relation ten times across five planets. I'd read it as a capacity running through a large part of the chart for arranging things until they make sense, whether materials, ideas, sounds or people. The same system's rule for patterns applies here too: a figure describes emphasis, never a fixed identity, and no single factor rules a chart. I read the five planets first, each in its sign, and the figure as the way they can work together.

Its five houses need the birth time, and so does a Moon at a corner, which moves about 13° a day and stays within a degree or two of its point for only a few hours. That's one reason I [wait for the time before reading anything](/blog/unknown-birth-time/), and why [how I read](/how-i-read/) starts with the planets rather than the drawing.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Measure before you believe.** Note the orb of each of the ten aspects. A figure held together by wide orbs is a sketch, not a structure.
- **Name the five.** Write down what each planet does in your chart. The figure only means something once its corners do.
- **Find the one thing they make.** Five functions that can organise each other tend to suit a craft with many parts: a garden, a score, a system, a shop.
- **Practise rather than wait.** The quintile family describes potential. It grows with repetition, not with recognition.

Two neighbouring figures show what happens when a pattern's name outruns its rule: the [envelope](/astrology/envelope/), which no author defines the same way, and the [hard rectangle](/astrology/hard-rectangle/), whose sides the name leaves open. The grand quintile, at least, has its geometry fixed by its name, and five planets to read before any of it.
