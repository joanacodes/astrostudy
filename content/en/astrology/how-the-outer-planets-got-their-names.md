Mercury to Saturn were named in antiquity, after gods who were already in the sky. The three planets found with telescopes had to be named by letter, vote and argument, and each name was somebody's choice.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

Uranus came first and took longest. William Herschel saw it on 13 March 1781 from the garden of his house in Bath, and reported it as a comet; by 1783 it was accepted as a planet. Asked by the Astronomer Royal, Nevil Maskelyne, to give it a name, Herschel chose *Georgium Sidus*, George's Star, after his patron King George III, explaining to Joseph Banks that the old names belonged to “the fabulous ages of ancient times”. Others proposed Herschel, Neptune, Astraea, Cybele, Minerva, even Hypercronius. In a paper read in Berlin on 12 March 1782, Johann Elert Bode proposed Uranus, the Latinised Greek god of the sky, arguing that the name should follow the mythology of the older planets: as Saturn was the father of Jupiter, the new planet should take the name of Saturn's father. In 1789 Martin Klaproth named his new element uranium in support. The name became universal only in 1850, when HM Nautical Almanac Office, the last holdout, dropped Georgium Sidus.

Neptune was named after a quarrel. Johann Gottfried Galle found it at the Berlin Observatory on the night of 23–24 September 1846, less than a degree from where Urbain Le Verrier had calculated it would be. Galle proposed Janus; in England, James Challis proposed Oceanus. Le Verrier claimed the right to name it, put forward Neptune while falsely stating that the Bureau des Longitudes had approved it, and in October tried to call it Le Verrier instead, backed by the observatory director François Arago. The idea met stiff resistance outside France, and French almanacs went back to calling Uranus Herschel and the new planet Leverrier. On 29 December 1846 Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve spoke for Neptune at the Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences, and in August 1847 the Bureau des Longitudes adopted the name, Arago keeping out of the decision.

Pluto was named by a vote. Percival Lowell began searching for a ‘Planet X’ in 1906; after his death the search resumed, and on 18 February 1930 Clyde Tombaugh found a moving object on photographic plates taken that January at Lowell Observatory. The news went out on 13 March, and more than a thousand suggestions followed. Minerva, the staff's first choice, already belonged to an asteroid, and Cronus had been pushed by an unpopular astronomer; Pluto, the Roman god of the underworld, won a unanimous vote and was published on 1 May 1930. The first person among the letter-writers to propose it is credited as Venetia Burney, an eleven-year-old in Oxford interested in classical mythology. She suggested it to her grandfather, Falconer Madan, at breakfast; he passed it to the astronomer Herbert Hall Turner, who cabled Lowell Observatory on 16 March. Whether she was really the first has been doubted, and a French astronomer had suggested Pluto for Planet X in 1919, though nothing shows the Lowell staff knew. The name fitted: Pluto was a son of Saturn, whose other children already had planets or asteroids, and its first two letters were Percival Lowell's initials. Pluto was classed a dwarf planet in 2006, and kept its name.

The myths travelled further than the Latin. In Chinese, Japanese and Korean, Uranus is the ‘heavenly king star’ and Neptune the ‘sea king star’, and Pluto, after a Japanese proposal, is the star of the king of the underworld.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

Three new planets, and three new rulers. Astrologers gave Uranus to [Aquarius](/astrology/aquarius/), Neptune to [Pisces](/astrology/pisces/) and Pluto to [Scorpio](/astrology/scorpio/) as their modern rulers. Before that, Aquarius belonged to Saturn, Pisces to Jupiter and Scorpio to Mars, and astrologers who work traditionally still read them that way. I read both rulers for each of the three signs ([Traditional and modern rulers](/astrology/traditional-and-modern-rulers/)).

Astrology took the names as they came. It reads [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), named for the god of the underworld, as depth, endings and renewal, [Neptune](/astrology/neptune/), the sea god, as dreams, imagination and fog, and [Uranus](/astrology/uranus/), the sky, as change, freedom and surprise. A name chosen to honour a king, to complete a family tree or to win a vote is still only a label, and I don't read a planet's meaning from its name. The discoverers left their marks on the symbols too: the H on Uranus's glyph is Herschel's initial, and the PL on Pluto's is Lowell's ([The origin of the glyphs](/astrology/origin-of-the-glyphs/)).
