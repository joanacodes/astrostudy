Every ordinary chart is drawn from where you stood: the Earth, at the minute and the place of your birth. A heliocentric chart moves the observer to the centre of the Sun and redraws the same moment from there.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The word comes from the Greek *helios*, the Sun. Most astrology is geocentric: it places each planet where it appeared from the Earth. A heliocentric chart places each one where it would appear from the Sun, which means, astronomically, where it really is in its orbit.

Three things follow. The Earth becomes a planet in the chart, standing exactly opposite the Sun's position in your ordinary chart: a Sun at 10° Aries, seen from Earth, means an Earth at 10° Libra, seen from the Sun ([Earth in heliocentric charts](/astrology/earth-in-heliocentric-charts/)). Nothing is retrograde, because retrograde motion is an effect of watching from a moving Earth ([Retrograde, explained](/astrology/retrograde-explained/)). And there are no houses, Ascendant or Midheaven: those need a horizon and a meridian, and there is no place on the Sun to stand.

The idea is as old as the Sun-centred model of the planets itself. The first astrologer known to have considered applying Copernicus's model was Andreas Aurifaber, in his *Practica* for 1541. In the early 1650s, the English astrologer Joshua Childrey published an ephemeris of the planets' places and aspects "as they respect the Sun as centre of their orbs", and was associated with others, Vincent Wing, John Gadbury and John Goad among them, who wanted to reform astrology to fit the new natural philosophy. In 1899, Holmes Whittier Merton's *Heliocentric Astrology* gave the approach a book of its own.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

A program does it: choose heliocentric positions and every planet moves to its place as seen from the Sun. How far it moves depends on its distance. Over the years 2000 to 2040, by The Astro Study's count:

- **Mercury and Venus** can land anywhere. From Earth they never stray far from the Sun, Mercury about 28° at most and Venus about 47°; from the Sun, they run all the way round the zodiac, Mercury in 88 days and [Venus](/astrology/venus/) in about 225.
- **Mars** can shift by up to about 47°, **Jupiter** about 12°, **Saturn** about 6°.
- **Uranus, Neptune and Pluto** barely move, 3° at most for Uranus and about 2° for the other two.
- **The Moon** stays within about a sixth of a degree of the Earth, so it effectively disappears into the Earth's position.

Because the chart has no angles, it hardly depends on the birth time, unlike everything I read ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)): everyone born on the same day shares almost the same heliocentric chart, except for [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/), which covers between about 3° and 6° of its orbit each day.

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

By its supporters' account, mainly aspects and configurations between the planets, read as a supplement to the ordinary chart. No astrologer uses it on its own, and its advocates recommend it as a second view.

It is a fair picture of the Solar System, and a poor picture of a person's sky. The view from the Sun is the astronomer's; the view from the Earth is the one you were born under, with the Sun rising or setting, the Moon in its phase, and the houses that place each planet in a part of life. I read the chart from there ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Its limits {#its-limits}

With no houses and no angles, a heliocentric chart can't say where in a life anything falls, and because it changes so little within a day, it can't tell apart people born hours apart. The [Moon](/astrology/moon/), one of the most personal bodies in an ordinary chart, all but vanishes. Other alternative frames make different trade-offs: the [draconic chart](/astrology/draconic-chart/) keeps the houses and moves the zodiac, and [harmonic charts](/astrology/harmonic-charts/) keep the zodiac and multiply the angles.
