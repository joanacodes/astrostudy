When the Greek books reached Persia and then Baghdad, astrology gained translators, a court to serve and some formidable critics. Persian and Arabic astrology is the bridge between Ptolemy and medieval Europe, and it added more than it carried.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

It began before Islam. In Sasanian Iran, Greek works on astronomy and astrology were translated into Middle Persian alongside material from India, and the learning that grew from them mixed Greek, Mesopotamian, Iranian and Indian ideas. Under the Abbasid caliphs, from the eighth century, astrology became a discipline of its own, written in Arabic.

Its most famous act was a foundation. In 762 the caliph al-Mansur founded Baghdad, and a group of astrologers led by Naubakht chose the moment, among them [Masha’allah](/astrology/mashaallah/), a Persian Jewish astrologer who lived from about 740 to 815. From the middle of the eighth century to the end of the tenth, a very large number of secular Greek books were put into Arabic; Ptolemy's *Tetrabiblos*, translated in the ninth century, became in the historian George Saliba's words “by far the most influential source of medieval Islamic astrology”. Historians disagree about the House of Wisdom usually credited with this work: Dimitri Gutas argues that the name may mean no more than a storehouse of books, or stand for Baghdad's wider circle of scholars, rather than an academy.

The great teacher was [Abu Ma’shar](/astrology/abu-mashar/), who lived from 787 to 886 and came from Balkh. The historian David Pingree judged him no great innovator, but his practical manuals for training astrologers shaped the Islamic world and, through translation, Byzantium and western Europe; the *Great Introduction* dates from around 848 ([Abu Ma’shar’s introductions](/astrology/abu-mashars-introductions/)). Another writer in Arabic, Sahl ibn Bishr, left books that later shaped Guido Bonatti in the thirteenth century and William Lilly in the seventeenth.

Astrologers worked in four branches: nativities, the most common; elections, choosing the moment to begin something ([electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/)); questions, judged from the chart of the moment they were asked ([horary astrology](/astrology/horary-astrology/)); and the general astrology of nations.

It also met serious objections. Al-Farabi, Ibn al-Haytham, Avicenna, [al-Biruni](/astrology/al-biruni/) and Averroes rejected parts of it, mainly its judicial branch: they found its methods conjectural rather than empirical, and at odds with religious teaching. Avicenna accepted that the planets act on the world but denied that anyone could know their effects precisely. Al-Biruni, court astrologer at Ghazni, wrote a textbook of astrology in Persian and Arabic, the *Book of Instruction in the Elements of the Art of Astrology*, and criticised the forecasting its last chapter teaches.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

Timing techniques, first. The [firdaria](/astrology/firdaria/) divide a life into periods ruled in turn by the seven traditional planets and the two nodes, 75 years in all; their best documented early form is in Abu Ma'shar, and the scholar Martin Gansten thinks them probably Persian. Abu Ma'shar also wrote on solar revolutions, the yearly chart for the Sun's return to its birth degree that astrologers now call the [solar return](/astrology/solar-return/).

History on the scale of centuries, next. Masha'allah held that the conjunctions of Jupiter and Saturn, about every twenty years, timed great events, and that their passage from one triplicity to the next mattered most. Abu Ma'shar's book on conjunctions carried the idea to Europe ([Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/)).

And a great many points. The lots of the Greeks multiplied: Abu Ma'shar describes 55 ([doctrine of the lots](/astrology/doctrine-of-the-lots/)).

The critics left something too. In the fourteenth century Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya asked why astrologers gave influence to al-Ra's and al-Dhanab, the Head and the Tail, “two imaginary points”: the Moon's nodes ([nodal axis](/astrology/nodal-axis/)). He was right that they are calculated, not bodies; they mark where the Moon's path crosses the Sun's. I read them all the same, and I look at both, the mean node and the true ([mean node and true node](/astrology/mean-node-and-true-node/)). Avicenna's position, that the planets may act but nobody can know exactly how, is not far from mine: I don't claim to know how astrology works, and [how I read](/how-i-read/) says what I do instead.

From the twelfth century, Latin translators in Spain carried these books north: [Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/). The road back to [Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/) ran through Baghdad.
