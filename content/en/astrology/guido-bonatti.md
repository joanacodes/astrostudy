In thirteenth-century Italy, when an emperor's ally or a city council wanted the stars consulted, Guido Bonatti was the name they sent for. Dante knew it well enough to put him in hell, and astrologers kept reading him for centuries.

## Who {#who}

Neither his birth nor his death is dated. He died between 1296 and 1300, and the accounts his English translator brings together suggest he was then in his eighties. He comes into view in 1233, in Bologna, winning a public dispute with a friar, Giovanni Schio of Vicenza, who held that astrology had no scientific basis.

His clients read like a roll call of the emperor's party: the emperor Frederick II himself, Ezzelino III da Romano, Guido Novello da Polenta and Guido I da Montefeltro, and the communes of Florence, Siena and Forlì. All of them were Ghibellines, who backed the emperor against the pope, and every one of them was excommunicated at some time or another. An astrologer of that side worked in politics whether he meant to or not.

How he ended is unclear. A tradition says he took the Franciscan habit late in life, and in 1924 the order's own journal claimed him as a Friar Minor “who drew scholars to his professional chair from all parts of Europe”, and credited him with a *Theoria Planetarum*, printed in Venice in 1506. The Italian historian Ludovico Antonio Muratori told a darker story, that robbers killed him near Cesena on his way home from Paris, but nothing confirms it.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He wrote the book that outlived him. The *Liber astronomiae*, his Book of Astronomy, dates from around 1277 and was still a classic textbook two hundred years later ([Guido Bonatti’s Book of Astronomy](/astrology/guido-bonattis-book-of-astronomy/)). By then astrology had a firm place in the Latin world of universities and courts ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)), and Bonatti's book is one of the places where you can see what a working astrologer of that world was expected to know.

He put the stars to work for the state. Among his tasks was timing the military campaigns of the Count of Montefeltro, and two modern astrologers, Michael Harding and Charles Harvey, credit him with what may be the earliest use of [midpoints](/astrology/midpoints/), the degree halfway between two planets, to refine that timing. If they are right, the midpoint is far older than the school that built a system on it in the twentieth century ([the Hamburg School (Uranian astrology)](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/)). Choosing the hour of a battle, or of anything else, is a branch I leave alone ([electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/)), and midpoints aren't among the techniques my readings use; I read a birth chart for tendencies ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

He argued for astrology in public. His dispute with the friar in Bologna came more than forty years before the bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemned 219 propositions in 1277, some of them drawn from astrology: what astrology could claim was already being fought over in the open, and Bonatti fought on its side.

His reputation became a warning in literature. Dante named him in the *Inferno* among those punished for divination, and the image of the astrologer in hell has stayed attached to his name.

And he crossed into English. Four centuries later, [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/) published *Anima Astrologiae, or a Guide for Astrologers* (1676), “translated from Guido Bonatus”, so English astrologers could read him in their own language.

He wrote a few generations after the translators of Toledo had put the Arabic astrologers into Latin, a tradition whose critics, in its own language, included [al-Biruni](/astrology/al-biruni/). A century and a half after him, in Florence, one of the cities he had served, [Marsilio Ficino](/astrology/marsilio-ficino/) would turn astrology into a matter of Platonic philosophy and medicine.
