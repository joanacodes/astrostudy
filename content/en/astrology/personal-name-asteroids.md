Somewhere among the asteroids there may be one with your name, or your partner's, or your mother's. It's a lovely thing to find. It isn't a message, and this page says plainly why.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Asteroids are numbered once their orbits are known, and only a few receive names. On 25 May 2026, 26,067 minor planets had names, out of more than 880,000 numbered. Most of the names honour people, astronomers above all, beside figures from myth and fiction, places, animals and plants. A personal-name asteroid is simply one whose name happens to match a person's: yours, or someone you love.

The first asteroids took their names mostly from women of Greek and Roman myth, and some groups still keep to mythology: near-Earth asteroids such as [Apollo (asteroid)](/astrology/apollo-asteroid/) are meant to get mythological names. Elsewhere, names came from everywhere. 966 Muschi, one of Walter Baade's finds, was named after his wife's nickname; Max Wolf named his first asteroid, 323 Brucia, after Catherine Wolfe Bruce; [Hidalgo](/astrology/hidalgo/) honours a Mexican priest, and [Karma](/astrology/karma/) an idea.

Some names mislead. Asteroid 1181 Lilith, found in 1927, was named by its discoverer, Benjamin Jekhowsky, after the French composer Lili Boulanger ([Asteroid Lilith (1181)](/astrology/asteroid-lilith-1181/)). It has nothing to do with the Lilith I read, which is a point of the Moon's orbit, not a body ([Which Lilith is mine?](/astrology/which-lilith-is-mine/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The rules belong to the International Astronomical Union. Once an asteroid has a number, its discoverer may propose a name with a short citation saying whom or what it honours, and the Working Group for Small Bodies Nomenclature approves and publishes it. If a body is still unnamed ten years after its designation, others with a claim to the discovery may propose one, and the working group sometimes names asteroids itself. Names must be pronounceable, preferably one word, and since 1982 no longer than 16 characters. Military and political leaders must have been dead for at least a hundred years; pets are discouraged; people, companies and products known only for business are refused, and so is a citation that reads like an advertisement. Automated surveys now find asteroids by the thousand, so each discoverer may propose only two names every two months, and most new finds are never named at all.

Placing one in a chart works like any small body: a program that includes numbered asteroids gives its degree, the birth time gives its house ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)), and a tight [conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/) with a planet or an angle is what makes people sit up.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Reading meaning into a name match is a stretch, and here is why, plainly:

- **The name was chosen for someone else.** Its citation says whom it honours: an astronomer, a discoverer's wife, a composer, a town. Sharing the spelling doesn't move the honour to you.
- **The sky didn't change when it was named.** Every asteroid circles the Sun under a catalogue designation first, and the name is added later, with a committee's approval. Left unnamed, as most now are, it would sit in exactly the same place in your chart.
- **The odds favour a match.** With 26,067 names, many of them people's, and every variant of your name to try, finding "yours" takes patience, not fate. Once found, it will land somewhere that seems to mean something, because any degree of a chart can be made to.
- **Nothing has tested it.** My notebook covered the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith; no asteroid was on the list, and the system I built for my readings computes none ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

One more thing. Looking up the asteroid with a partner's or an ex's name and reading it in your chart is still a way of reading about someone without asking them ([Synastry and consent](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)). If a name asteroid pleases you, enjoy the coincidence, then go back to your planets ([How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The asteroid with my name was named after me.”** Unless its citation names you, it honours someone else who shared the spelling.
- **“You can buy an asteroid name.”** Official names go through the discoverer and the IAU's working group, which refuses citations that read like advertising; the IAU doesn't recognise star names sold commercially either.
- **“Every asteroid has a name.”** Most don't: in May 2026, fewer than one numbered minor planet in 30 had one.
