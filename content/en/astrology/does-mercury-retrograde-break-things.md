Mercury retrograde gets the blame for lost keys, frozen laptops and messages sent to the wrong person. I understand the appeal: a bad week feels lighter when it has a name. But the name describes something the sky only appears to do, and you deserve the difference spelled out.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

No, not as far as I know. Mercury never actually turns round; it only looks that way from Earth. I know of no published study that tested whether things break, fail or go missing more often while Mercury is retrograde, so I can't point you to a finding either way, and I won't invent one. What the controlled tests of astrology found in general is set out in [Does astrology work?](/astrology/does-astrology-work/), and like the headline about a thirteenth sign ([Did my sign change?](/astrology/did-my-sign-change/)), this one travels faster than the astronomy.

In my readings, a transit like this is weather, not a forecast: something you might notice, never a prediction of what will happen to you.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**What Mercury does.** Retrograde is astronomy's old word for a planet that seems, from Earth, to stop and drift back against the stars for a while, although in reality the planets keep orbiting in the same direction all the time. Those backward loops puzzled the ancients: in the third century BC, Apollonius explained them with circles turning on circles, the epicycles, keeping the Earth at the centre. Mercury's backward stretch comes around its closest approach to Earth and lasts from about 8 to 15 days on either side of it; the range is that wide because Mercury's orbit is noticeably stretched. It happens three or four times a year, for about three weeks each time. [Retrograde, explained](/astrology/retrograde-explained/) shows the geometry, and [Mercury retrograde](/astrology/mercury-retrograde/) what astrologers read into it.

**What tested it.** Nothing that I know of. I know of no published study of whether more goes wrong during Mercury retrograde, and I'd rather tell you that than cite one I haven't read.

**Why it can feel true anyway.** What has been studied is how a belief like this keeps itself going. People who believe in astrology tend to remember the predictions that came true and forget the ones that didn't: one form of [confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/), a tendency psychologists find well beyond astrology. A phone that dies during a retrograde gets remembered as proof; the one that dies three weeks later doesn't get counted at all.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My notebook can't help with this one, and I'd rather say so than stretch it. I checked birth charts, mine and then other people's with their consent: every placement in its sign and house, a tick or a cross. Mercury retrograde as a passing transit isn't a placement of anyone's birth chart, so it was never what I was counting.

What the notebook says about Mercury is modest. Natal Mercury, like the Sun, the rising sign and Venus, held about a third of the time, and overall **30 to 40%** of what I checked held, while **Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90%**. It's a notebook, not a study; it proves nothing about astrology in general.

So I don't predict from Mercury retrograde, in a reading or anywhere else. If you were born with Mercury retrograde, I read it as part of your rhythm: several retrograde planets often describe someone who processes inside first and speaks later, and [that isn't delay](/blog/retrograde-planets/). As for the weeks when the sky does it, slow down if that helps, double-check what matters, and don't let the name of a season decide what you dare to do. Blaming a planet for a bad week is a gentle cousin of blaming a sign for a person, and [Are some signs toxic?](/astrology/are-some-signs-toxic/) says what I think of that.
