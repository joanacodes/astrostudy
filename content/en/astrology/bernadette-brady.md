Bernadette Brady writes about the fixed stars, and the door she uses to bring a star into a chart is the paran: a star on one of the chart's angles at the same moment as a planet. Older practice, as it reached the twentieth century, let a star in only when a planet sat on its degree.

## Who {#who}

She is an astrologer and author. *Brady's Book of Fixed Stars* was published by Red Wheel Weiser in 1999 and *Star and Planet Combinations* by The Wessex Astrologer in 2008; Weiser Books brought out *Predictive Astrology: Tools to Forecast Your Life and Create Your Brightest Future* in an edition of 2022. The United Astrology Congress has given her its Regulus Award for Theory and Understanding, a prize it describes as recognising work that uses astrology with other disciplines; its past recipients also include [Liz Greene](/astrology/liz-greene/) and [Demetra George](/astrology/demetra-george/).

Her stars have a long history behind them. Ptolemy described the natures of the bright stars by likening them to the planets ([Fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)). By the time Nicholas DeVore wrote his *Encyclopedia of Astrology* in 1947, the stars were little consulted in natal astrology, and those who did use them gave a star weight only in close conjunction with a birth planet, never by aspect ([Conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)).

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

She opened a second way in. *Brady's Book of Fixed Stars* sets out paranatellonta, parans for short: a star that is rising, setting, culminating or at its lowest point at the same moment that a significant planet stands on one of those points ([Parans](/astrology/parans/)). A conjunction asks whether a planet shares a star's degree of the zodiac; a paran asks whether the two share the sky's turning, on the horizon or the meridian, at the same instant. The difference matters most for stars far from the Sun's path. Such a star doesn't rise when its zodiac degree rises: depending on how far north or south of the ecliptic it lies, and on the latitude of the birthplace, it can come up well before or after that degree. [Sirius](/astrology/sirius/), nearly forty degrees south of the Sun's path, is the brightest example. A conjunction by degree passes over that difference; a paran is built on it, which is also why a paran can't be read off an ephemeris and has to be worked out for the place.

That makes a star as dependent on the birth time as the angles are. A paran is a matter of what was rising or culminating over a particular place, so it exists only for a known time and place, which is one more reason I don't read a chart without the time ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). It's the same geometry as the maps of [Jim Lewis](/astrology/jim-lewis/), where two planets' lines cross at the places both stood on angles together.

She paired stars with planets. In *Star and Planet Combinations* she links [Altair](/astrology/altair/), which a tradition citing Manilius calls a military star, with boldness, determination and risk-taking.

She has written on forecasting too. Her *Predictive Astrology* is cited for the view that astrology shows a person's strengths and weaknesses, talents and opportunities rather than a fate written in stone. That's how I hold the harder stretches of a chart too: a timetable, not a wall ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

I describe the stars rather than read them. The charts I prepare compute none, and my notebook, which ticked or crossed each planet in its sign and house, never put a star to the test ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

Her stars reach back to Ptolemy's natures. [Chris Brennan](/astrology/chris-brennan/) went back further and wider, to the whole Hellenistic tradition from which Ptolemy's astrology came.
