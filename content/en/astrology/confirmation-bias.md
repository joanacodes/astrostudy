Confirmation bias is the one I'd ask anyone to keep in mind before they tell me astrology has never been wrong about them, and before they tell me it's always wrong. It doesn't care which side you're on.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Confirmation bias is real and well studied: psychologists have been measuring it since 1960. It means we tend to look for, read and remember evidence in the way that suits what we already believe. Research on people who believe in astrology has found it at work in two forms, remembering the predictions that came true and forgetting the rest, and not telling apart a message that shows real insight from one that doesn't. That doesn't settle whether astrology works. It does mean that feeling confirmed is not the same as having been tested, for anyone who reads a chart, including me.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

The habit was noticed long before it was measured. In his *Novum Organum*, in 1620, Francis Bacon wrote that a biased weighing of evidence drove "all superstitions, whether in astrology, dreams, omens, divine judgments or the like".

The modern research began with Peter Wason, an English psychologist. In an experiment published in 1960, he told people that the numbers 2, 4, 6 followed a rule and asked them to find it by proposing sets of three numbers of their own; after each, they were told whether it fitted. The rule was simply any ascending sequence. People found it very hard: they announced rules far narrower than the real one, and they kept offering sets that fitted the rule they already had in mind, rather than sets that could have proved it wrong. Wason read this as a preference for confirmation over refutation, and the name comes from him, although the 1960 paper itself doesn't use it.

The main criticism came in 1987 from Joshua Klayman and Young-Won Ha. Wason's results, they argued, showed a *positive test strategy*, trying the cases your idea predicts, rather than a bias as such: a shortcut that is often informative, and that misleads when the true rule is much broader than your guess, as it was in Wason's task. Since then, research has asked less whether people confirm, and more whether they test their ideas in a way that can tell them something.

People also weigh evidence unevenly. In a study at Stanford published in 1979, Charles Lord, Lee Ross and Mark Lepper gave supporters and opponents of the death penalty the same pair of invented studies, one seeming to support it and one not. Each side judged the study that agreed with them the better one, found fault with the other, and almost everyone ended where they began. Both camps did it.

About astrology in particular, Raymond Nickerson's 1998 review describes the two forms above: believers tend to remember the predictions that came true and not those that failed, and they often don't distinguish a message that shows special ability from one that doesn't. The second is close to [subjective validation](/astrology/subjective-validation/), the pull of a statement that feels personal. Nick Allum, writing in 2010 on why some people consider astrology scientific, counts confirmation bias among the psychological factors that sustain the belief.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My [notebook](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/) is exactly the kind of evidence this bias feeds on. I printed my chart and went through every placement, the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet in its sign and house, with a tick or a cross; then I did the same, with their consent, for family, friends and strangers online. About a third held, 30 to 40%, with the Sun sign, the rising sign, [Venus](/astrology/venus/) and [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/) among the placements that held at that rate. [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/), [Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/) and [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/) held 75 to 90% of the time.

I tried to give the misses their chance. No half marks, and the three that held best were the three I checked most, because I didn't want it to be true. That's the opposite of what the people in Wason's experiment did, and it still isn't enough: I kept the count myself, by hand, mostly on people I knew, and nobody audited it. It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general.

What it changed is [how I read](/how-i-read/). When something in your reading rests on a placement that held less often in my tests, I tell you, so that you can weigh it yourself, misses included.
