The Milky Way is one galaxy among a crowd. With the Andromeda galaxy and others it sits near the edge of a supercluster, a vast flattened family of galaxies, and the Super-Galactic Centre is astrology's way of putting the heart of that family into the zodiac. Astronomers have given it two addresses.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

In the 1950s the astronomer Gérard de Vaucouleurs noticed that the galaxies around us lie, more or less, in a flattened system: the Local Supercluster, also called the Virgo Supercluster. It contains the Local Group, our galaxy's own neighbourhood, near one edge, and it is roughly centred on the Virgo Cluster, a gathering of about 1,300 galaxies some 54 million light years away. Its dominant subcluster is centred on the giant galaxy M87.

In the 1980s a team of seven astronomers found galaxies streaming towards a concentration of mass in another direction, behind the Milky Way's own dust. Donald Lynden-Bell and his colleagues called it a new supergalactic centre, in 1988, and Alan Dressler gave it the name that stuck: the Great Attractor. In 2014 it was taken as the centre of a still larger structure, the Laniakea supercluster, of which the Virgo supercluster is one part.

So "the" Super-Galactic Centre can mean either. Neither was known before the 20th century, and neither is a single object: the Virgo Cluster spreads across about 8° of sky, and the Great Attractor is a broad region rather than one galaxy.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Projected onto the zodiac, the two centres land far apart:

- **The Virgo Cluster**, taken at M87: about 1°04′ Libra in 1930, 2°03′ in 2000, 2°25′ in 2026 and 2°46′ in 2050, some 14° north of the ecliptic. The Sun passes its degree around 24 to 26 September, just after the September equinox ([solstices and equinoxes](/astrology/solstices-and-equinoxes/)), when the [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/) enters [Libra](/astrology/libra/).
- **The Great Attractor**, near galactic longitude 307° and latitude 9°: roughly 12° Scorpio, about 41° south of the ecliptic. A planet "on" it shares its zodiac degree while passing far to the north of it.

Like the stars, both move forward through the tropical zodiac by about a degree every 72 years, because the equinoxes slide back against the sky ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)). The Virgo Cluster has some 27½ degrees of Libra still to cross before it reaches Scorpio, which will take until about the year 3989.

There's a neat symmetry with the [Galactic Centre](/astrology/galactic-centre/), the centre of our own galaxy: it lies a little before the December solstice point, at the end of Sagittarius, and the Virgo Cluster a little after the September equinox point, at the start of Libra.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The Super-Galactic Centre is a single object.”** The Virgo Cluster holds over a thousand galaxies across about 8° of sky, and the Great Attractor is a whole region.
- **“It's the centre of the universe.”** It's the heart of one supercluster, among some ten million in the observable universe.
- **“Astronomers agree on where it is.”** The phrase has been used for the Virgo Cluster and for the Great Attractor, two directions about 40° apart in the zodiac.
- **“It's part of the Milky Way.”** It lies tens of millions of light years beyond our galaxy.
