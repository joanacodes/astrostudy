Once you know where your Vertex is, you already know your Anti-Vertex: the same degree, in the sign straight across the wheel. It is the quieter half of a pair, and most of what it says, it says in answer to its partner.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The prime vertical is the great circle of the sky that runs through the east and west points of the horizon and the point directly overhead. It crosses the ecliptic, the Sun's path through the zodiac, twice. One of those crossings is the [Vertex](/astrology/vertex/); the other, exactly 180° away, is the Anti-Vertex.

So the two are bound to each other, the way the North and South Nodes are bound on the [nodal axis](/astrology/nodal-axis/): one never moves without the other, and you can't place one in a sign without placing the other in the opposite sign. A Vertex at 12° Libra means an Anti-Vertex at 12° Aries; a Vertex at 3° Pisces, an Anti-Vertex at 3° Virgo.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

There's no separate calculation. A program that works out the Vertex has the Anti-Vertex for free, which is why many charts draw only the first and leave you to find the second. If your chart shows neither, that's a setting, not a gap in your chart.

Everything that makes the Vertex sensitive applies here. The point turns with the sky as fast as the Ascendant, so a few minutes of birth time move it and an approximate time can put it in the wrong sign. It depends on the latitude of the birthplace as well as the time. [Without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/), there's no Vertex and so no Anti-Vertex, and I don't read a chart without one.

The house it falls in comes from the house system; in Placidus, the one I use, it is placed by the cusps like any other point. Placidus cusps come in opposite pairs, the 1st across from the 7th, the 5th from the 11th, so the Anti-Vertex always falls in the house opposite the Vertex's.

It's easy to confuse with the [East Point](/astrology/east-point/), and the names don't help. Both are worked out from the time of birth, but from different circles of the sky, which makes them two separate points, each with its own degree. Check the label before you read anything into either.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The Anti-Vertex makes most sense beside the Vertex. Astrologers who use it treat it as the far end of the Vertex's line, much as the [Descendant](/astrology/descendant/) is read against the Ascendant: two opposite signs and two opposite houses, each describing something the other lacks.

Read that way, the Vertex end is where encounters that feel significant tend to be described, and the Anti-Vertex end is the side of the chart that meets them: the sign and house from which you answer what arrives. A planet close to the Anti-Vertex is, by the same token, a planet close to the opposite end of the Vertex: astrologers who use the axis read it as touching both.

None of this is something every school accepts. The Vertex itself divides astrologers, and its opposite point even more so. If you meet a reading built on the Anti-Vertex, ask how much weight it puts there; [how I read](/how-i-read/) explains how I weigh a chart's parts.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The Anti-Vertex is the Vertex's dark side.”** Opposite doesn't mean opposed. The two ends of an axis are read as a balance, not as good and bad.
- **“It's calculated separately and can disagree with the Vertex.”** It is always exactly opposite. If two charts show them out of line, one of the charts has different birth details.
- **“A missing Anti-Vertex means something.”** Many programs simply don't draw it.
- **“It's another name for the East Point.”** Two different circles, two different points.
