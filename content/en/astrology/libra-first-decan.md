Libra's first ten degrees open the second half of the zodiac: from here to the end of Pisces, the Sun's path runs south of the celestial equator. In the northern hemisphere the Sun crosses them in the first days of autumn; in the southern, in the first days of spring.

## Its rulers {#its-rulers}

In the old scheme of [faces](/astrology/faces/), these ten degrees belong to the Moon. The faces run through the planets in the [Chaldean order](/astrology/chaldean-order/) without a break, and the Moon follows Mercury, which holds the last ten degrees of Virgo ([Virgo, third decan](/astrology/virgo-third-decan/)). William Lilly printed the whole table in *Christian Astrology* in 1647.

The modern decans, ruled by element, hand the first slice of every sign to that sign's own ruler: here, [Venus](/astrology/venus/). In that scheme Venus rules twice over, [Libra](/astrology/libra/) as a whole and this part of it, while the next two decans go to the rulers of the other air signs, Aquarius and then Gemini ([triplicities](/astrology/triplicities/)).

So the two systems disagree, and I don't force them to agree. The face is the older layer and the slightest of the five dignities: a [Moon in Libra](/astrology/moon-in-libra/) in these degrees gains a sliver of standing from its own face, and nothing more. Venus, by element, mostly repeats what Libra already says.

## Its image {#its-image}

Medieval books of magic such as the *Picatrix* went on to describe images of the decans. The older layer, and the one I can vouch for, is a list of names. Wallis Budge's list of the Egyptian decans sets Bekathȧ here; in the fourth century [Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/) wrote Seuichut or Senichut; and Cosmas of Maiuma, an eighth-century bishop and hymn-writer, gave the slice to Erinys, one of the goddesses of vengeance that Greek myth also called the Eumenides, the Gracious Ones.

A Fury at the door of the sign of fairness makes a good story, and I tell it as one. No planet here answers to her. Nor can Bekathȧ be found in tonight's sky: the names of the Egyptian decans survive, but which stars they meant is still unknown ([the Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)).

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

A Sun here is a [Sun in Libra](/astrology/sun-in-libra/) born from about 23 September to 2 October, and the decan adds a shade to it, not a second sign. The Sun's arrival at 0° Libra shifts by a few hours, sometimes a day, from one year to the next, so for a birthday on 22 or 23 September the exact time decides whether the Sun had left Virgo.

With any planet in these degrees I start from Libra's way of working, weighing one side against the other, and let the rulers tint it. Venus asks whether the exchange is fair; the Moon's face asks how it feels to the people inside it. Mercury here, for instance, tends to read the mood of a conversation before making its point.

[Libra rising](/astrology/libra-rising/) in its first decan is a matter of minutes. At the latitude of Paris these ten degrees take about 55 minutes to cross the horizon, at London's about 57: Libra rises slowly, yet a time out by half an hour can still move the Ascendant into the next decan, and every house cusp with it. The Ascendant's decan, like every house, depends on the time of birth, which is why I don't read a chart without it ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Two slower bodies have lingered here. [Pluto in Libra](/astrology/pluto-in-libra/) crossed these degrees from late 1971 to 1976, looping back and forth at both ends, so most people born in those years share them, and only the house makes them personal. The fixed star [Vindemiatrix](/astrology/vindemiatrix/), Virgo's grape-gatherer, stood inside this decan until about 2005, at 9°56′ Libra in 2000; precession has since carried it to 10°18′, just over the line ([Libra, second decan](/astrology/libra-second-decan/)).
