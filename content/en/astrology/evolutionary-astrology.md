Psychology turned astrology inward. In the late twentieth century a group of astrologers turned it backwards in time as well, and began to read the chart as the present moment of a soul with a long history. They called their approach evolutionary astrology.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

Steven Forrest, one of its founders, describes it as “a form of psychological astrology which is integrated with metaphysics. Where a twentieth century psychological astrologer might focus on the dynamics of childhood, the evolutionary astrologer focuses on the childhood of the soul—prior lifetimes, and the issues left unresolved from them.”

He used the term in his 1984 book *The Inner Sky*. Jeffrey Wolf Green spread it with *Pluto: The Evolutionary Journey of the Soul* (1985), and the two later wrote *Measuring the Night* (2000) together. Their techniques are their own, but they share one assumption: the present birth chart derives from conditions experienced in prior lifetimes.

They were not alone in the idea, only in the name. Martin Schulman had already published *Karmic Astrology, Volume 1: The Moon's Nodes and Reincarnation* in 1975, and in the same years Stephen Arroyo, in *Astrology, Karma & Transformation: The Inner Dimensions of the Birth Chart*, and A. T. Mann, in *The Divine Life* (1986), explored the chart in terms of [karma](/astrology/karma/) and rebirth without calling it evolutionary. The scholar Kieron Le Grice describes Arroyo's book as influenced by Rudhyar, which shows how close the two schools stood.

Forrest sums the method up as “the fusion of humanistic psychology on one hand with ancient metaphysics on the other”. In his account a chart describes an “evolutionary predicament or a karmic predicament”, the illusions and issues a person brings with them, and also what he calls “the medicine”, the way of working through it. He insists on choice: “I unabashedly preach the primacy of free will in shaping our experience,” he writes, and “none of us is limited to a 'nature' that is cast in stone by the positions of the planets.” He also calls it the most verifiable form of astrology he has met. His later titles show the range of the school: *The Changing Sky* (1986), on [transits](/astrology/transits/) and progressions read in an evolutionary way, *The Book of Pluto* (1995) and *Yesterday's Sky: Astrology and Reincarnation* (2008).

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

Two placements moved to the centre of the chart. Green put [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/) in his title, and Schulman put the Moon's nodes in his, read in terms of reincarnation. In this school the [South Node](/astrology/south-node/) usually stands for what a soul brings with it, and the [nodal axis](/astrology/nodal-axis/) for the road from old ground to new.

A strong stress on choice. Whatever one makes of reincarnation, Forrest's insistence that a chart describes a predicament and not a sentence is one I share.

And a question about evidence, which it raises itself. Forrest calls his approach verifiable; my own testing was narrower. I put a tick or a cross beside each placement for what it said about the living person, and a past life can't be marked either way ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). So I leave prior lifetimes out of my readings and read the nodes in this life: where a person starts, and where they're asked to go. Since the nodes can be calculated two ways, I check both, and say so when the [mean node and the true node](/astrology/mean-node-and-true-node/) fall in different signs.

Evolutionary astrology took Rudhyar's turn beyond the personality as far as it would go ([humanistic astrology](/astrology/humanistic-astrology/)). Other astrologers of the same decades went the opposite way, back to the oldest texts they could translate: [the traditional revival](/astrology/traditional-revival/).
