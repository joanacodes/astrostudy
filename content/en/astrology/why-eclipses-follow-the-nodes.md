A new moon happens every month, a solar eclipse only a few times a year. The difference is a tilt of about five degrees between the Moon's path and the Sun's, and it ties every eclipse to the two points where those paths cross: the Moon's nodes. That's why the eclipses move through the zodiac with the nodes, and why I read them together.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Picture the sky as two great circles. One is the ecliptic, the path the Sun seems to follow through the year. The other is the Moon's path, tilted to it by about 5°, as Fred Espenak's eclipse pages for NASA put it. Two tilted circles cross at two points, exactly opposite each other: the [North Node](/astrology/north-node/), where the Moon crosses going north, and the [South Node](/astrology/south-node/), where it crosses going south. Old books call them the Dragon's Head and the Dragon's Tail.

At a new moon the Moon is in the Sun's direction, and at a full moon in the direction of the Earth's shadow. But because of the tilt, it usually passes above or below them, and nothing is eclipsed. The three bodies can only line up when the new or full moon falls near a node. Espenak gives the limit as about 17°: a new moon within that distance of a node gives a solar eclipse somewhere on the Earth, a full moon a lunar one.

So an eclipse is a new or full moon in the right place, and the right place is set by the nodes. The Sun reaches one node or the other about every 173 days, which is why eclipses come in [eclipse seasons](/astrology/eclipse-seasons/), two a year.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The nodes aren't fixed. The Sun's pull swings the Moon's orbit slowly round, and the nodes slide backwards through the zodiac, about 18 months in each pair of opposite signs and about 18.6 years for the whole circle. The eclipses follow, backwards too. In the dates this site keeps, from the true node, the North Node is in Aquarius from July 2026 to March 2028, in Capricorn until September 2029, then in Sagittarius until March 2031. The eclipses track it: Leo and Aquarius through 2026 and 2027, Cancer and Capricorn in 2028 and 2029, Gemini and Sagittarius in 2029 and 2030.

There are two ways of calculating the nodes, the smoothed mean node and the wobbling true node, a degree or two apart at most ([mean node and true node](/astrology/mean-node-and-true-node/)). I look at both, and near the edge of a sign their dates can differ by weeks.

The pattern also repeats. After 223 lunations, about 18 years and 11 days, the Sun, the Moon and the nodes come back to nearly the same arrangement, and a similar eclipse returns: Espenak calls this the saros ([saros cycles](/astrology/saros-cycles/)). The site's eclipse file shows it plainly. The total solar eclipse of 1 August 2008, at 9°32′ Leo, comes back as the total eclipse of 12 August 2026, at 20°02′ Leo, and that of 22 July 2009, at 29°26′ Cancer, as the total eclipse of 2 August 2027, at 9°55′ Leo: each about ten degrees further on.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Your natal nodes keep a record too: around the time you were born, eclipses were falling near the axis they mark, which is why some astrologers look at the [prenatal eclipse](/astrology/prenatal-eclipse/). About every 18.6 years, at your [nodal return](/astrology/nodal-return/), the eclipses come back to that same axis.

In a reading I treat an eclipse the same way wherever it falls. It counts within about three degrees of a natal planet, angle or node, at either end of its own axis, and I read it through that point and its house, as emphasis, never as an omen. The [eclipses on your natal planets](/astrology/eclipses-on-your-natal-planets/) page goes through those contacts one by one.

I read the two nodes as one line ([nodal axis](/astrology/nodal-axis/)), so an eclipse near your South Node is no worse than one near your North Node: one end describes what is familiar, the other what is less practised. Which houses that line crosses needs your [birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The nodes cause eclipses.”** They're crossing points with nothing at them. The eclipse is the Sun, the Earth and the Moon in line; the nodes only mark where that can happen.
- **“An eclipse can happen at any full moon.”** Most full moons pass above or below the Earth's shadow. Only those near a node are eclipsed.
- **“Eclipses near the North Node are lucky, near the South Node unlucky.”** I read the two ends as one axis, and an eclipse at either end as emphasis, never luck.
- **“Mean or true node changes the eclipses.”** It changes where the node is drawn in a chart, by a degree or two at most. The eclipses are where the Sun and Moon put them.
