Deneb Algedi is the tail of the sea-goat, and a fixed star that a planet can actually pass. It sits so close to the Sun's path that the Moon sometimes slides in front of it, which makes a conjunction with it something the sky can show as well as the chart.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Deneb Algedi is δ Capricorni, the brightest star of Capricornus, at magnitude 2.87 in the Yale catalogue. It is really two stars orbiting so close, and so edge-on to us, that they eclipse each other every 24½ hours, dimming the light by at most a quarter of a magnitude. At 38.7 light-years it is the nearest eclipsing pair known.

Its constellation and its sign have parted company. Charts for 2026 place the star at 23°54′ [Aquarius](/astrology/aquarius/), charts for 2000 at 23°32′ and charts for 1930 at 22°33′. Around 150 CE, in Ptolemy's day, it stood at about 28° of the sign of Capricorn; the March equinox, from which the signs are counted, has slipped back against the stars since then and carried the tail into the next sign ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).

What sets it apart is its latitude: only 2.6° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/). The Moon's path is tilted about 5° to the Sun's, so the Moon can cover this star, and astronomers watched it do so in 1951, 1962 and 1988; planets can too, though much more rarely ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

The name is Arabic, ðanab al-jady, the tail of the goat: the fish tail of the sea-goat. An older alternative name for the star is Scheddi.

Medieval astrology in Europe and the Arab world placed it among the fifteen Behenian stars, each thought a root of power for certain planets and paired with a stone and a plant for making talismans. Deneb Algedi's planets were [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/) and [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/), its stone chalcedony and its plant marjoram, and it had a kabbalistic sign of its own. [Vega](/astrology/vega/), 38° earlier in the zodiac, is the other Behenian star in this stretch of sky.

The tail has kept a double reputation. Anne Wright, a modern writer on the fixed stars, links its flexible tail with good and bad fortune alike: fortune that turns, in the tradition's words, rather than fortune that's promised. In Chinese astronomy it is the fourth star of the Line of Ramparts, a long row of stars running through Capricornus, Aquarius and Pisces.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

In the tradition's terms, a planet or angle within about a degree of the star is conjunct it: from 22°54′ to 24°54′ Aquarius for a birth in 2026, about 1°20′ earlier for one in 1930 ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). The Sun arrives on about 11 and 12 February, so those birthdays put a [Sun in Aquarius](/astrology/sun-in-aquarius/) on the goat's tail.

Because the star lies so near the Sun's path, the planets that reach its degree usually pass within a few degrees of it in the sky, and the Moon now and then right over it. That's a real meeting, unlike the paper contacts of stars far to the north or south. Whether the Moon was on it at your birth, though, turns on the hour ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Saturn and Mercury, the pair the Behenian list gave it, are the tone a star astrologer would lay over the planet there. The goat's tail lies almost on the planets' own road, and still I keep it out of readings, which compute no stars; its double fortune is lore to know, never something I'd hand anyone as a prediction.
