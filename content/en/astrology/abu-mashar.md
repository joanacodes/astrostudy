Abu Ma’shar came to astrology late, by the traditional account at forty-seven, and later historians judged him the greatest astrologer of the Abbasid court. Medieval Europe called him Albumasar and learned much of the subject from his books.

## Who {#who}

By David Pingree's dates, he was born in 787 in Balkh, a town of Khurasan, and died in 886 at Wasit, in Iraq; another account puts his life almost two decades later, from 805 to 885. He belonged to the third generation, after the Arab conquest, of a Khurasani learned elite still attached to Persia's older writings, and he was a scholar of hadith before he was an astrologer. According to the biographical tradition he turned to astrology only at forty-seven, around 832 or 833. A bitter dispute with al-Kindi, the leading Arab philosopher of his day, convinced him that he needed mathematics to follow philosophical arguments.

Astrology at court carried risks. Under the caliph al-Musta'in, a prediction of his came true and displeased the caliph, who had him whipped; Ibn al-Nadim preserves his comment: “I hit the mark and I was severely punished.” He also had a cause of his own. In his book on conjunctions, he drew on the astrology of Sasanian Persia to predict the collapse of Arab rule and the return of Persian rule, a forecast of the kind I don't make, about any country ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

He did not trust every tradition he received, either. He doubted that the [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/) who wrote the *Almagest* was the one who wrote the astrology book, and admitted that he did not know.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He wrote the textbook. His *Great Introduction*, written around 848, is a manual for training astrologers, and from the eleventh century it was read in Latin and Greek ([Abu Ma’shar’s introductions](/astrology/abu-mashars-introductions/)). In it he wrote that “only by observing the great diversity of planetary motions can we comprehend the unnumbered varieties of change in this world”, a sentence that asks for patience before it asks for belief.

He argued from the sea. The same book relates the tides to the Moon's position and notes two high tides a day. Abu Ma’shar rejected the Greek idea that moonlight raised them; for him the Moon had a virtue that drew the water, and Albert the Great later built his own theory of the tides partly on that. The tide is real, and the Moon does pull it, by gravity rather than by virtue. Whether the Moon also moves our moods is a different question, and it has been tested ([Do full moons change behaviour?](/astrology/do-full-moons-change-behaviour/)).

He made history a matter of conjunctions. His book on religions and dynasties, probably his most important, was commented on by Roger Bacon, Pierre d'Ailly and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola. The great conjunctions of Jupiter and Saturn that preoccupied European astrologers until the end of the sixteenth century reached them above all through translations of his book on them ([Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/)).

He brought Aristotle with him. The historian Richard Lemay argued in 1962 that Abu Ma’shar's writings were very likely the single most important source through which medieval Europe recovered Aristotle's natural philosophy before the middle of the twelfth century ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)).

And he kept old images alive. In the sixth book of the *Great Introduction*, he describes the figures of the decans, the ten-degree thirds of each sign, according to Greek, Indian and Persian authorities, and much of the Persian material goes back to Teucer of Babylon ([the Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)).

His older colleague at the Abbasid court had been [Masha’allah](/astrology/mashaallah/). Nearly a century after Abu Ma’shar's death, [al-Biruni](/astrology/al-biruni/) was born: a scholar who would teach astrology while doubting its forecasts. The world all three worked in is [Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/).
