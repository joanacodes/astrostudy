Whole Sign is the simplest house system there is: one sign, one house. It's also the one with the longest history behind it, and the one many astrologers have returned to since the 1980s. I don't use it, and I'll say why at the end, but it deserves to be understood on its own terms.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

In Whole Sign houses, your rising sign is the whole of the 1st house, from its first degree to its last, wherever the Ascendant falls in it. The next sign is the 2nd house, the one after the 3rd, and so on round the twelve. Every house is exactly one sign, so the sign and the house always agree: if Taurus is your 2nd house, every planet in Taurus is in your 2nd.

The Ascendant's degree doesn't open the 1st house; it's a point inside it. With 25° Virgo rising, a planet at 5° Virgo is in the 1st house, although it rose some time before the Ascendant did. And the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) floats free of the houses. It lands in whichever sign-house holds it: the 10th, or often the 9th or the 11th, and further from the equator, now and then, the 8th or the 12th. At the latitude of London or Paris, it falls outside the 10th in more charts than not.

Two things never happen in Whole Sign. No sign can be intercepted, since every sign is a house ([intercepted signs](/astrology/intercepted-signs/)), and there is no polar limit: wherever there is a rising sign, the houses follow.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Whole Sign was the main house system of Hellenistic astrology, and it has been used without a break in Indian astrology. In the West it fell out of use, probably around the 10th century, and by the 20th it was all but unknown. It was rediscovered in the 1980s and 1990s, and Robert Hand's *Whole Sign Houses: The Oldest House System* (2000) made the case for it. Historians still argue over how the ancient authors worked: Martin Gansten's study of Vettius Valens finds houses often approximated by sign, with calculation by degree upheld in principle as the more accurate method.

Several traditional techniques count in whole signs too. [Annual profections](/astrology/annual-profections/) move the rising sign on one sign a year, so a profected year and a Whole Sign house are the same thing, and the old aspects were counted sign to sign as well ([whole-sign aspects](/astrology/whole-sign-aspects/)).

Don't confuse it with the [natural zodiac](/astrology/natural-zodiac/), which always starts at Aries: Whole Sign starts from your own rising sign, and it needs the birth time to find it ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I use [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/), a quadrant system like [Koch](/astrology/koch/), and Whole Sign gives up the two things I value most in it: the Midheaven on the cusp of the 10th, and houses built from the turning of the sky over the birthplace. That's a choice of method, not a judgement. Whole Sign's strengths are real: it's simple, it reads the same in every latitude, it fits the traditional techniques that count by sign, and it never splits a sign between two houses.

What I ask is consistency. A planet can sit a whole house apart in the two systems, so read about your chart in the system it was drawn in, and compare like with like ([which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/)). Every house on this site is written for Placidus, and I say so whenever it matters ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“In Whole Sign, the Midheaven is the 10th house.”** It's a point that can fall in the 9th, 10th or 11th, and further out at high latitudes.
- **“Whole Sign ignores the Ascendant's degree.”** The degree stays a sensitive point of the chart; it just doesn't start a house.
- **“Whole Sign died out in antiquity.”** It has been used without a break in Indian astrology, and the West took it up again in the 1980s.
