Every position in a chart comes with numbers, and the numbers come in an old, slightly odd format: degrees, minutes and seconds, counted in sixties. It's the system used for angles everywhere, and it takes about a minute to learn.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The zodiac is a circle, and a circle has 360 degrees. Each of the twelve signs takes 30 of them. A degree is split into 60 minutes of arc, written with a single stroke, ′, and each minute into 60 seconds of arc, written with two, ″. These minutes and seconds measure angles, not time, though they count in the same sixties as a clock.

So a position like 16°53′ Scorpio means sixteen degrees and fifty-three minutes into the sign of Scorpio. Programs and ephemerides often write it 16♏53, with the sign's glyph standing in for its name ([Glyphs: the cheat sheet](/astrology/glyphs-the-cheat-sheet/)). The new moon of 9 November 2026 falls at exactly that point.

Counted round the whole circle from 0° Aries, the same point is 226°53′: Scorpio starts at 210°, after seven signs of 30° each. That way of counting is [zodiacal longitude](/astrology/zodiacal-longitude/), and it's what software works with underneath.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Degrees inside a sign run from 0 to 29. A planet at 0°12′ has only just arrived; one at 29°48′ is about to leave. Because the count starts at zero, 16°53′ lies in the seventeenth degree of its sign, and astrologers use both ways of speaking about it, which trips people up. The first and last degrees of a sign have pages of their own ([0° of a sign](/astrology/0-of-a-sign/), [The anaretic degree (29°)](/astrology/anaretic-degree-29/)).

A few sizes help the numbers mean something:

- **The Moon's disc** is about half a degree across, some 30 minutes of arc.
- **The Sun** moves about a degree a day, and **the Moon** about 13.
- **The Ascendant** moves a degree in about four minutes of clock time on average, which is why the birth time matters so much ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).
- **Precession** shifts the equinox by about 50 seconds of arc a year ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)).

Programs differ in how they round. One may show 29°59′ where another shows 0°00′ of the next sign, or drop the minutes altogether. Near the edge of a sign or a house, always look at the minutes, and check whether the figure was rounded or cut short.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In most of a reading, the sign and the house carry the meaning, and the degree sits in the background. It comes forward in three places.

- **Aspects.** Whether two planets are in aspect depends on the distance between them, in degrees and minutes, and on how far from exact it's allowed to stray ([orbs](/astrology/orbs/)). Two planets at 3° and 9° of Aries are 6° apart; at 3° Aries and 9° Taurus, they're 36° apart.
- **Edges.** A planet a few minutes from the next sign, or from a house cusp, is where the birth time has to be right, since a small error moves it across the line. In Placidus, the house system I use, the cusps fall at uneven degrees, so the minutes count there too.
- **The angles.** The Ascendant and Midheaven are the most time-sensitive degrees in a chart. A birth time rounded to the quarter-hour can move them by several degrees, and I'd rather check the time than read a degree that might not be yours.

Seconds of arc hardly ever matter in a natal chart, because no birth time is that precise. They belong to astronomy, and to the slow drift of the stars.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“16° means the 16th degree.”** It means sixteen whole degrees have passed, so the planet is in the seventeenth.
- **“The 29th degree is doomed.”** It's the last degree of a sign, a place of completion that some astrologers watch closely. No degree is a sentence.
- **“The minutes on a chart are minutes of time.”** They're minutes of arc, sixtieths of a degree: the same word for a different measure.
- **“The more decimals, the truer the chart.”** A chart is only as precise as the birth time behind it, and seconds of arc can't fix a time rounded to the hour.
