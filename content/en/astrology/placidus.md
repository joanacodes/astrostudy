Placidus is the house system behind every chart I read and every house on this site. It's also the one most often criticised for its uneven houses, so here is what it does, where it came from, where it stops working, and why I chose it anyway.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Placidus is a quadrant system. The two axes of the chart, horizon and meridian, cut it into four quarters, and the four angles open the four angular houses: the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) the 1st, the IC the 4th, the Descendant the 7th and the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) the 10th. What sets Placidus apart is how each quarter is cut into three ([which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/) compares the others).

It cuts by time. On any day, at any place, each degree of the zodiac rises in the east, climbs to the meridian and sets in the west, and the time it takes to climb depends on the degree and on the latitude. Placidus takes that climb for each degree and divides it in three. The 12th house begins at the degree that has made a third of its climb from the horizon, the 11th at the degree that has made two thirds. Below the horizon, the 2nd and 3rd cusps come from each degree's time under the earth in the same way, and the other four cusps lie opposite.

In the language of the old seasonal hours, where daylight is cut into twelve equal hours whatever its length, the 12th cusp is the degree that rose two of its own daytime hours ago, the 11th four, the Midheaven six ([planetary hours](/astrology/planetary-hours/) use the same unequal hours). At the equator, where every degree spends six sidereal hours climbing, the houses come out close to 30° each.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The name belongs to Placidus de Titis (1603–1668), an Olivetan monk who taught mathematics at the University of Pavia from 1657 and published tables of houses. He didn't invent the method: Giovanni Antonio Magini had published it in 1602, and the twelfth-century astrologer Abraham Ibn Ezra had described it as Ptolemy's, an attribution Placidus accepted. His tables and books, translated into English in 1789 and 1814, popularised it, and it is now the most widely used house system in Western astrology.

Away from the equator the houses grow unequal, because the zodiac rises at a slant: some signs come up quickly and others slowly. At the latitude of London, a Placidus house can be under 20° wide or over 50°, and a sign can sit wholly inside one house with no cusp of its own ([intercepted signs](/astrology/intercepted-signs/)).

Beyond the polar circles, at about 66° north or south, the method stops. Some degrees of the zodiac never rise or set there, so they have no climb to divide, and the system can't be calculated ([houses at high latitudes](/astrology/houses-at-high-latitudes/)). [Koch](/astrology/koch/), its close relative, has the same limit.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I use Placidus for three reasons. It puts the Ascendant and the Midheaven where I want them, on the cusps of the 1st and 10th houses, so the two most personal points of a chart are also where its houses begin ([house cusps](/astrology/house-cusps/)). It builds the houses out of time, the turning of the sky over one place, which is exactly what a birth time and place record. And it gives me one consistent frame: every page on this site about houses, planets in houses and transits through them is written in it, so what you read here and what I tell you in a reading agree.

None of that makes the other systems wrong. [Whole Sign houses](/astrology/whole-sign-houses/) were the main system of Hellenistic astrology and are simpler, equal houses keep every house the same size, and each is a reasoned answer to the same question. What matters is to keep to one and to read about your chart in the system it was drawn in.

Placidus does ask for a precise birth time. Its cusps move about a degree every four minutes, so a time rounded to the hour can move a planet across one ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). I check the time before I lean on a house ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Placidus invented the Placidus system.”** Magini published it in 1602, and Ibn Ezra had already credited the method to Ptolemy. Placidus made it famous.
- **“Placidus houses are 30° each.”** Only near the equator, roughly. Elsewhere they are shares of time, and their widths vary a great deal.
- **“Placidus fails for anyone born in the north.”** It fails beyond the polar circles. Everywhere else, including the whole of Britain and France, it works as designed.
- **“Placidus is the most popular system, so it must be the best.”** Being common makes it convenient for comparing charts; it proves nothing. I use it for the reasons above, not for its popularity.
