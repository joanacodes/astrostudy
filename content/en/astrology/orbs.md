Two planets are almost never exactly 90° apart, or exactly 120°. Orbs answer the question that follows: how close is close enough? Astrologers have argued about it for centuries, so here's the practice on this site, and why it's one reasonable choice among several.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

An orb is the leeway an astrologer allows either side of an exact aspect. The [square](/astrology/square/) is 90°; with an orb of 6°, two planets anywhere from 84° to 96° apart count as square. The distance is measured in degrees and minutes ([degrees, minutes and seconds](/astrology/degrees-minutes-and-seconds/)), and the nearer to exact, the tighter the aspect is said to be.

In the tradition, the orb belonged to the planet, not to the aspect. William Lilly, in *Christian Astrology* (1647), gave each planet an orb of its own and took half of each, a moiety: two planets were in aspect when they came within the sum of their two halves. Many astrologers today set orbs by aspect instead, wider for the [conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/), square, trine and opposition than for the minor aspects, and often wider again when the Sun or Moon is involved. Neither way is the only right one, and a careful astrologer says which orbs a reading uses.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Orbs differ from one astrologer to the next, and from one kind of chart to the next. These are the ones this site uses.

- **Natal aspects.** 8° when the Sun or the Moon is one of the two bodies, 6° otherwise. That's the orb behind every count on these pages: when a natal aspect page says that about 1 person in 11 is born with it, it means within that orb.
- **Minor aspects.** A degree or two, sometimes about one. They sit close together on the circle, and a looser orb would blur one into the next.
- **Synastry.** In my synastry readings, each body has its own orb, and a contact takes the wider of the two: 8° for the Sun and Moon; 6° for Mercury, Venus and Mars; 5° for Jupiter and Saturn; 4° for Uranus, Neptune and Pluto; 3° for Chiron, Lilith and the North Node. The full orb applies to a conjunction or an opposition, a little less to a trine or a square, and less again to a sextile: with the Sun, that's 8°, 6.8° and 4.8°.
- **Transits.** In my forecasts, a transit counts from 1½° before the exact angle to 1° after it. A slow planet can stay inside that window for weeks, or for months when it turns retrograde over the point.

Two refinements matter almost as much as the number. An aspect still tightening reads differently from one already past exact ([applying and separating aspects](/astrology/applying-and-separating-aspects/)). And an orb can stretch an aspect across a sign boundary: the Sun at 29° Aries and the Moon at 2° Leo are 93° apart, a square by distance, although Aries and Leo are both fire signs and normally make a trine ([out-of-sign aspects](/astrology/out-of-sign-aspects/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In the system I built for my readings, the orb changes how strongly an aspect speaks, never what it means. A square within a degree is a steady presence in a life; the same square at 7° is something felt now and then, louder in some seasons than in others. The two planets, their signs and their houses decide what the aspect is about. The orb only turns the volume up or down.

So I read tight aspects first and wide ones lightly, and I'd rather leave out an aspect at the very edge of its orb than build a paragraph on it. That matters most for the minor aspects, where a loose orb can turn almost any pair of planets into something. It's also why I'm open about the numbers: if another astrologer uses wider orbs and finds an aspect I didn't mention, neither of us is necessarily wrong. We drew the line in different places. How I weigh the rest is in [How I read](/how-i-read/).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“An aspect is either exact or it doesn't exist.”** Exact aspects are rare. Every astrologer allows some leeway; the only question is how much ([exact aspects](/astrology/exact-aspects/)).
- **“There's one correct orb.”** Astrologers have disagreed for centuries, from Lilly's planetary orbs to today's tables by aspect. Good practice is to say which orbs you use.
- **“A wider orb makes an aspect more powerful.”** The reverse: the closer to exact, the stronger it reads.
- **“Orbs are the same in every kind of chart.”** Natal, synastry and transit orbs differ, here and elsewhere, because each answers a different question.
