Alpheratz belongs to two figures at once: it is the head of Andromeda, the chained princess, and a corner of the Great Square of Pegasus, whose horse gave it its Arabic name. It stands at 14°40′ Aries, and old star lore attached a promise to it: honour and riches.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Alpheratz is Alpha Andromedae, magnitude 2.06, the brightest star of Andromeda whenever [Mirach](/astrology/mirach/) dims. It marks the upper left corner of the Great Square, with [Scheat](/astrology/scheat/) at the upper right, about 97 light-years away. To the eye it is one star; it is really two, circling each other every 96.9 days, and the brighter one has an unusual atmosphere, heavy with mercury and manganese.

Its tropical position was 13°19′ [Aries](/astrology/aries/) for a birth in 1930, 14°18′ in 2000 and 14°40′ in 2026. Its latitude is 25.7° north of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), within a fraction of a degree of Mirach's, which lies 16° further along the zodiac at 0°46′ Taurus. The two bright stars of Andromeda run side by side above the Sun's path, and neither ever comes near a planet.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Ptolemy counted the star in both Andromeda and Pegasus, and Johann Bayer gave it a letter in each, Alpha Andromedae and Delta Pegasi; when the modern constellation boundaries were drawn, it went to Andromeda alone. Its names kept the horse. Alpheratz, Alpherat and Sirrah all come from the Arabic surrat al-faras, the navel of the horse, and have nothing to do with the word alpha. Medieval astronomers also called it the head of the woman in chains.

W. T. Olcott's *Star Lore of All Ages* (1911) records two more things. Alpheratz was one of the Three Guides that mark the prime meridian of the heavens, with [Algenib](/astrology/algenib/) and Beta Cassiopeiae: in the catalogue, both Alpheratz and Algenib lie within a few degrees of the circle that runs from the celestial pole through the March equinox. And it was believed to bless those born under its influence with honour and riches. That is the tradition speaking, a fortunate star in its own vocabulary, and I report it as such ([benefics and malefics](/astrology/benefics-and-malefics/)).

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Honour and riches is the kind of promise I won't attach to anyone's chart. Money isn't something a chart can forecast ([Can astrology pick investments?](/astrology/can-astrology-pick-investments/)), and a planet near 14° Aries describes how that planet works in you, not what life will hand you.

In the tradition, a planet would need to sit within about a degree of the star's longitude to count ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)): 13°40′ to 15°40′ Aries for a birth in 2026, 12°19′ to 14°19′ for one in 1930. The Sun crosses that span on about 3 and 4 April each year, and Saturn sat in it for births in parts of 1938–39, spring 1968 and 1997–98. The Moon spends only a few hours there each month, which the birth time alone can settle ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

The latitude counts as much as the degree. At 25.7° north, Alpheratz is never beside a planet in the sky, and "conjunct" here means sharing a number on the zodiac's scale ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

On this site Alpheratz is described, not read: stars have no place in the system I built for my readings, nor among the placements my notebook tested ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).
