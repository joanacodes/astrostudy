An eclipse tends to be followed, eighteen years and eleven days later, by another very like it. The oldest record of that echo comes from Babylonian astronomers of the last centuries BCE, and the cycle behind it, the saros, is the reason eclipses come in families.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The saros is 223 lunations, new moon to new moon: 6,585.32 days, which comes to 18 years and 11 days plus about 8 hours, or 10 or 12 days instead of 11, depending on how many leap years fall in between. Three of the Moon's rhythms run out almost together over that span: 223 rounds of phases, nearly 242 returns to the same node and nearly 239 returns to its nearest point to the Earth. So the Sun, the Moon and the nodes come back to almost the same arrangement ([why eclipses follow the nodes](/astrology/why-eclipses-follow-the-nodes/)), at almost the same distance and in almost the same season, and a very similar eclipse follows, alike in appearance and in length.

Eclipses one saros apart form a saros series. A series of [solar eclipses](/astrology/solar-eclipses/) opens with a small partial eclipse near one of the poles, moves its track across the Earth one saros at a time, and closes with another partial eclipse near the other pole. A series lasts between 1,226 and 1,550 years and counts 69 to 87 eclipses. About forty solar series are running at any time, and as many lunar ones, so the eclipses of a single [eclipse season](/astrology/eclipse-seasons/) belong to different families.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

For a chart, what counts is where the returning eclipse falls. Eighteen years and eleven days carry the Sun about ten degrees past its earlier place, so each return lands about ten degrees further on. One family shows it plainly in the eclipses this site keeps. The total [lunar eclipse](/astrology/lunar-eclipses/) of 4 May 2004, with the Moon at 14°39′ Scorpio, came back on 16 May 2022 at 25°16′ Scorpio, and returns on 26 May 2040 at 5°49′ Sagittarius.

The extra hours move the eclipse on the ground, not in the chart. Each return comes about eight hours later in the day: that lunar family peaked at 20:30 UT in 2004 and 04:11 UT in 2022. A solar eclipse's track shifts about a third of the way round the Earth to the west, so the same place rarely sees two members of one family in a row. Three saros, about 54 years and a month, bring the hour back round; [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/) knew that triple cycle as the exeligmos, the turn of the wheel.

Halfway through, 9 years and 5½ days on, comes an eclipse of the other kind with matching features: after a total or an annular eclipse of the Sun, a total eclipse of the Moon. That half-cycle is called the sar.

The name came late. Edmond Halley applied it to the cycle in 1686, taking it from the Suda, a Byzantine lexicon of the 11th century, and Guillaume Le Gentil objected in 1756 that the Chaldeans had meant something else by it. The name stayed. The cycle itself was known long before: 223 months are inscribed on the Antikythera mechanism, made in Greece around 150 to 100 BCE, and the steady records of [Babylonian astrology](/astrology/babylonian-astrology/) had shown it earlier still.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read an eclipse through the natal point it touches, within about three degrees, at either end of its axis ([eclipses on your natal planets](/astrology/eclipses-on-your-natal-planets/)). By that rule, the saros seldom brings an eclipse back onto the same planet: ten degrees is far outside the orb. A family passes over a chart like a slow procession, one member every eighteen years, each a step further along the zodiac.

Two other rhythms return eclipses closer to home. An eclipse often has a twin about nineteen years later, falling on almost the same date and almost the same degree; and about every 18.6 years, at your [nodal return](/astrology/nodal-return/), the eclipses come back to the axis of your natal nodes. Every eclipse also belongs to some saros series, including the one before your birth that some astrologers read as part of the chart ([prenatal eclipse](/astrology/prenatal-eclipse/)): its next member came about eighteen years later, ten degrees on.

What the saros gives me is a sense of scale. The eclipse touching your Sun this year belongs to a family more than a thousand years long, which has crossed countless charts before yours. I read it as emphasis on that point, weather, not a forecast, never an omen ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“An eclipse returns to the same degree every 18 years.”** A saros return lands about ten degrees further on. Near-identical degrees come back on other cycles.
- **“A saros return replays what happened eighteen years before.”** It brings back a similar eclipse, not a similar year. The sky's geometry repeats; a life doesn't.
- **“Every eclipse in a series is total.”** A series opens and closes with partial eclipses; the central ones, total or annular, come in its middle stretch.
- **“The saros is an astrologer's invention.”** It's astronomy, born of the Moon's own rhythms, and astronomers still number eclipses by their saros series.
