In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries astrology had its last long run as a learned art. Printing spread it, philosophers fought over it, and some of the men who built modern astronomy earned part of their living by it.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

It began in Florence with a priest-physician. [Marsilio Ficino](/astrology/marsilio-ficino/), who lived from 1433 to 1499, translated Plato and wrote *De vita libri tres*, the *Three Books on Life* (1489), full of medical and astrological advice for keeping well. He was accused of heresy before Pope Innocent VIII that same year and acquitted, and in 1494 he described his astrology as allegorical rather than dogmatic.

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, thirty years younger and part of the same Florentine circle, wrote the sharpest attack of the age, the *Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem*, published in part in Bologna after his death in 1494. He condemned the determinism of the astrologers of his day, chiefly because it left no room for free will. Pico's nephew edited the manuscript, and may have made it harsher than Pico had.

Courts kept astronomers who also cast charts. [Tycho Brahe](/astrology/tycho-brahe/), as part of his duties to the Danish crown, presented an almanac at the start of each year and drew up the horoscope of each prince at birth. [Johannes Kepler](/astrology/johannes-kepler/) was imperial mathematician, and his first obligation was astrological advice to the emperor Rudolf II. He cast more than 800 nativities, wrote *On Giving Astrology Sounder Foundations* (1601), and in *Tertius Interveniens* (1610) called most of the astrologers' rules “evil-smelling dung”, in which an industrious hen might still find a grain, “even a pearl or a gold nugget”.

The great conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 1583, the last in the water signs before the fiery ones began, was widely expected to bring apocalyptic change. A papal bull against divination followed in 1586. When nothing happened by the feared year of 1603, public interest died away, the historian Keith Thomas writes.

Astrology kept its scholars a while longer. [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/) de Titis, who lived from 1603 to 1668, published his astrological works and tables in Italy in the middle of the century. And in London [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/), who had begun publishing popular astrological texts in 1644, in the middle of the Civil War, brought out *[Christian Astrology](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/)* in 1647, the first manual of its kind printed in English rather than Latin. By 1659 his annual almanac sold around 30,000 copies a year.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

My house system, for one. The method that carries Placidus's name was not his invention: Giovanni Antonio Magini had published tables of it in 1602, and it may be older still. It is now the most commonly used house system in modern tropical astrology. It's the one I use: it puts the angles on the cusps of the 1st, 4th, 7th and 10th houses and lets the houses come out unequal.

New aspects. In *Harmonices Mundi* (Linz, 1619), Kepler added to Ptolemy's aspects the [quintile](/astrology/quintile/), the [biquintile](/astrology/biquintile/) and the [sesquiquadrate](/astrology/sesquiquadrate/), and I read all three.

Horary astrology in plain English. The second book of *Christian Astrology* teaches how to answer a question from the chart of the moment it is asked, with worked examples ([horary astrology](/astrology/horary-astrology/)).

And an attitude I recognise. Kepler went looking for the grain in the dung; my own version was a notebook, a tick or a cross beside every placement, and I report what held less as well as what held more ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

Within a few decades of Lilly's book, astrology lost its place among the learned: [the decline after the 17th century](/astrology/decline-after-the-17th-century/). It had come to Florence by way of the medieval schools ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)).
