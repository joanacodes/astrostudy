Acumen is Ptolemy's Cluster, Messier 7, the bright, scattered cluster beside the Scorpion's sting, and of the clusters astrology lists around the sting, the one Ptolemy wrote down. In 2026 its centre stands in the last degree of Sagittarius; around 2092 it will cross into Capricorn.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Acumen is Messier 7, catalogued as NGC 6475: an open cluster about 980 light-years away and about 200 million years old, easy to see with the naked eye near the Scorpion's sting. It spreads over about 80′ of sky, more than a degree and some two and a half times the width of the full Moon, and its stars together shine at magnitude 3.3. Its brightest single star is only magnitude 5.6, but over a thousand stars probably belong to it, scattered across several degrees. It is the southernmost object in Messier's list.

The cluster's centre stood at about 28°42′ [Sagittarius](/astrology/sagittarius/) in 2000 and stands at about 29°04′ in 2026. At a degree every 72 years, the centre will cross into [Capricorn](/astrology/capricorn/) around 2092, and until then every chart puts it in Sagittarius ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)). At 11.4° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), it stays outside the band the planets travel.

Its neighbour [Aculeus](/astrology/aculeus/), the Butterfly Cluster, lies about three degrees back along the zodiac; Acumen is the larger and brighter of the two.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Ptolemy recorded it around AD 130 as a nebula, a cloudy patch of light, and the cluster is often named after him. Giovanni Battista Hodierna, before 1654, counted 30 stars in it; Charles Messier made it the seventh object of his 1764 list of comet-like objects, and John Herschel described it as "coarsely scattered clusters of stars". In Chinese astronomy it is Yú, the Fish.

Ptolemy wrote down this faint cloud, yet [Mira](/astrology/mira/), which at its brightest outshines it, has no place in his catalogue at all.

Its astrological name, Acumen, comes from the Latin root for sharp and pointed, the root of acute. The cluster also left its mark on a star: [Lesath](/astrology/lesath/), in the sting, owes its name to a slip, since its older name, Alascha, came from the Arabic al-laṭkha, the foggy patch, which meant this cluster.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Acumen's birthdays are the last of Sagittarius: about 20 and 21 December, a day earlier in some years, on the eve of the solstice ([Sun in Sagittarius](/astrology/sun-in-sagittarius/)).

In 1988, the year Saturn and Uranus met, both were within a degree of Acumen together three times, in the winter, in early summer and in the autumn, and their June meeting fell within about a quarter of a degree of the cluster's centre ([Saturn conjunct Uranus](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-uranus/)). Neptune had crossed its degree in 1983 and 1984, and Pluto followed in 2007 and 2008.

Acumen sits outside what I read: my system computes no stars or clusters, so I describe it here and leave its meaning to the traditions that use it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). Acumen's stars reach about two-thirds of a degree either side of its centre, so the one-degree window measured from the middle barely extends past the cluster's edge ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)).
