Stars rise every day, but for a few weeks each year a star rises in daylight, drowned by the Sun. The morning it can be seen again, a pale point low in the east just before dawn, is its heliacal rising. Calendars were built on that morning long before horoscopes were.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Against the background of the stars, the Sun drifts eastward by about a degree a day, a full circle in a year. A star the Sun is catching up with sets earlier each evening until its last sighting, low in the west after sunset: its heliacal setting. For some weeks it is up only in daylight. Then, once the Sun has moved far enough past it, the star clears the eastern horizon early enough before sunrise to be seen. That first sighting is the heliacal rising. From then on it rises a little earlier each day and stays visible longer before dawn.

The same star returns to the dawn about a year later, a sidereal year for stars near the ecliptic. The date drifts all the same: [precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/) carries a star's heliacal rising slowly through the calendar, all the way round in about 26,000 years. Latitude moves it too, and so does the weather, since haze can hide the first appearance for a morning or two.

Planets have heliacal risings as well. Each one follows a conjunction with the Sun: the planet passes the Sun, is lost in its light, then reappears in the morning sky. For Venus and Mercury, which never stray far from the Sun, it is the return of the morning star.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Sumerians, Babylonians, Egyptians and Greeks all timed farm work by the heliacal risings of particular stars. In Egypt the star was [Sirius](/astrology/sirius/). Its return to the dawn sky came close to the start of the Nile's yearly flood, and the 365-day civil calendar seems to have been set up when its New Year, Wep Renpet, fell on that return. With no leap years, the calendar slipped against the star by a day every four years or so; the records of that slippage led to the Sothic cycle and, in time, to the Julian and Alexandrian calendars. At the latitude of Cairo the rising has long fallen around 19 July in the Julian calendar, which in our time is 1 August.

In Greece, the dawn rising of the Pleiades opened the sailing season, and Hesiod used it in his Works and Days to mark the farmer's year ([Alcyone and the Pleiades](/astrology/alcyone-and-the-pleiades/)). In New Zealand the Māori call the Pleiades Matariki, and their heliacal rising, around June, begins the new year.

Astrology took the habit from Egypt. The Egyptians told the hours of the night by 36 decan stars, one for each ten degrees of the circle, whose heliacal risings also marked the ten-day weeks of their calendar ([The Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)). Manilius, in the 1st century AD, wrote of the stars that rise and set together with particular degrees of the zodiac, the ancestors of today's [parans](/astrology/parans/). Star calendars needn't follow the Sun, either: the [28 lunar mansions](/astrology/28-lunar-mansions/) mark the Moon's path instead, about one station for each day of its circuit.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

For the fixed stars, this stays history and astronomy: the readings I prepare don't include them, so a star's heliacal rising in your birth year is something I can describe, not something I calculate ([Fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)).

Planets are another matter, because their distance from the Sun is in every chart. When a planet has just passed the Sun and is pulling clear of its glare, I read the function it stands for as coming into view: newly at work, about to be seen. The heliacal rising is the day that becomes visible in the real sky; the chart only shows the approach ([Under the beams](/astrology/under-the-beams/)).

Whether the first sighting happened on the morning you were born depends on the latitude, the horizon and the weather, and no chart records the weather. My notebook didn't measure any of this: it ticked planets by sign and house ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A heliacal rising is the day a star rises.”** Stars rise every day, for part of the year in daylight. The heliacal rising is the first morning a star can be seen doing it, just before dawn.
- **“It falls on the same date everywhere.”** Latitude moves it: Sirius was seen returning eight to ten days later on Egypt's Mediterranean coast than at Aswan.
- **“The date never changes.”** Precession carries every star's heliacal rising through the year, a full circuit in about 26,000 years.
- **“Only stars have one.”** Planets do too, each time they come out of a conjunction with the Sun.
