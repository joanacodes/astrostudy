If Ptolemy's book tells you why astrology was thought to work, Vettius Valens' tells you what astrologers did with a chart in the second century. He wrote in Greek, and in his pages older astrologers survive at second hand.

## Who {#who}

Very little is known of his life beyond what his book lets slip. His modern editors call him Valens of Antioch, and he lived and worked in Alexandria at roughly the same time as [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/); historians have read the two side by side ever since.

His work is the *[Anthology](/astrology/vettius-valens-anthology/)*, and even its size depends on who is counting. David Pingree's critical edition of the Greek, published in Leipzig in 1986, is titled *Anthologiarum libri novem*, nine books; other accounts describe ten. It reached us through three manuscripts, all copied in 1300 or later, more than a thousand years after he wrote.

He handed on what older astrologers had taught. David Pingree concluded that Valens drew on Teucer of Babylon, an astrologer known for the constellations said to rise with each part of the zodiac; the Babylon in his name was probably the fortress of that name in Egypt, near modern Cairo, and no complete work of his survives. Valens also traced the art to a founder: he claimed that the Egyptian and Phoenician knowledge of the stars came from Abraham. Origin stories of that kind were common, and they tell you more about the authority an astrologer wanted than about where the art began.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He counted houses by degree. The third book of the *Anthology* contains the earliest known quadrant method: the arc from each angle to the next is cut into three equal parts, one house each. The method was later attributed to the philosopher Porphyry, and it still carries his name ([Porphyry houses](/astrology/porphyry/)). The scholar Martin Gansten has looked closely at how Valens actually worked. He often placed a planet's house by its sign alone, as a first approximation, much as [Whole Sign houses](/astrology/whole-sign-houses/) do, while holding throughout that the calculation by degree was the more accurate.

On that principle, Valens and I agree. I read houses in [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/), a quadrant system, so a planet's house depends on degrees, and degrees on the minute of birth. A chart drawn for noon because the time is missing can move a planet across a cusp, which is why I start from the birth time or not at all ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

He paid attention to single degrees. Valens, like Paulus Alexandrinus after him, worked with the *monomoiria*, the individual degrees of the zodiac taken one at a time. Much later, [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) gave each of the 360 degrees an image of its own, the Sabian symbols; attention to the single degree is far older than he was.

He described signs through the people born under them. Ptolemy explains what makes Cancer a tropical sign; Valens simply lists “tropical” as one of fourteen terms describing the sign, then sets out the character of those “so born”, changeable among them ([Cancer](/astrology/cancer/)). It's a habit astrology has never lost, and one I hold lightly. In my notebook the Sun sign and the rising sign, the placements everyone reads first, held about a third of the time ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

He also kept the lots, points measured from the Ascendant by the distance between two others. They were at the core of Hellenistic technique, and Ptolemy had no patience for them. The best known is the [Part of Fortune](/astrology/part-of-fortune/), by day the Ascendant plus the Moon minus the Sun, which moves with the Ascendant and so with the minute of birth.

In the century before Valens, the same art had been put into verse by [Dorotheus of Sidon](/astrology/dorotheus-of-sidon/). The tradition all three belong to is [Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/).
