The micromoon gets fewer headlines than its opposite, but it comes round just as often: a full moon at the far end of the Moon’s orbit, a little smaller and a little dimmer than usual, though you’d hardly notice without a photograph to compare.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Moon’s distance from Earth swings through the month between perigee, about 363,300 km on average, and apogee, about 405,500 km, by NASA’s figures. A micromoon is a full or new moon that falls near apogee: the counterpart of the [supermoon](/astrology/supermoon/), which falls near perigee. Neither name has an official definition, and each source picks its own margin, so lists of micromoons for the same year can differ.

Like the [blue moon](/astrology/blue-moon/), it names a particular full moon rather than a different kind of Moon. The phase is the same, the sign is the same as it would be at any distance, and only the size and brightness change, and only a little.

There is also a link with a point astrologers read. The far end of the Moon’s orbit is what they call Lilith, and at a micromoon the Moon itself is close to that month’s farthest point. But the Lilith I read is mean Lilith, the far point averaged out, which can sit tens of degrees from where the Moon actually reaches its farthest; [Which Lilith is mine?](/astrology/which-lilith-is-mine/) explains the difference. A micromoon doesn’t fall on your mean Lilith just because it happens at apogee.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The farthest full moon of the cycle comes round on the same clock as the nearest: about every 14 lunations, with the two roughly seven lunations apart. So if one year’s nearest full moon falls in winter, the farthest tends to fall about half a year later. Compared side by side, the nearest full moon is up to about a seventh wider than the farthest and sends up to nearly a third more light, by NASA’s comparison.

Where distance really shows is at a new moon. Seen from Earth, the Moon and the Sun look almost exactly the same size, so when the Moon is far enough away, its disc is too small to cover the Sun entirely, and a central eclipse leaves a ring of sunlight round it: an annular eclipse. In the dates I use, four of the solar eclipses between 2026 and 2030 are annular: 17 February 2026, 6 February 2027 and 26 January 2028, all in Aquarius, and 1 June 2030, in Gemini. A total eclipse needs the Moon near enough to hide the whole Sun ([Solar eclipses](/astrology/solar-eclipses/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read a micromoon as the full or new moon it is: by its sign, and only when it falls within a degree of a point in your chart that something slower is already working on. Distance isn’t part of that rule, so a smaller full moon doesn’t count for less, and a birth at one isn’t read differently from any birth at a full or new moon.

Even with eclipses, distance changes the look more than the weight. The system I built for my readings gives a penumbral lunar eclipse less weight than a total, partial or annular one, and an annular solar eclipse counts as fully as a total one: within about 3° of a natal point, at either end of its axis, read through that point and its house. The house, as always, needs your birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

So as weather, a micromoon is simply a slightly smaller full moon, and a good reminder that the Moon’s distance changes all the time.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A micromoon is a weak full moon.”** Its light is a little less; its meaning in a chart is the same as any full moon’s.
- **“You’ll notice how small it is.”** On one evening, without a comparison, almost nobody does. The difference shows in photographs taken months apart.
- **“An annular eclipse is a partial one.”** In an annular eclipse the Moon crosses the Sun centrally but is too small to cover it, leaving a ring; in a partial one it covers only part of the Sun, off-centre.
- **“A micromoon falls on Lilith.”** The Moon is near its actual farthest point, but mean Lilith, the averaged one, can be tens of degrees away. Check the degree before reading anything into it.
