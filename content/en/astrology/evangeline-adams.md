Evangeline Adams made astrology a paying profession in America, with a thriving practice in New York City and, by the late 1920s, a newsletter with thousands of subscribers. She was arrested for it three times, and no case against her succeeded.

## Who {#who}

She was born on 8 February 1868 in Jersey City, New Jersey, to George and Harriet Adams, née Smith. She built her consulting practice in New York City and wrote popular books on astrology: *The Bowl of Heaven*, her autobiography, in 1926, *Astrology: Your Place in the Sun* in 1927 and *Astrology: Your Place Among the Stars* in 1930. David Bromley has called her “America's first astrological superstar”.

The occultist Aleister Crowley, newly arrived in New York in late 1914, earned part of his living doing freelance work for her. On 12 April 1923 she married George Edwin Jordan in New York; a newspaper headline a few days earlier announced “Stars Make Match; Even Pick Wedding Day”. She died on 10 November 1932.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

She won astrology a hearing in court. Practising astrology was not legal in New York at the time, and Adams was arrested for fortune-telling in 1911, 1914 and 1923, yet none of the cases succeeded. At the trial of May 1914, after she described the character of the judge's son from his birth data, the judge acquitted her of all wrongdoing and praised her skill. It is a reading I wouldn't give. Three details, a date, a time and a place, open a whole life to whoever holds them, so I read the chart of someone who has agreed to it, not of a person who isn't in the room ([synastry and consent](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)).

She also shows where prediction for money leads. Adams had a reputation for market forecasts, and in the run-up to the crash of 1929, thousands of subscribers to her newsletter followed her advice to invest in stocks. Carol Krismann sums up the sceptics: Adams had no knowledge of economics, her predictions were always fuzzy, “foretelling disaster but not specific disasters”, and she said the market would go up at a time when it was in a period of remarkable growth anyway. Believers, Krismann adds, “often forgot the erroneous predictions and used the ones that happened to come true”, and Kenneth Fisher, an investment analyst, makes the same point: her few hits were publicised while her misses were ignored. That habit of the mind has a name ([confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/)), and it's the reason my own count of what held keeps every miss beside every hit.

A forecast that the market will rise, made while it is rising, tells you nothing, and investment is one subject I won't read a chart for at all ([Can astrology pick investments?](/astrology/can-astrology-pick-investments/); [financial astrology, in history](/astrology/financial-astrology-in-history/)).

She chose her moments, too. If the newspaper was right, she let the stars pick her own wedding day, in the year of her third arrest: [electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/), the choosing of dates, which I leave to others. A wedding is a beginning two people make; I'd rather read how each of them loves than tell them which morning to marry.

What lasted was the profession itself. Her fame, her courtroom win and her books showed that an astrologer could work in the open in an American city, with a public that bought books on the subject. Many of the questions she answered, from markets to wedding days, are ones I've learned to decline ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

Her trials ran alongside [Alan Leo](/astrology/alan-leo/)'s in London ([the Theosophical revival](/astrology/theosophical-revival/)). In the same decades, [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) took American astrology somewhere quieter: study groups, symbols and the shapes of whole charts.
