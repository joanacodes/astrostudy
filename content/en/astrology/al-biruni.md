For a time al-Biruni earned his place at court as an astrologer, and he wrote a textbook of the art in two languages. He also told his readers which part of it he couldn't defend, and that honesty is why he matters to me.

## Who {#who}

He was born around 973 in an outlying district of Kath, the capital of Khwarazm; his name comes from the Persian word for “outskirts”. His mother tongue was Khwarezmian, an Eastern Iranian language, and he spent his first twenty-five years in Khwarazm studying jurisprudence, theology, grammar, mathematics, astronomy, medicine and philosophy. When the dynasty he favoured fell in 995, he moved between the courts of Bukhara, Tabaristan and Gurganj, and corresponded with Avicenna; their exchange survives.

In 1017 Mahmud of Ghazni took Kath, and al-Biruni was brought to Ghazni with other scholars, possibly as a prisoner. He was made court astrologer, and from that year, at forty-four, he followed Mahmud's campaigns into India. The book he finished around 1030, the *Tārīkh al-Hind*, is the work his fame as a scholar of India rests on. In it he promised to “place before the reader the theories of the Hindus exactly as they are”, beside similar theories of the Greeks, and its translator Eduard Sachau called it “a magic island of quiet, impartial research in the midst of a world of clashing swords, burning towns, and plundered temples”.

Of the 146 books he wrote, 95 were on astronomy, mathematics and related subjects. His own list of his works up to 1036 has a category for “astrological aspects and transits” and another for astrology. He died at Ghazni, around 1050.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He taught astrology from the ground up. His *Book of Instruction in the Elements of the Art of Astrology*, the *Kitab al-tafhim*, is a book he wrote in Persian as well as Arabic, and it counts among the most important early works of science in Persian. It begins with geometry, goes on to arithmetic and the structure of the universe, and comes to judicial astrology only at the end, on the principle that nobody deserves the name of astrologer without the sciences before it.

He taught the forecasting of the last chapter and criticised it in the same book, and later wrote a refutation of astrology, set against an astronomy he supported wholeheartedly. That is close to the position I'd want from any teacher: here is the method, and here is where it's weak. Mine is set out in [How I read](/how-i-read/): I read a chart for tendencies, and the [transits](/astrology/transits/) of a period as weather, not a forecast.

He checked his authorities. In his great astronomical work, the *Mas'ud Canon*, he observed that the Sun's apogee moves, where Ptolemy had held it fixed. And he cared about the thing every chart depends on, an exact date: he worked out a method for converting dates of the Hindu calendar into the Greek, Arab and Persian calendars used in his day.

He carried India into Arabic. His book on India includes an extensive commentary on Indian astronomy, much of it a translation of Aryabhata's work, set beside the Greek ideas he knew ([Vedic astrology (Jyotish)](/astrology/vedic-astrology-jyotish/)).

He left evidence about a house system. The method later named after Campanus of Novara was used in the East before his time, and al-Biruni's writings are part of the evidence ([Campanus](/astrology/campanus/)). I use [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/) myself; Campanus is one of the systems I describe rather than dismiss ([Which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/)).

He came after the great teachers of Baghdad, [Abu Ma’shar](/astrology/abu-mashar/) among them, and stands with the philosophers who doubted them in [Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/). In thirteenth-century Italy, [Guido Bonatti](/astrology/guido-bonatti/) would build a Latin textbook on the Arabic astrologers, and defend the art in public debate.
