In 1985 the journal *Nature* published a test of natal astrology that astrologers had helped to design. It is one of the best-known tests of astrology, so here is what it asked, who took part, and what came out.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

No better than chance. The astrologers in Carlson's test could not match birth charts to the right personality profiles more often than guessing would, and when they felt sure of a match they were no more often right. They had agreed, before it began, that the test was fair.

Carlson concluded that the result “clearly refutes the astrological hypothesis”. I'd rather say that plainly than soften it. It is what the test found.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**Who designed it.** Shawn Carlson carried out the test while he was still an undergraduate. Its protocol was agreed before the experiment by a group of physicists and a group of astrologers, the astrologers nominated by the National Council for Geocosmic Research. They advised the experimenters, helped make sure the test was fair, and helped write down the central proposition of natal astrology that would be put to the test. They also chose 26 of the 28 astrologers who took part; the other two volunteered afterwards. The astrologers came from Europe and the United States.

**What they were asked to do.** Match more than a hundred natal charts to personality profiles produced by the California Psychological Inventory, or CPI, a standard and well-accepted personality questionnaire. The astrologers had picked the CPI themselves, as the instrument closest to the kind of information they believed a chart could give. The test was double-blind. That means neither the people being tested nor the people running the test know which answer is the right one, so nobody can steer the result, even without meaning to.

**What came out.** The study was published in *Nature* in 1985. The astrologers did no better than chance at matching charts to profiles. Their confidence didn't help: when they were sure they had found the right match, they were no more likely to be right. Carlson's conclusion was the sentence quoted above.

**What sits beside it.** Carlson's was not the only test of its kind. In 2003, Geoffrey Dean and Ivan Kelly reported a meta-analysis pooling 40 studies, with 700 astrologers and more than 1,000 birth charts. In ten of them, astrologers had to pick the right interpretation of a chart from among several; once the date and other obvious clues were removed, no significant result pointed to the right one. Dean and Kelly also studied people born at almost the same moment, a question with its own page: [Time twins: Dean and Kelly](/astrology/time-twins-dean-and-kelly/).

What I take from this is narrow, and I think it's fair. The claim being tested was one the astrologers had helped to word, and it did not hold under conditions they had accepted. That's a result to sit with, not to argue away.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My notebook is not a test like Carlson's, and I won't put it on the same shelf. I went through my own chart placement by placement, Sun, Moon, rising, each planet in its sign and house, and put a tick or a cross beside each one. Then I did the same, with their consent, for family, friends and strangers.

Overall, about a third held: 30 to 40%. The Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury, the placements everyone reads first, are among those. Saturn, Lilith and Chiron were different: they held 75 to 90% of the time.

It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general. It doesn't answer Carlson's result, and it isn't meant to. What it does is decide how I work. When something in a reading rests on a placement that held less often in my tests, I say so, and you can weigh it for yourself. That's the whole of [how I read](/how-i-read/), and the count itself is in [my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).
