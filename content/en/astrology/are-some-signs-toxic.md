Search for any sign alongside the word toxic and you'll find a list: the sign that ghosts, the sign that lies, the sign to run from. If your own sign is on it, it can sting, and if someone you love is on it, it can worry you. So let me be plain from the start.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

No sign is toxic. A sign describes a style, never a verdict on someone's character, and the research I know of gives no support for sorting people by their Sun. Sun-sign stereotypes aren't facts: they're simplifications, repeated until they sound like knowledge.

When a relationship really is harmful, the harm is in behaviour, in how someone treats you, again and again, and that belongs to the person, not to their birthday. If that's why you're asking, the question to take seriously isn't their sign. Please talk to someone you trust, and [When to see a professional instead](/astrology/when-to-see-a-professional-instead/) is a better door than any chart.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**Signs and marriage.** If some signs really were bad news for others, it ought to show in who marries whom. The sociologist David Voas looked for a link between star signs and marriage in the census records of more than 20 million people in England and Wales, and found no effect. [Sun-sign compatibility, and why it isn't enough](/astrology/sun-sign-compatibility-and-why-it-isnt-enough/) looks at what compatibility can mean instead.

**How stereotypes stick.** Numerous studies have found that people who know astrology tend to describe their own personality in terms of the traits their Sun sign is said to have, and more strongly when they know the study is about astrology. People who aren't familiar with astrology show no such tendency. Psychologists call it self-attribution, and it points to the descriptions, not the date of birth, as the source of the match. [Sun signs and personality: the studies](/astrology/sun-signs-and-personality-the-studies/) goes through them, and [Astrology versus personality tests](/astrology/astrology-versus-personality-tests/) looks at the questionnaires psychologists use instead.

**What a label can do.** Beliefs about a birth sign can change lives. In Japan, many believed that women born in a Fire Horse year would be unmarriageable and bring bad luck to their father or husband. In 1966, a Fire Horse year, the number of babies born in Japan fell by more than a quarter, as parents tried to avoid the stigma of having a daughter born then. That's a different astrology from mine, but the lesson carries: call a sign dangerous, and real people pay for it.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

In my notebook, the Sun sign held about a third of the time. It was one of the placements everyone reads first, with the rising sign, Venus and Mercury, and overall **30 to 40%** of what I checked held. That's why I hold sun-sign descriptions lightly, and why I'd never judge a person by one, any more than I'd blame a planet for a bad week ([Does Mercury retrograde break things?](/astrology/does-mercury-retrograde-break-things/)).

What held far more often, **Saturn, Lilith and Chiron, 75 to 90% of the time**, aren't villains either. They show where a person carries their hardest lesson, the wound they keep circling, the part of them they were told to hide. Between two people, in [synastry](/synastry/), they're where you can hurt each other without meaning to, and where, once you know it, you can help each other heal. That's the opposite of a toxic label: it's a reason for care.

It's a notebook, not a study; it proves nothing about astrology in general. But it's why I'll say this as plainly as I can: "You're a Scorpio, so…" is not a reading. It's an excuse with a vocabulary. A chart describes tendencies, never permissions, and nobody is the villain of a chart. [How I read](/how-i-read/) has the rest.
