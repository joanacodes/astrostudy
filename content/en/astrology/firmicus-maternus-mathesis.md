[Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/) wrote the *Mathesis* in the mid-330s, in Latin, at the urging of Lollianus Mavortius, a senior official of Constantine's empire, and dedicated it to him. It runs to eight books, and it is the fullest account of astrology the Roman world has left us: the Greek horoscopic tradition, set down in the language the West would go on reading.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Its full title is *Matheseos libri VIII*, eight books of astrology, and internal evidence dates it to around 334 to 337, the last years of Constantine's reign. Its dedicatee carried Mars in his name: *Mavortius* invokes Mavors, an old Latin name of Mars. Lollianus was a pagan, and his career ran from the governorship of Campania to the consulship of 355.

It had no successor of its size in Latin for a very long time. When Latin readers next had astrology on that scale, in the twelfth century, it came out of Arabic: [Abu Ma’shar’s introductions](/astrology/abu-mashars-introductions/) were put into Latin in 1133 and again in 1140 ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)).

Printing brought the *Mathesis* back in stages. Long excerpts from books three to five first appeared in 1488 at Augsburg, inside Johannes Engel's *Astrolabium planum*; a complete edition followed at Venice in 1497; and in 1499 Aldus Manutius printed it again with text that had been missing, from a manuscript Francesco Negri had found. The modern Latin text is the Teubner edition of W. Kroll, F. Skutsch and K. Ziegler, published between 1897 and 1913. English readers have Jean Rhys Bram's translation of 1975 and James Herschel Holden's of 2011, and in 1831 the English Platonist Thomas Taylor had already put one passage into English: the one on the Thema Mundi.

## What it says {#what-it-says}

Firmicus is open about some debts and silent about one. He names Dorotheus and tells us he came from Sidon ([Dorotheus’ Carmen Astrologicum](/astrology/dorotheus-carmen-astrologicum/)), and among the Romans who had written on astrology he lists only Germanicus, Cicero and Fronto. He traces the art itself to Egypt, to King Nechepso and his priest Petosiris, to whom he says it was handed down. Manilius, the poet of the *Astronomica*, is never mentioned, yet scholars find that Firmicus follows his method of teaching closely and almost certainly used him, or someone inspired by him, as a guide.

The passage Taylor translated gives the [Thema Mundi](/astrology/thema-mundi/), the positions of the stars at the beginning of the world's great periods: a symbolic chart of the world's birth, not a moment anyone could have observed. The charts I read are the opposite: a real moment in a real place, with its time to the minute ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

For the finer divisions of the zodiac, Firmicus follows the Egyptian terms, the five unequal stretches of each sign given to the five planets, and holds that a planet in its own terms is as strong as one in its own sign ([Terms (bounds)](/astrology/terms-bounds/)). Book four sets out the decans, three to a sign, each with its planet ([The Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)).

Book seven opens on a call for secrecy, recalling the oaths of silence required by Orpheus, Pythagoras, Plato and Porphyry. Several ancient astrologers guarded their art in that way ([Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/)). My own count went the other way. I tested my chart placement by placement, a tick or a cross beside each, then other people's with their consent, and published how I counted ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general; but a method set out in the open can at least be argued with.
