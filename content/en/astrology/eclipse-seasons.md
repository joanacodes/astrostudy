Eclipses don't arrive one at a time, scattered through the year. They come in clusters of two or three, a fortnight apart, in two windows about six months apart: the eclipse seasons.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

An eclipse needs the Sun, the Moon and the Earth in line, and that only happens near the Moon's nodes, where its tilted path crosses the Sun's. Fred Espenak, in his eclipse pages for NASA, describes a zone about 34° wide centred on each node: the Sun takes about 34½ days to cross it, and that window is an eclipse season. Any new moon inside it is a [solar eclipse](/astrology/solar-eclipses/), any full moon a [lunar eclipse](/astrology/lunar-eclipses/).

Since the Moon takes about 29½ days from one new moon to the next, a window of 34½ days always catches at least one new moon and one full moon, about two weeks apart, and now and then a third. In the eclipse file this site keeps, from 2000 to 2040, there are 87 seasons: 75 with two eclipses and 12 with three.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The nodes slide slowly backwards through the zodiac, so the Sun meets each node again a little before a full year has passed. Espenak gives the mean gap between the middles of two seasons as 173.3 days, a little under half a year. Two seasons make about 346½ days, so each year's pair arrives about 19 days earlier than the year before. You can watch it in the list: February and August in 2026, then February and July 2027, January and July 2028, then December and June. After about 18.6 years, the time the nodes take to go round the zodiac, the seasons are back on the same dates.

A calendar year holds four to seven eclipses. In the file, every year from 2000 to 2040 has at least four, and 2038 has seven, because it catches the edges of three seasons. So does 2029, with eclipses in January, June, July and December.

And the seasons keep to a pair of signs for a while. While the nodes spend about 18 months crossing a pair of opposite signs, the eclipses fall on or near that axis: Leo and Aquarius in 2026 and 2027, Cancer and Capricorn in 2028 and 2029, Gemini and Sagittarius by 2030. [Why eclipses follow the nodes](/astrology/why-eclipses-follow-the-nodes/) tells that part of the story.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

An eclipse season is a window in the sky, not in your life. What matters for a chart is whether one of its eclipses lands within about three degrees of a natal planet or angle, at either end of its axis. If none does, the season passes over your chart like weather over someone else's town. If one does, I read that eclipse through the natal point it reaches and that point's house, as emphasis on what the point describes, a penumbral one counting for less, and never as an omen.

A season can also set up what follows. In the rule I follow for lunations, a new or full moon only counts as a trigger inside a window something slower has opened, and an eclipse on one of your planets is one of the things that opens such a window. The next new or full moon within a degree of the same point can then bring it into focus.

Because the eclipses keep to one pair of signs for a year or two, they also tend to keep to one pair of opposite houses in your chart, the same way the transiting [nodal axis](/astrology/nodal-axis/) does. Which pair needs your birth time; that's [how I read](/how-i-read/): your chart first, the sky second.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Eclipse season is a dangerous time for everyone.”** It's geometry: the Sun close to a node. An eclipse only counts where it touches a chart, and never as an omen.
- **“Whatever happens during eclipse season was fated.”** An eclipse marks emphasis on part of a chart. It doesn't decide events, and nothing in it is fixed.
- **“There's one eclipse season a year.”** There are two, about six months apart, and some years catch the edges of three.
- **“Eclipse seasons always fall in the same months.”** They come about 19 days earlier each year and go round the calendar in about 18.6 years.
