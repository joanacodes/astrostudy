Carl Jung was a psychiatrist, and astrology was one of many subjects he studied while mapping the mind. Three of his ideas, the archetype, the collective unconscious and synchronicity, became the working vocabulary of a whole school of astrologers.

## Who {#who}

He was born on 26 July 1875 at Kesswil, in the Swiss canton of Thurgau. He worked as a research scientist at the Burghölzli psychiatric hospital in Zurich, under Eugen Bleuler, and his word association test made his name early. Sigmund Freud saw him as an heir: in 1910 he proposed Jung as president of the new International Psychoanalytical Association, and Jung was elected for a two-year term. Astrology was already part of his research: in a letter of 12 June 1911 he told Freud he was making horoscopic calculations, in search of a clue to the core of psychological truth. Their break followed Jung's *Psychology of the Unconscious* (1912), and from 1913 he built his own system, analytical psychology.

His books include *Psychological Types* (1921), where introversion and extraversion became the two attitudes of a theory of personality, *Psychology and Alchemy* (1944), *Aion* (1951) and the essay on synchronicity he published with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli in 1952. He ranged across anthropology, alchemy, Eastern and Western philosophy and astrology, and although many called him a mystic, he preferred to be seen as a man of science. He died at Küsnacht on 6 June 1961.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He moved the planets inside. His claim about astrology's symbols was psychological: the gods that astrologers placed in the sky were, at their origin, images of the collective unconscious projected outward. On that reading, a planet in a chart is a figure of the psyche before it is a body in space, and the twentieth century's psychological astrologers took him at his word ([Psychological astrology](/astrology/psychological-astrology/)). It's close to how I read the three placements that held best in my notebook, Saturn, Chiron and Lilith, at 75 to 90%: as a hard lesson, an old wound and a hidden part, met by a person in themselves ([The three that held: Saturn, Chiron and Lilith](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/)). The same openness is what critics fault in the school he inspired: a figure that fits almost any life invites confirmation bias, the tendency to see what confirms a belief and miss what doesn't ([Confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/)).

He offered a connection without a cause. Astrological configurations were, for Jung, one example of synchronicity: events that fall together in time and carry meaning, with no causal line between them. It let astrologers speak about the sky without claiming that Saturn pushes anyone, and it has a cost the critics point to, since a link of meaning can be neither measured nor refuted. He also put horoscopes to a statistical test, on hundreds of married couples, and accounts of what he found differ ([Jung's marriage experiment](/astrology/jungs-marriage-experiment/)).

He shaped the astrologers who came after him. [Dane Rudhyar](/astrology/dane-rudhyar/) learned astrology while reading Jung, and with his protégé Alexander Ruperti was among the first to join the two. A generation later [Liz Greene](/astrology/liz-greene/) and Stephen Arroyo, both strongly influenced by the Jungian model, gave psychological astrology its standing, and Greene returned to the source in *Jung's Studies in Astrology* (2018), a study of his own use of it.

Where I stop is where he started. Jung was a doctor of the mind, and a chart read in his language is still not a consultation with one. When what someone carries is psychological, the right door is a professional's, and I say so plainly ([When to see a professional instead](/astrology/when-to-see-a-professional-instead/); [How I read](/how-i-read/)).

A French psychologist took the counting much further. Jung looked for meaning in coincidences between two charts; [Michel Gauquelin](/astrology/michel-gauquelin/) counted births by the thousand, and found nothing in the signs.
