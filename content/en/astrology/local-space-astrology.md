A birth chart tells you where the planets were along the zodiac. It doesn't tell you which way to face to see them. Local space astrology does: it turns the chart into a compass, centred on the place you were born.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Astronomers describe a body's place in the local sky with two angles. Its azimuth is its direction around the horizon, counted from north through east: north 0°, east 90°, south 180°, west 270°. Its altitude is its height above the horizon, or its depth below it. Both belong to one place and one moment; a minute later, or a town away, they are different.

Local space astrology reads the birth chart in that frame. Each planet is placed at its bearing, with its height, rather than in a sign. It is one of the systems of astrological cartography, and the history behind it is German before it was American: it was developed by Friedrich Sieggrün, an early collaborator of [Alfred Witte](/astrology/alfred-witte/) in the [Hamburg School](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/), and later made popular in the United States by Steve Cozzi, author of *Planets in Locality* (1988), and Michael Erlewine, whose *Local Space: Relocation Astrology* appeared in 1981. Erlewine had founded Matrix Software in 1977 and was the first person to program astrology on microcomputers.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

You need the exact time and place of birth, and a program that gives each planet's azimuth and altitude. The local space chart is then a compass: the birthplace at the centre, the horizon around it, each planet on the bearing where it stood. As a mapping technique, local space carries those bearings out from the birthplace across a map.

The frame shows things the wheel hides:

- **Rising isn't simply east.** A planet on your [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) is rising, so it lies somewhere on the eastern half of the horizon, but its exact bearing depends on its [declination](/astrology/declination/). The Sun rises due east at the equinoxes and well north of east in June.
- **The Midheaven points south.** A planet on the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) stands on the meridian, which, seen from Britain or France, means due south.
- **Height matters.** The wheel shows only whether a planet was above or below the [horizon](/astrology/horizon-axis/); local space says how far.

Every bearing changes as the Earth turns, so the birth time decides all of them ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

Directions from one place. Where [astrocartography](/astrology/astrocartography/) maps, all over the world, the places where each planet stood on an angle at your birth minute, local space keeps you at the spot where you were born and asks in which direction each planet lay. Read the way astrocartography reads its lines, a planet's direction would be the way in which its themes come forward: Saturn's bearing for structure and responsibility, Venus's for what you value and are drawn to.

I don't draw local space charts, any more than astrocartography maps ([how I read](/how-i-read/)), and for the same reason: they tend to be used to choose where to live or to travel. A [relocation chart](/astrology/relocation-charts/) at least keeps the planets in their signs and aspects; a bearing keeps only a direction, and a direction is a thin reason to move a life.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

A bearing has no zodiac in it. The signs your planets occupy, and most of the aspects between them, play no part in the directions, so the chart describes where things were in the sky rather than how they relate.

It is also bound to one point on the ground. Every direction starts from the birthplace, and the picture changes with every mile and every minute. A time rounded to the hour turns the whole compass; a birthplace given only as a region blurs it. And a direction, however exact, can't promise anything waiting along it.
