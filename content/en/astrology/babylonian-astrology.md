Long before anyone cast a birth chart, scholars in Mesopotamia read the sky as a letter addressed to their king. Babylonian astrology is the oldest layer of the astrology I practise, and the place the twelve signs come from.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

It grew out of divination. Thirty-two clay models of sheep's livers, from about 1875 BC, are the oldest detailed divination texts known, and the sky omens that followed were written in the same pattern: if this is seen, that will follow. Historians usually date organised sky-reading from Old Babylonian texts of about 1800 BC; the scattered references said to be older don't add up to a system.

The gods showed themselves through the planets. Marduk, the god of Babylon, was the planet Jupiter, and a bad sign in a planet meant that its god was disturbed. The great reference work was *Enūma Anu Enlil*, 68 or 70 tablets depending on the version, holding between 6,500 and 7,000 omens on the Moon, the Sun, the weather, the stars and the planets, every one of them read for the king and the state. Historians date it differently: one account has it in use by the sixteenth century BC, another places its writing in the seventh.

Scholars drew on it for regular reports to the Assyrian kings, and well over 500 of these have been published. A typical one begins: “If the moon becomes visible on the first day: reliable speech; the land will be happy.” This was astrology for a country. Ulla Koch-Westenholz describes it as concerned almost wholly with the welfare of the state and its king; private lives had no place in it.

An omen was a warning rather than a sentence. Rituals existed to turn its harm aside, and eclipses were feared above all other signs. In one report to King Esarhaddon about a lunar eclipse, a scholar expecting a flood offers to cut through a dike himself, at night, so that the omen would be fulfilled by a harmless substitute. The sky was read as a set of signs, not as the cause of events.

The zodiac came late in the story. Around the end of the fifth century BC, Babylonian astronomers divided the Sun's yearly path into twelve equal signs of 30°, by analogy with twelve schematic months of 30 days. Modern calculations place this between 409 and 398 BC, under Persian rule. The signs were tied to the stars: Cancer began at the star called the Rear Twin, β Geminorum.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

First, the frame of every chart. The twelve signs of the [zodiac](/astrology/zodiac/) are those 30° divisions. What has changed is where they start. The Babylonian signs were measured from the stars, as the [sidereal zodiac](/astrology/sidereal-zodiac/) still is; the [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/) I use counts them from the March equinox, as Ptolemy explained it in the second century AD.

Second, the planets' characters. A tablet from the Seleucid era already sorts the planets into those of good nature and those of harmful nature, the ancestors of [benefics and malefics](/astrology/benefics-and-malefics/). The historian Francesca Rochberg-Halton lists what Greek astrology later shared with Babylon: the zodiac, the [trine](/astrology/trine/) and the planetary [exaltations](/astrology/exaltation/), the signs where a planet was thought to be at its height. From Marduk's planet to the [Jupiter](/astrology/jupiter/) of a reading is a long road, with no break in it.

Third, the habit of writing the sky down. The Babylonian astronomical diaries were kept so steadily that they showed the eighteen-year cycle of eclipses, the saros, and [lunar eclipses](/astrology/lunar-eclipses/) have kept some of the weight Babylon gave them. [Mundane astrology](/astrology/mundane-astrology/), the reading of nations and public events, is one of the oldest branches there is.

What I leave behind is the verdict. An omen told a king what would happen to his land. I read transits as weather, not a forecast, and I don't predict events at all: [how I read](/how-i-read/) explains why.
