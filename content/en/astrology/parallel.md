The chart wheel measures one direction only, along the zodiac. A parallel measures the other, north and south of the celestial equator, and can link planets the wheel shows as nowhere near each other.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Every planet has a [declination](/astrology/declination/), its distance north or south of the [celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/), the line the Earth's equator would draw across the sky. When two planets share a declination on the same side, both 12° north, say, or both 20° south, they are in parallel. Like two towns on the same line of latitude, they lie on the same parallel, however far apart they are round the sky.

Astrologers who use parallels read them like a [conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/): two functions joined and working together, each keeping its own nature. Not many astrologers do use them. The parallel and its pair, the [contra-parallel](/astrology/contra-parallel/), are the two aspects of declination, and most charts are read without either.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Declination and the zodiac are tied together by the Sun's path. A body sitting exactly on the ecliptic has a declination set by its zodiac degree: 0° at 0° Aries and 0° Libra, furthest north at 0° Cancer, furthest south at 0° Capricorn, about 23°26′ either way. Two degrees mirrored across the Cancer–Capricorn axis, such as 5° Gemini and 25° Cancer, share the same declination, here about 21°08′ north; tradition calls such a pair antiscia ([antiscia and contra-antiscia](/astrology/antiscia-and-contra-antiscia/)).

So for bodies on the ecliptic, a conjunction is always a parallel too, and so is a pair of antiscia. But the planets wander a little above and below the ecliptic, the Moon by about 5°, so a conjunction need not be a parallel, and a parallel can join planets whose signs make no major aspect at all, as Gemini and Cancer don't.

A planet [out of bounds](/astrology/out-of-bounds-planets/), past the Sun's furthest reach of about 23°26′, can't be parallel the Sun at all; its only possible partners are bodies that also stray near or beyond that line.

How close two declinations must be varies from one astrologer to another, as it does for every aspect ([orbs](/astrology/orbs/)). Most chart software lists each planet's declination in a column beside its zodiac position: look for two values that match, with the same direction, both north (+) or both south (−).

The [Moon](/astrology/moon/) changes declination fastest, reaching more than 28° north or south in some years, so its parallels can begin and end within a single day. For the Moon, the birth time decides ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

A parallel never changes a planet's sign, house or aspects; it adds a thread. Where I find one, I read the two planets as a pair, the way I'd read them in conjunction, but with a lighter hand: the aspects on the wheel come first, and a parallel confirms or adds to them. When it links two planets the wheel leaves apart, a Venus and a Saturn with no aspect between them, say, it can be the detail that explains why the two behave as one in someone's life.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A parallel is a conjunction.”** It is read like one, but measured on another scale: two planets can be parallel while signs apart, and conjunct without being parallel.
- **“Parallel planets sit side by side in the sky.”** They share a distance from the equator, not a place: one can be rising while the other sets.
- **“Planets in the same sign are parallel.”** On the ecliptic, two degrees of one sign never share a declination unless they are the same degree; off it, even a close conjunction can miss, the Moon's by several degrees.
