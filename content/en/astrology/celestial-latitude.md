Pluto's orbit is tilted about 17° to the Earth's, enough to carry it well outside the band of sky the ancients drew round the zodiac. It still has a sign in every chart, because a sign is measured along the Sun's path, and how far a body strays above or below that path is a separate number: its celestial latitude.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A position on the sky needs two coordinates, and astrology takes both from the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), the Sun's yearly path. One runs along it: [zodiacal longitude](/astrology/zodiacal-longitude/), which gives the sign and the degree. The other runs across it: celestial latitude, counted north (+) or south (−) from 0° on the ecliptic to 90° at the ecliptic poles. Astronomers write it β, beside λ for longitude.

It has a cousin measured from a different circle. [Declination](/astrology/declination/) is counted from the celestial equator and pairs with [right ascension](/astrology/right-ascension/); latitude is counted from the ecliptic and pairs with longitude. The two circles are tilted about 23.4° to each other, so the same planet has two different values, one in each frame.

How far a body can stray depends on the tilt of its orbit to the Earth's. The Sun never leaves the ecliptic, which is its own path. The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5°, and the planets' only a little: Mercury 7.0°, Venus 3.4°, Mars 1.9°, Jupiter 1.3°, Saturn 2.5°, Uranus 0.8°, Neptune 1.8°. So the zodiac is a narrow belt, about 8° either side of the ecliptic, and the Babylonians measured the planets against reference stars lying within about 9° of it. [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), at about 17°, can wander well out of the belt.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Moon shows latitude at work. Twice a month it crosses the ecliptic, heading north at the [North Node](/astrology/north-node/) and south at the South Node, and about a week after each crossing it reaches its furthest, some 5° from the line. A new or full moon is an eclipse only when it falls close to a node, with the Moon's latitude near zero; the rest of the time, the new moon passes above or below the Sun and the full moon above or below the Earth's shadow ([Why eclipses follow the nodes](/astrology/why-eclipses-follow-the-nodes/)).

Latitude also feeds declination. A body's distance from the celestial equator depends both on where it is along the ecliptic and on how far it has strayed from it, so a body with latitude can go further north or south than the Sun ever does. The Moon's 5° are why it goes [out of bounds](/astrology/out-of-bounds-planets/) so often in some years and hardly at all in others. In the figures I work from, its highest declination is 28.4° in 2026, then 27.7°, 26.6° and 25.2°, and only 23.6° in 2030, against the Sun's 23.44°: as the nodes travel round on their 18.6-year cycle, the Moon's tilt adds to the ecliptic's or works against it ([The Moon out of bounds](/astrology/moon-out-of-bounds/)).

And latitude decides whether one body can pass in front of another. The Moon reaches the degree of every planet about once a month, but it covers a planet or a star only when their latitudes match as well. That's why the wheel shows a conjunction with the Moon every month, and an occultation, the Moon actually hiding the planet, comes only now and then.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read latitude on its own. No planet changes sign or house because of it, since both come from longitude, and a planet 5° above the ecliptic is read in the same degree as one lying on it. Latitude reaches my readings only through what it does to the sky.

The first is eclipses. The eclipses I include in a forecast are new and full moons close enough to the nodes for the Moon's latitude to be near zero, and they count only within about three degrees of a natal point. The second is declination: when latitude carries the Moon beyond the Sun's furthest reach, the Moon is out of bounds, one of the few north–south facts I bring into a reading.

Everything else stays with longitude, the measure a chart is built on. Saying which coordinate a point rests on is part of the same honesty I try to keep everywhere else ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Celestial latitude is the latitude of your birthplace.”** That one is geographic: it goes into the program to work out your houses. Celestial latitude belongs to the planets.
- **“A planet outside the zodiac belt has no sign.”** Its sign comes from its longitude. Pluto can wander more than 8° from the ecliptic and keeps a sign all the same.
- **“Latitude can move a planet into another sign.”** Sign and degree come from longitude alone; latitude only says how far above or below the line the planet stood.
- **“Two planets in the same degree are lined up one behind the other.”** Only when their latitudes match too, which is rare enough to have its own name: an occultation.
