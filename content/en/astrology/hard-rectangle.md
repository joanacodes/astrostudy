Some pattern names describe a drawing before they describe a meaning. The hard rectangle is one of them: four planets whose lines make a rectangle, with hard aspects along its sides.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

Any rectangle drawn inside a circle has its diagonals running through the centre. On a chart wheel, that means four planets can only draw a rectangle if they make two [oppositions](/astrology/opposition/), each planet facing another across the chart. The two short sides then span the same arc, so do the two long ones, and a short side and a long side always add up to 180°.

The [mystic rectangle](/astrology/mystic-rectangle/) fills those sides with soft aspects, sextiles of 60° and trines of 120°. The hard rectangle's name asks for [hard aspects](/astrology/hard-and-soft-aspects/) instead, and the arithmetic leaves little choice. Squares on all four sides make a square, and that figure already has a name: the [grand cross](/astrology/grand-cross/), which the count behind this site finds for about 1 person in 80. Among the angles many astrologers call hard, the only other pair that adds up to 180° is the [semi-square](/astrology/semi-square/), 45°, with the [sesquiquadrate](/astrology/sesquiquadrate/), 135°: a long, narrow rectangle with two of each along its sides.

That is what the geometry allows. Whether the authors who use the name mean exactly that, I couldn't confirm in a source I could check, so I describe the drawing the name implies and leave the definition open. The [grand quintile](/astrology/grand-quintile/), its neighbour in this family, has the opposite fortune: its name fixes its whole geometry. And [Thor's hammer](/astrology/thors-hammer/) uses the same family of angles, a square with two sesquiquadrates, in a figure of three planets.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The count behind this site includes the mystic rectangle, about 1 person in 28, and the grand cross, about 1 in 80, because each has one rule that every chart can be tested against. The hard rectangle has none that I could check, so it isn't counted, and I won't guess at a share. If a program shows you one, look at the orbs it allows on the sides: the count's own rule keeps the sesquiquadrate within 3°, and programs differ.

In the system I built for my readings, an opposition relates two functions through polarity: two ends made aware of each other, to be integrated rather than fought. A semi-square adds a local, constructive tension that asks for small corrections; a sesquiquadrate, a compound tension that asks for something to be restructured. None of them means failure, crisis or punishment. So if those are the sides, I'd read a hard rectangle as two polarities whose ends keep nudging and pushing each other, asking for steady adjustment rather than one big decision. A figure is emphasis, not identity: its four planets come first, each in its sign, and the rectangle is the way they press on one another.

Two oppositions usually mean two axes of houses, four areas of life in two pairs, and only the time of birth places them; a Moon at one corner can come and go within the day. A reading without the houses would rest on half a chart, and that's why my rule is simple: [no birth time, no reading](/blog/unknown-birth-time/).

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **See what's actually on the sides.** Your [aspect grid](/astrology/aspect-grid/) lists each aspect and its orb; the label a program puts on the figure is only a label.
- **Start from the two oppositions.** They carry the questions. The sides describe how each end gets pushed.
- **Treat the sides as adjustments.** A semi-square asks for a small correction, often; a sesquiquadrate, now and then, for a rebuild.
- **Compare with the figures that have rules.** Squares all round: read the grand cross. Soft sides: the mystic rectangle.

Four planets in hard aspect make a lot of pressure to be born with, and also a frame that holds: each end has neighbours that won't let it settle into a rut. The work this figure asks for is adjustment, not victory.
