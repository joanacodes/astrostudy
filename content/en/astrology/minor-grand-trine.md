Not every triangle in a chart is grand, and this one is the more useful for it. Two planets in trine with a third tucked between them make a small, flat triangle that turns up in nearly half of all charts.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

Take two planets about 120° apart, in [trine](/astrology/trine/), and put a third roughly halfway between them, about 60° from each. The third planet makes a [sextile](/astrology/sextile/) to both ends, and the three draw a flat triangle along one side of the chart: one trine for its long side, two sextiles for its short ones. In signs, the two ends usually share an element and the middle planet sits in a complementary one, so the whole figure keeps to one polarity: planets in Aries and Leo with a third in Gemini, fire, fire and air.

It's called minor because it has one trine where the [grand trine](/astrology/grand-trine/) has three, and its shape is open where the grand trine is closed. It's also a building block. Two of them overlap inside the [cradle](/astrology/cradle/), and one sits inside every kite: a planet opposite the middle one would close the trine's two ends into a grand trine and turn the whole into a kite.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Nearly half the charts in the count behind this site hold one: about 4 people in 9 born from 1930 to 2009 have a minor grand trine among the ten planets, with each aspect allowed 8° when the Sun or Moon is involved and 6° otherwise. Add the Ascendant, the Midheaven, Chiron, Lilith or the nodes, or widen the orbs a little, and it would be more common still. It's one of the most ordinary figures a chart can hold.

That changes how I read it. A pattern this common says little on its own; it earns attention when it repeats something the rest of the chart already says, the same planets or the same houses coming up again and again. Patterns emerge from repetition, and a minor grand trine is often one strand of a larger repetition rather than the thing itself.

When it does stand out, I read it through its middle planet. The system I built for my readings describes the trine as flow that keeps each function itself, and the sextile as cooperation that has to be engaged before it becomes anything. In a minor grand trine, the two ends of the trine support each other easily, and the middle planet is offered a share of that ease by both, on the condition that it takes it up. With Mercury in the middle, the flow tends to become words and plans; with Mars, action; with Saturn, structure that lasts.

The middle planet's house shows where that happens, and it needs the time of birth, like the houses of the other two. A figure with the Moon in it can come and go within a day. Common as the figure is, I [wait for the time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) before reading it, because its working point lives in a house.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Work from the middle.** The planet between the other two is where the figure does its work. Start there.
- **Take up the sextiles.** They're offers. Nothing happens until you accept them, and when you do, the trine's ease has somewhere to land.
- **Notice the repetition.** If the same planets or houses come up elsewhere in the chart, the minor grand trine is part of a bigger story.
- **Don't inflate it.** Nearly half of charts have one. It's a resource, not a rank.

Its neighbour in this family, the [trapeze](/astrology/trapeze/), is a four-planet figure whose definition authors still disagree about; this one, at least, everyone draws the same way. A minor grand trine is a small, reliable kind of help: easy to overlook, and easy to use once you've seen it.
