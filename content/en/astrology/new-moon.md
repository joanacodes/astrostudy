The new moon is the one phase of the Moon that nobody sees. For a day or two the Moon rises and sets with the Sun, its dark side turned towards us, and you only know the month has begun again when a thin crescent appears in the evening sky.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A new moon is the moment the Moon passes the Sun: both at the same degree of the zodiac, the Moon’s sunlit side facing away from Earth. Astronomers time it to the minute, as the instant the two share the same longitude. It always falls in the Sun’s sign, at the Sun’s degree. The new moon of 9 November 2026, for instance, has the Sun and Moon together at 16°53′ Scorpio, a [New moon in Scorpio](/astrology/new-moon-in-scorpio/).

From there the Moon draws ahead, about 12° a day on average, and the cycle runs on through the [waxing crescent](/astrology/waxing-crescent/) to the first quarter and the full moon. Astrologers who use Dane Rudhyar’s eight phases, from *The Lunation Cycle* (1967), treat the new moon not as an instant but as the first of eight equal phases: the Moon 0° to 45° ahead of the Sun, about 3¾ days. A birth two days after the exact new moon, when a fine crescent may already show at dusk, still belongs to it.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Between 2026 and 2030, in the dates I use, the time from one new moon to the next runs from about 29¼ to about 29¾ days, 29½ on average. Twelve lunations fall about eleven days short of a year, so most years hold twelve new moons and some, like 2027 and 2030, thirteen. Each new moon lands about a sign further on than the last, following the Sun round the zodiac.

Most months the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun, because its path is tilted against the Sun’s. When a new moon falls close to the lunar nodes, where the two paths cross, the Moon covers part or all of the Sun: a solar eclipse. In the same five years there are twelve, two a year and four in 2029, beginning with an annular eclipse in Aquarius on 17 February 2026 and a total one in Leo on 12 August 2026. They have their own page, [Solar eclipses](/astrology/solar-eclipses/).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Born at a new moon, you have the Sun and Moon together in your chart: [Sun conjunct Moon](/astrology/sun-conjunct-moon/). The system I built for my readings treats a conjunction as two functions joined into one process, neither of them erased, so I read it as purpose and need starting from the same place. That can make for a single-mindedness that serves you well, and over the years it asks for one piece of work: telling what you want from what you need.

The birth time can matter here. The Moon gains about 12° a day on the Sun, so if you were born within a day or so of the exact new moon, the time decides whether your Moon was still just behind the Sun, at the end of the previous cycle, or already ahead of it ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)).

As weather, I treat a new moon as a marker, not a message. The rule I work with is strict: it counts only within a degree of a point in your chart, and only when something slower is already working on that point, a transit, a progression, a solar arc or an eclipse. Then it can bring that season into focus, briefly. On its own, a new moon is never a forecast, and the house it falls in needs your birth time.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“You can see the new moon.”** The new moon itself is invisible, lost in the Sun’s light. What you see a day or two later, low in the west at dusk, is the waxing crescent.
- **“Every new moon is an eclipse.”** Only those close to the nodes: two a year between 2026 and 2030, and four in 2029, out of twelve or thirteen new moons.
- **“A new moon falls in the sign opposite the Sun.”** That’s the full moon. A new moon is always in the Sun’s own sign: while the Sun is in Scorpio, so is the new moon.
- **“Wishes made at the new moon come true.”** Writing down what you want can help you see it clearly. The sky doesn’t read the list.
