Nostradamus is the name people reach for when they mean prediction. The man behind the name was an apothecary and physician who wrote almanacs, and who said in print that he was not a prophet.

## Who {#who}

Michel de Nostredame was born on 14 or 21 December 1503, the sources differ, in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, one of at least nine children of a notary. His father's family had been Jewish; his grandfather, a grain and money dealer in Avignon, had converted to Catholicism around 1459 or 1460 and taken the name Nostredame. Michel entered the University of Avignon at fourteen and had to leave after little more than a year, when plague closed it. In 1529 he enrolled in medicine at Montpellier and was expelled almost at once, when the student procurator found he had worked as an apothecary, a manual trade the statutes forbade; the record of his expulsion still exists. His first wife and their two children died in 1534. From 1545 he fought plague in Marseille, then in Salon-de-Provence and Aix, and in 1547 he settled in Salon, married a widow, Anne Ponsarde, and had six children.

He wrote an almanac for 1550, Latinising his name for the first time, and it sold so well that he wrote one or more every year after. Together they held at least 6,338 prophecies. *Les Prophéties* followed in 1555, quatrains in a deliberately obscure style of word games and mixed languages. Catherine de' Medici read his almanacs, summoned him to Paris and had him draw up horoscopes for her children; by his death she had made him counsellor and physician-in-ordinary to the young Charles IX. In 1561 he was briefly imprisoned for publishing his almanac for 1562 without a bishop's permission. He died at Salon on 1 or 2 July 1566.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He became the model of the public prophet without accepting the title. “Not that I would attribute to myself either the name or the role of a prophet,” he wrote in the preface to his son César, and he said the same to the king. His quatrains drew on collections of older prophecies and on historical precedent, projected forward.

His astrology was criticised in his own day. Professional astrologers such as Laurens Videl attacked his competence and his reliance on comparing future planetary configurations with those of past events. Nostradamus usually expected clients to send him their charts ready-made, and when he cast them himself, from the published tables, he often failed to adjust the figures for the place or the time of birth. That is the step I never skip: the time decides the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) and the houses, and without it I don't read ([how to find your birth time](/astrology/how-to-find-your-birth-time/)).

His fame shows how vague words find their events. Historians find that the links made between his quatrains and later history come from misreadings and mistranslations, sometimes deliberate, and that no quatrain is known to have been read as predicting a specific event before it happened, except in terms general enough to fit many. The famous claim that quatrain I.35 foretold the death of Henry II first appeared in print in 1614, fifty-five years after the king died. Words loose enough to fit anyone feel written for us, and the mind keeps the hits and forgets the misses ([the Barnum (Forer) effect](/astrology/barnum-forer-effect/); [subjective validation](/astrology/subjective-validation/)).

So he stands, for me, at the opposite pole from what I do. I don't predict events, and least of all a death ([Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)); I read transits as weather, not a forecast, and a chart for tendencies ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). The almanac trade he joined went on long after him, and its English star, a century later, was [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/). The astronomer who wanted astrology reformed rather than prophesied, [Johannes Kepler](/astrology/johannes-kepler/), was born five years after Nostradamus died; their century is [Renaissance astrology](/astrology/renaissance-astrology/).
