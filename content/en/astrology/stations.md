Before a planet turns retrograde, and again before it turns direct, it seems to stop. For a few days it hangs at almost the same degree of the zodiac, and that standstill is called a station.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A station is the turning point of a planet’s apparent motion, seen from Earth, and every retrograde has two. At the station retrograde, a planet that has been moving forward slows to a stop and begins to move backwards; at the station direct, it stops once more and goes back to [direct motion](/astrology/direct-motion/). What happens between the two is the subject of [Retrograde, explained](/astrology/retrograde-explained/).

Take Mars, in the dates I work from. It stations retrograde on 10 January 2027 at 10°25′ Virgo, spends 81 days moving back, and stations direct on 1 April 2027 at 20°55′ Leo. Mercury makes the same two turns for each of its three or four retrogrades a year, and the slow planets, from Jupiter out, once in each direction every year.

Not everything stations. The [Sun](/astrology/sun/) and the [Moon](/astrology/moon/) always move forward, seen from Earth, so they never stop to turn. The lunar nodes and Lilith don’t either, and the system I built for my readings leaves them out of its station rule on purpose.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Nothing in space stops. Each planet keeps going round the Sun, and so does Earth; a station is the moment when, from our moving point of view, the planet’s drift against the stars runs down to nothing before reversing. Because that happens gradually, a station isn’t a sharp edge but a slow turn: the planet’s speed falls towards zero over several days, passes through it, and picks up again the other way.

So a stationary planet lingers. Mercury stays within about a degree of its station point for about a week; Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto, far slower, hover around theirs for weeks, even months. That lingering is why astrologers watch stations: a planet that stops on a degree spends longer there than on any ordinary pass.

Where exactly a station begins and ends is a matter of convention, not astronomy. Tables give the minute of the turn, and around it every astrologer draws the line somewhere. In the system I built, a planet counts as stationary when it moves at less than a tenth of its average daily speed: under 0.14° a day for Mercury, 0.12° for Venus, 0.05° for Mars, 0.008° for Jupiter, 0.003° for Saturn, and down to 0.0004° for Pluto. That gives each turn a few days of stillness, up to a week or so.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In a birth chart, I read a stationary planet as a function caught at a turning point. Its sign, house and aspects say what it does, as for any planet; the station adds weight. A planet born at its station retrograde was about to turn inward, one born at its station direct had just finished, and both tend to speak with more insistence than the same planet moving at its usual pace. It isn’t stuck, and it isn’t better: it’s louder. If it was moving backwards, it also carries the inward rhythm I describe in [Natal retrograde planets](/blog/retrograde-planets/).

In transits, a station on a point of your chart keeps the planet there far longer than an ordinary pass. That’s why a slow planet stationing on your Sun, your Moon or one of your angles tends to colour a whole season. I read it as a longer spell of the same weather, never as an event on its way. It also asks for precision: the exact degree of the natal point matters, and for the angles, so does the birth time.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“You’ll feel a station to the hour.”** The turn is gradual: the planet slows over days. The minute printed in a table is a calculation, not a jolt.
- **“Everything stalls when a planet stations.”** Stationary describes how the planet seems to move against the stars, not what happens to your plans.
- **“A planet is stationary only on the day of the turn.”** It lingers. Mercury stays within a degree of its turning point for about a week, the slow planets for far longer.
- **“A stationary planet in a birth chart is blocked.”** It is emphatic, not blocked: a function at a turning point, with all its usual meaning and more weight on it.
