Some planets stand in a degree where they hold nothing: not the sign, not the exaltation, not even the smallest slice of the zodiac. The tradition calls such a planet peregrine, a word that once meant a traveller far from home.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

To call a planet peregrine, an astrologer checks all five essential dignities at its degree. Is it in its own sign, or in its exaltation? Does it rule the element of its sign, by day or by night? Does it hold the term or the face of that degree? If the answer is no every time, the planet is peregrine. The Latin *peregrinus* meant a foreigner, one from abroad, and a peregrine planet is a guest of other planets wherever it stands.

Peregrine isn't another name for a debility. Detriment and [fall](/astrology/fall/) are particular weak places, opposite a planet's own sign and its exaltation; peregrine is the absence of every dignity. A planet in its fall can still hold a triplicity or a term, and a peregrine planet can stand in a sign that is neither its detriment nor its fall.

The check is done on a [table of dignities](/astrology/table-of-dignities/), one row per sign and one column per dignity, and it gives a yes or a no for every degree.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Because terms and faces change every few degrees, a planet can be peregrine at one degree and not a few degrees further on. Mars at 15° Gemini holds no sign, exaltation or triplicity there: Gemini belongs to Mercury, Mars is exalted in Capricorn, and the air signs are ruled by Saturn, Mercury and Jupiter. But the second [face](/astrology/faces/) of Gemini, from 10° to 20°, is Mars's own, and [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/) said the face's one use was exactly this, to keep a planet from being peregrine.

The answer also depends on whose rules you follow. The [terms](/astrology/terms-bounds/) differ between the Egyptian table and the one Ptolemy preferred, and the [triplicity rulers](/astrology/triplicity-rulers/) change between day and night charts. The same planet can be peregrine in one astrologer's reckoning and not in another's, or in a night chart and not in a day one, so the label is only as good as the table behind it.

The tradition treated a peregrine planet as weak, and the condition was counted a malefic one. Help could still come from outside: the planet that rules a sign could assist a planet standing in it, a relationship the old books call reception.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

A traveller depends on its hosts, and that's where I look. The planet that rules the sign a peregrine planet stands in tells me how it is received: well placed, it lends the guest a good deal; poorly placed, it has less to give ([dispositors and the final dispositor](/astrology/dispositors-and-the-final-dispositor/)). If the two planets stand in each other's signs, the exchange gives the guest a footing of its own ([mutual reception](/astrology/mutual-reception/)).

Where a planet is peregrine, the sign it stands in offers it no manner of its own, so it borrows: it works in the style of whatever it touches, and its aspects say more about it than its sign does. That can make it adaptable rather than weak. I read such a planet as a function shaped by experience rather than handed over ready-made: it learns its manner from its company.

I'm careful about evidence here. [My notebook](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/) checked each planet in its sign and house; it gives no figure for peregrine planets, or for any dignity, so I don't claim one.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A peregrine planet is powerless.”** It has no dignity in its degree, which is different from having no function. Its strength comes from elsewhere: its ruler, its aspects, its house.
- **“Peregrine means in detriment.”** Detriment is one particular weakness; peregrine is the absence of every dignity, in any sign.
- **“Any planet outside its own sign is peregrine.”** Triplicity, term or face can still give it a foothold, even in a sign that belongs to another planet.
- **“It's a modern label.”** It comes from the tradition: Lilly was already writing about it in 1647.
