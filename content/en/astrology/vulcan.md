Vulcan is the planet astronomy announced and then had to take back. For half a century it had an orbit, a discoverer and a medal for the man who saw it, everything except a body, and astrology has kept the name longer than astronomy did.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

In 1859 Urbain Le Verrier, the French mathematician who had predicted Neptune from the wanderings of Uranus, finished a careful study of [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/)'s motion. Something was off. Mercury's orbit slowly swings round, its closest point to the Sun creeping forward, and the creep was larger than Newton's physics allowed, by 43 arcseconds a century. Le Verrier put it down to an unseen planet inside Mercury's orbit, or a belt of small bodies there. A name was waiting: in 1846 Jacques Babinet had suggested *Vulcan*, after the Roman god of fire, for a planet so close to the Sun.

Late that year a country doctor, Edmond Lescarbault, wrote to say he had watched a small black dot cross the Sun on 26 March 1859, timing it with an old clock and the pendulum he used to take his patients' pulses. Le Verrier took the train to his village, questioned him, and on 2 January 1860 announced the new planet to the Académie des Sciences; Lescarbault was given the Légion d'honneur. From the one sighting, Le Verrier worked out an orbit: a year of 19 days and 17 hours, 21 million kilometres from the Sun, never more than 8° from it in our sky.

Nobody saw it again for certain. An astronomer working in Brazil said he had been studying the Sun, with a better telescope, at the very moment of the transit, and had seen nothing cross it. Two experienced observers claimed sightings at the total eclipse of 1878, but their positions didn't match. Eclipse expeditions kept looking until 1908, when the Lick Observatory, after photographing three eclipses, declared the search closed. In 1915 Einstein's general relativity accounted for Mercury's extra 43 arcseconds without any planet at all. The name survives in the vulcanoids, small asteroids that might orbit inside Mercury's path; none has been found.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Vulcan had its users while astronomers argued. In St Louis, John H. Tice, a weather forecaster, claimed to base his predictions on the movements of Mercury and of Vulcan, and the weather almanac he published from about 1875 circulated widely among farmers ([Astrometeorology](/astrology/astrometeorology/)).

In the esoteric astrology Alice Bailey taught, Vulcan outlasted the telescope searches. Her teaching of the seven rays, seven kinds of energy each tied to particular planets and signs, gives the first ray, will or power, two planets, Vulcan and [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), and two signs, [Taurus](/astrology/taurus/) and Pisces.

Set in a chart, a planet on Le Verrier's orbit would never stray more than 8° from the Sun, far closer than Mercury, which gets up to about 28° away: it would always sit in the Sun's sign or a neighbouring one. At the other end of the Solar System, astrologers have pictured unseen planets too, among them one beyond Pluto ([Transpluto (Isis)](/astrology/transpluto-isis/)), and Uranian astrology later gave the smith god's name to a point of its own, far beyond Neptune ([Vulkanus](/astrology/vulkanus/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

There's no Vulcan in my readings, because there's no Vulcan to place: everything I read has a position astronomy computes from a real orbit: the planets, Chiron, and the points taken from the Moon's own orbit, Lilith and the nodes. Vulcan's orbit was built on one man's sighting, and no one could repeat it.

If what draws you to Vulcan is its closeness to the Sun, Mercury does that work in a chart: never more than about 28° from the Sun, it describes how you think and speak, and in my notebook it was among the placements everyone reads first, which held about a third of the time ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). Power, in my readings, is part of what Pluto describes, by sign, house and aspect ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Vulcan was discovered in 1860.”** It was announced in 1860, on the strength of a sighting nobody could confirm, and given up once general relativity explained Mercury's orbit.
- **“Vulcan was an astrologers' invention.”** Astronomers proposed it, named it and announced it; astrologers took it up afterwards, and kept it once astronomy had let it go.
- **“Einstein proved there is nothing at all inside Mercury's orbit.”** His theory removed the need for a planet there. Astronomers still look for small vulcanoids, and haven't found one yet.
