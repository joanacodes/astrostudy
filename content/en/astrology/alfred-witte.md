Alfred Witte surveyed land in Hamburg for a living, and he read a birth chart with a surveyor's habits: measure the distances, find the halfway points, add and subtract. The circle that formed round him became the Hamburg School, and in America, Uranian astrology.

## Who {#who}

He was born in Hamburg on 2 March 1878, and he lived and died in the city. Beside his profession he was an amateur astronomer, and he wrote on astrology from 1913. His teaching drew collaborators, Friedrich Sieggrün and Ludwig Rudolph among the first, and in 1925 an association was founded in Hamburg round it ([The Hamburg School (Uranian astrology)](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/)).

Rudolph was also his publisher. His press, the Witte-Verlag Ludwig Rudolph, founded in 1927, printed the rulebook the two men wrote together, the *Regelwerk für Planetenbilder*, in 1928, then a second edition in 1932, which was registered at the Library of Congress in 1935. That same year the press issued a perpetual ephemeris for the four hypothetical points Witte had placed beyond Neptune, [Cupido](/astrology/cupido-uranian-point/), Hades, Zeus and Kronos, so that they could be set in a chart for any date. He took these points further than a calculation: in articles later gathered in the anthology *Der Mensch – eine Empfangsstation kosmischer Suggestionen*, he described the colours of the two nearest, Cupido and Hades.

His work reached America in his lifetime. Richard Svehla, owner of the Phoenix Bookshop in Cleveland, Ohio, introduced it there in the early 1930s, obtained the authorisation to translate the rulebook, and published it in 1939 as *Rules for Planetary Pictures*, subtitled “Uranian System of Astrology – Hamburg School by Alfred Witte”.

At home the decade closed in on him. The Nazi regime counted him an enemy: the *Regelwerk* was banned on 2 October 1936, and copies were later burned. The Gestapo kept Witte under observation, and when astrologers were interned in June 1941, Rudolph among them, he was left free. He was found dead in Hamburg on 4 August 1941, at 63. Carl Otto Fleischhauer's book on him, published in 2003, records that he took his own life, worried about his state pension.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He turned the midpoint into a method. The degree halfway between two planets was not his discovery, and he revived it rather than inventing it; what he built on it was a grammar. A planet standing on the [midpoint](/astrology/midpoints/) of two others, or two positions added together and a third taken away, makes a planetary picture: several factors of a chart read as one statement. He did the work on movable dials, the idea behind the dial his school went on to use ([The Uranian 90° dial](/astrology/uranian-90-dial/)).

He asked astrology to accept bodies nobody had seen. His points were posited while astronomers were still searching beyond Neptune, before Pluto was found in 1930, and Pluto turned out to be none of them. Precision is not proof. An ephemeris can give Kronos a position for every day of a century, and that says nothing about whether anything is there. I keep it in mind whenever a technique hands me exact figures: the exactness belongs to the arithmetic, not necessarily to the sky.

He left two lines of descent. One kept his hypothetical points, and goes by the name Svehla gave it in English. The other kept the arithmetic and set the points aside: [Reinhold Ebertin](/astrology/reinhold-ebertin/), who built [cosmobiology](/astrology/cosmobiology/) on Witte's rulebook, acknowledged in his own handbook how much he had drawn from it.

None of it enters my readings. I work with the planets, Chiron and Lilith, each in its sign, its house and its aspects, and I use neither midpoints nor hypothetical points ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). The nearest thing to his arithmetic on this site is the [composite chart](/astrology/composite-chart/), which sets each planet of a couple at the midpoint of the two partners' positions, a technique I describe rather than draw.

His students refounded the school in Hamburg in 1947. In the years he was building it, [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) was teaching Americans to read the spread of the planets round the whole wheel; Witte read the distances between them, one pair at a time.
