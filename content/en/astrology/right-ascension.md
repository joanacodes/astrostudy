If declination is the sky's latitude, right ascension is its longitude: the second coordinate astronomers need to pin down any point among the stars. It's counted in hours rather than degrees, and that small oddity is what ties it so closely to the time of birth.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Right ascension measures how far east a body lies along the [celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/), starting from the direction of the Sun at the March equinox. That zero point is the same one the tropical zodiac starts from, 0° Aries, and today it lies in the constellation Pisces. Paired with [declination](/astrology/declination/), which says how far north or south of the equator a body stands, right ascension gives a full address on the sky.

It's usually written in hours, minutes and seconds, with 24 hours making the whole circle. One hour of right ascension is 15° of arc, one minute is 15 minutes of arc. Astronomers chose time because they locate a star by timing when it crosses the meridian, the line through the highest point of the sky, as the Earth turns.

The name is old. *Ascensio recta*, in Latin, was the point of the celestial equator that rises together with a body as seen from the Earth's equator, where the equator meets the horizon at a right angle. Seen from any other latitude, the meeting is slanted, and the old term for that is oblique ascension.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Sun's right ascension moves through the year, about six hours a season: 0h at the March equinox, 6h at the June solstice, 12h at the September equinox. Because the Sun crosses your meridian at local noon, the right ascension sitting on the meridian runs, in effect, as a clock of the sky.

That's where right ascension meets a birth chart. At any moment one value of it lies on your meridian, and the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) is the point of the zodiac that shares it, where the meridian crosses the ecliptic. The value advances as the Earth turns, minute by minute, so the Midheaven, and from it the rest of the houses in Placidus, can't be found without the time of birth and its place. That's the arithmetic behind my refusal to read without a time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

The zodiac uses a different frame from right ascension and declination. It is built on the ecliptic, the Sun's path, and measures positions by zodiac longitude and [celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/). Both frames start from the March equinox, but the ecliptic is tilted against the equator, so a planet's right ascension and its zodiac longitude only agree at the equinoxes and solstices.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read right ascension as a placement. No one has a right ascension “sign”, and I wouldn't attach a meaning to a planet's value in hours. What it does is quieter and more basic: it's part of the machinery that turns a date, a time and a place into the chart I then read. When I check a Midheaven or a house cusp against an almanac, right ascension is one of the numbers doing the work.

It also explains something people notice when they compare charts: two people born a few minutes apart in the same town share almost everything except the angles and the house cusps. The difference is the right ascension that had reached their meridian.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Right ascension is a kind of rising sign.”** It's a coordinate on the sky. The rising sign is the sign of the zodiac degree on the eastern horizon.
- **“The hours in right ascension are clock time.”** They measure angle, 15° to the hour, borrowed from the turning of the Earth.
- **“Right ascension and zodiac longitude are interchangeable.”** They're counted along different circles and differ except at four points.
- **“Astrologers ignore astronomy's coordinates.”** Every chart is calculated with them, the Midheaven above all.
