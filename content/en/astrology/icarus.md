Most asteroids keep a respectful distance from the Sun. Icarus dives inside the orbit of Mercury every thirteen months or so and climbs back out beyond Mars, which is how it earned the name of the boy who flew too close.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Icarus is a small stony asteroid, about 1.4 km across, that spins fast, once every 2.3 hours. Its orbit is a very stretched ellipse tilted 23° to the Sun's path. At its closest it comes 0.19 times the Earth's distance from the Sun, nearer than [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/) ever gets; at its farthest, 1.97 times, beyond the orbit of [Mars](/astrology/mars/). One circuit takes about 409 days, and on the way it crosses the paths of Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars. From its discovery until 1983 it was the asteroid known to pass nearest the Sun.

Walter Baade found it at Palomar Observatory on 27 June 1949, almost 29 years after he had found [Hidalgo](/astrology/hidalgo/). It belongs to the Apollo asteroids, the Earth-crossing group named after its first-found member ([Apollo (asteroid)](/astrology/apollo-asteroid/)).

On 14 June 1968 it passed about 6.4 million km from the Earth and became the first asteroid ever observed by radar. Its close approaches come in June, at intervals of 9, 19 or 28 years: the last was in 2015, at about 8 million km, and the next notable one is due on 13 June 2043, at about 8.8 million km. It may be the source of the Arietids, a strong meteor shower that falls in daylight, though a comet is another candidate, and astronomers use its orbit to test general relativity.

It is also the lowest-numbered of the asteroids classed as potentially hazardous. The label is a matter of two numbers, a size over 150 metres and an orbit passing within 0.05 times the Earth–Sun distance of the Earth's; it isn't a forecast of an impact.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Daedalus, the craftsman who built the labyrinth for King Minos of Crete, was imprisoned there with his son Icarus. To escape, he made two pairs of wings from feathers, thread, leather straps and beeswax, and warned his son to keep a middle course: too low and the sea would soak the feathers, too high and the Sun would melt the wax. Icarus, giddy with flight, climbed. The wax softened, the feathers fell away one by one, and he dropped into the sea and drowned. Daedalus named the nearest island Icaria after him, and the sea around it is still the Icarian Sea. Later writers made the escape a voyage by ship, with Icarus falling overboard; the version that lasted gave us the phrase "to fly too close to the Sun".

The asteroid's name was suggested by R. C. Cameron and Dr Folkman and published in January 1950, and the father has a minor planet of his own, 1864 Daedalus. Placed in a chart, Icarus gets a sign, a house that only a birth time can give ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)) and its contacts, like any small body. The myth supplies the rest, and that's where the trouble starts.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read Icarus. The system I built for my readings computes no asteroid, and Icarus had no line in my notebook, which covered the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

With this myth the danger is the obvious one: a fall. A body named after a boy who drowned, close to someone's Sun or Mars, can look like a warning of an accident, a failure or worse. No placement predicts any of those, and I never read a chart for them ([Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)).

The story itself is kinder than the warnings people draw from it. Daedalus asked his son to fly the middle way, neither skimming the sea nor chasing the Sun, and that's a fair thing to ask of a reading as well: no inflation, and no doom ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Icarus is on course to hit the Earth.”** Its orbit crosses the Earth's, but its close approaches keep it millions of kilometres away: about 6.4 million in 1968, about 8 million in 2015.
- **“Project Icarus was a real plan to blow it up.”** It was a student exercise at MIT in 1967, a contingency plan never needed, which later inspired the film *Meteor*.
- **“Icarus was only warned about the Sun.”** Daedalus warned him about the sea as well. Too low was as dangerous as too high.
