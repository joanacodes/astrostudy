Every so often a headline announces a black moon, as if something were about to happen in the sky. Nothing is: a black moon is a new moon that the calendar counts twice, and you can’t see it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The name is used in more than one way, and none of them is official. The most common is the second new moon in a single calendar month, the new-moon twin of the calendar [blue moon](/astrology/blue-moon/). Some writers use it instead for the third of four new moons in one season, mirroring the older, seasonal blue moon. Time and Date, which publishes sky calendars, gives both senses and notes that the term is not an official astronomical one; EarthSky gives the same two. I haven’t found a source I’d trust on who first used it, so I won’t guess.

Like the [wolf moon](/astrology/wolf-moon/) and the other popular moon names, it is laid over the lunar cycle by calendars and headlines, not by astronomy. The sky does nothing different on the night: the Moon passes the Sun, as it does every month, and stays out of sight.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A lunation, about 29½ days, is shorter than every calendar month but February, so the date of the new moon slips back by a day or so each month, and every few years a month that begins with a new moon fits a second one before it ends.

In Universal Time, the dates I use give two between 2026 and 2030. The first is 31 August 2027, at 8°06′ Virgo, after the new moon of 2 August, which is also a total solar eclipse. The second is 30 June 2030, at 9°09′ Cancer, after the new moon of 1 June, an annular eclipse. Time zones decide a great deal: the new moon of 31 August 2027 falls at 17:41 UT, already 1 September for anyone seven hours or more ahead of Greenwich, and the one of 30 June 2030, at 21:35 UT, is 1 July from three hours ahead. Whether your calendar has a black moon depends on where you hang it.

By the seasonal definition, counting the astronomical seasons from solstice to equinox, there is one in the same years: 20 August 2028, at 27°56′ Leo, the third of four new moons between the June solstice and the September equinox.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In astrology, black moon usually means something else entirely. [Black Moon Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/) is the averaged farthest point of the Moon’s orbit, a point I read in every birth chart and one of [the three placements that held best](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/) in my notebook; when an astrologer says the Black Moon, that is almost always what they mean. The calendar black moon has nothing to do with it: it is a [new moon](/astrology/new-moon/), in the Sun’s sign, like any other. If you are trying to work out which Lilith a chart is showing you, [Which Lilith is mine?](/astrology/which-lilith-is-mine/) untangles the names.

I read a calendar black moon as the new moon it is: by its sign, the Sun’s, and only if it lands within a degree of one of your natal points while something slower is already at work there. Two new moons in one month is a fact about our calendar, not a message, and it is weather, not a forecast.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A black moon is a dark omen.”** It is a quirk of the calendar. Nothing in the sky is different from any other new moon.
- **“You can see a black moon.”** A new moon is invisible, lost in the Sun’s light, whichever number it is in the month.
- **“The black moon is Black Moon Lilith.”** Lilith is a point of the Moon’s orbit, read in a birth chart; the calendar black moon is a new moon that happens to be the second in a month.
- **“It is an ancient tradition.”** The sky calendars that use the name treat it as a popular label, defined in different ways. I have found nothing that traces it far back.
