A Sun at 19°56′ Leo and a Sun at 139°56′ are the same Sun, written two ways. The first is how a chart writes it; the second is the single number underneath, the body's zodiacal longitude.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Zodiacal longitude is how far a body lies along the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), counted eastward, in the order of the signs, from the point where the Sun stands at the March equinox. It runs from 0° to 360°. Astronomers call it ecliptic or celestial longitude and write it λ; its partner, [celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/), says how far north or south of the ecliptic the same body lies.

The way of writing it is very old. Late in the fifth century BCE, most likely within a few years of 400 BCE, Babylonian astronomers divided the ecliptic into twelve equal signs of 30°, and in doing so gave the sky its first known coordinate system. Astronomers went on counting degrees within signs into the nineteenth century, and eighteenth-century textbooks write positions in the form ♌19°55′58″ where a modern one would print 139°55′58″. Astrology kept the signs; astronomy now counts straight round.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Each sign begins at a round number:

- **Aries** 0°, **Taurus** 30°, **Gemini** 60°
- **Cancer** 90°, **Leo** 120°, **Virgo** 150°
- **Libra** 180°, **Scorpio** 210°, **Sagittarius** 240°
- **Capricorn** 270°, **Aquarius** 300°, **Pisces** 330°

To turn a position into longitude, add its degrees to the start of its sign: 16° Capricorn is 286°. Four of those starting points are the year's turning points. Astronomers define the March equinox as the moment the Sun's longitude reaches 0° and the September equinox as the moment it reaches 180°; the solstices fall at 90° and 270° ([Solstices and equinoxes](/astrology/solstices-and-equinoxes/)). That is the tropical zodiac at its plainest: longitude counted from the equinox of the moment, so the zero point moves with [precession](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/) and the signs stay with the seasons.

Everything that moves in a chart is a change of longitude. A planet moving on through the signs gains it; a retrograde planet loses it; at a station, it stops changing for a while ([Retrograde, explained](/astrology/retrograde-explained/)). And an aspect is a difference of longitude. At the full moon of 26 September 2026, the Moon stands at 3°37′ Aries, longitude 3°37′, and the Sun at 3°37′ Libra, longitude 183°37′: exactly 180° apart, an [opposition](/astrology/opposition/).

A chart uses geocentric longitude, a planet's place as seen from the Earth. Seen from the Sun, every planet's longitude only ever grows; the backward loops belong to our point of view.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Every placement I read starts as a longitude. The sign and degree come from it directly. The house comes from comparing it with the longitudes of the house cusps, and those depend on the minute and place of birth: that's why a planet keeps its sign whatever the hour while its house waits on the time, and why I don't read a chart without one ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

The timing in my forecasts runs on longitude too. A [solar return](/astrology/solar-return/) is cast for the moment the Sun gets back to its exact natal longitude, to the minute of arc. A transit is a moving planet reaching a natal longitude, or a set distance from it ([transits](/astrology/transits/)). [Secondary progressions](/astrology/secondary-progressions/) and [solar arc directions](/astrology/solar-arc-directions/) carry the natal longitudes forward, the solar arc by about a degree a year, and the orbs I allow are measured in degrees of longitude: about one, for a solar arc.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The longitude in my chart is my birthplace's longitude.”** That one goes into the program to work out the houses. Zodiacal longitude runs round the sky, not the Earth, and every planet has its own.
- **“0° of a sign sits on the fence between two signs.”** 0° Leo is longitude 120°, the first degree of Leo; the planet crossed the line the moment it reached 120°.
- **“The 360° circle is a modern convention.”** The twelve signs of 30° go back to Babylonian astronomy around 400 BCE; counting straight round to 360 is the newer habit.
- **“A star's degree never changes.”** Longitudes are counted from the equinox of their own date, which slides back about 50″ a year, so a star's longitude has grown by about 22′ since 2000. A figure from an old list needs bringing up to date.
