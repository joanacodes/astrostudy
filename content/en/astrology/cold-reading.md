Cold reading works on the person in front of the reader: what they wear, how they speak, how they react. It can make a stranger feel known without a chart, a palm or a card having anything to do with it.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Cold reading is real and well described: a set of techniques that let a reader seem to know a stranger, by speaking with confidence, offering lines that fit many people and watching the person for clues. A reading written from a birth date, time and place alone isn't cold reading in the strict sense, because there's no face to read. But its habits can slip into any reading, written or spoken, and a reader can use them without knowing. That's why it's worth recognising.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

The classic description is Ray Hyman's, and his own story comes first. Before he became a psychologist, he read palms for money. By his account, he didn't believe in palmistry when he started, but his clients kept telling him he was uncannily accurate, and by the time he began college he was a true believer. Then a friend suggested an experiment: read the next client's palm by telling her the opposite of what her lines said. She was thrilled at how well he had captured her personality, and so were the next few clients he tried it on. Whatever was happening, he concluded, "it had nothing to do with the lines in the hand", and he changed his studies from journalism to psychology. It's his own account, not a controlled test, and it's about palms rather than charts; it's where his question began.

In 1977 Hyman published "'Cold Reading': How to Convince Strangers That You Know All About Them" in *The Skeptical Inquirer*. He listed what helps a reader persuade: an air of confidence, which, he told readers, can sell "even a bad reading" to most people; knowing from surveys and polls what people commonly believe, want and worry about; a prop, such as a crystal ball or a palm; attention to clothing, jewellery, mannerisms and speech; and flattery.

In 1988 Denis Dutton, writing in the journal *Experientia*, defined cold reading as a set of techniques used by mentalists, psychics, fortune-tellers and mediums. He regretted that academic psychology had paid it so little attention, since successful cold reading, he wrote, formed the basis for much of the belief in paranormal powers, and he called for the study of the methods proficient readers actually use. As his complaint suggests, much of what's known comes from people who practised it and then described it, Hyman among them.

The point I find most useful is that a reader can do all this without meaning to. Karla McLaren, a former New Age practitioner, has described putting her observations as questions rather than statements, to seem less of an expert; that politeness, she realised, invited people to "lean into the reading" and give her more to go on. And Hyman, remember, believed in his own palm reading.

Psychologists explain why it works with the Barnum effect, which David Marks counts as one case of [subjective validation](/astrology/subjective-validation/), and with confirmation bias. What a reader says can also shape what happens next, which is a different mechanism with its own page: the [self-fulfilling prophecy](/astrology/self-fulfilling-prophecy/).

## What I found {#what-i-found}

A reading from me is written from your date, time and place of birth, with a system I built, and I read it before it's sent. You don't need to write to me at all, so nothing in it depends on watching your face. That isn't a virtue I'm claiming; it's simply how the reading is made. The written words can still borrow cold reading's habits, a line that fits most people, a line that flatters, and you're entitled to ask any reader, me included, how a line tells you apart from anyone else.

What my own count gave: about a third held overall, 30 to 40%, and [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/), [Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/) and [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/) held 75 to 90% of the time. It's [a notebook, not a study](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), and it proves nothing about astrology in general. It also has the weakness this page describes, in another form.

I tested my family and my friends, people I knew well before I opened their charts, and what you know about someone colours what you see in their chart. The strangers online told me nothing about themselves first, and with everyone I worked in silence until I'd finished; then each person marked the lines. That kept the conversation out of the count, but not the person: they still knew the chart was theirs.
