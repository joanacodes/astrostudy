A grand trine is a closed loop. Hang a fourth planet opposite one of its corners and the loop gets a tail and a direction: the shape astrologers call a kite.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

Take a [grand trine](/astrology/grand-trine/), three planets about 120° apart, and add a fourth opposite one corner. The other two corners sit 120° from that one, so each falls 60° from the newcomer: the fourth planet makes an [opposition](/astrology/opposition/) to the corner it faces and a [sextile](/astrology/sextile/) to each of the other two. Drawn on the chart, the four make a kite, a long spine from the fourth planet to the corner it opposes, and two short sides out to the wings. Six aspects hold it together: three trines, one opposition, two sextiles.

Two patterns sit inside it. The original triangle is still there, and the fourth planet with the two wing planets forms a [minor grand trine](/astrology/minor-grand-trine/): two planets in trine, both sextile a third. In signs, a kite usually takes the four signs of one polarity, because opposite signs share a polarity: a fire grand trine gets its tail in an air sign, an earth one in a water sign. A grand trine in Aries, Leo and Sagittarius, with a planet in Libra facing the Aries corner, is a kite built of fire and air.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

About 1 person in 19 born from 1930 to 2009 has a kite among the ten planets, in the count behind this site, with every aspect counted within 8° when the Sun or Moon is involved and 6° otherwise. A degree or two of orb either way moves that figure a lot. The count also misses the kites that the Ascendant, the Midheaven, Chiron or the nodes complete: a grand trine with one corner on the Descendant has the Ascendant itself as its tail, and only the birth time shows it.

The system I built for my readings describes the trine as flow that leaves each function itself, the sextile as cooperation that has to be taken up, and the opposition as polarity, two functions learning to see each other. I read a kite as ease given a purpose. The grand trine supplies a talent that runs on its own; the planet opposite one corner puts a question to it, and the two sextiles give it ways to answer. I think of the corner it faces as the head of the kite: the function the whole figure tends to pull towards. What the kite is for depends on the planets. With the Sun at the head, it often shows as a sense of direction; with Saturn at the tail, as talent asked to take on structure and responsibility.

The spine runs between two houses facing each other, and that axis tells me where the purpose plays out: self and partner, what's mine and what's shared, home and public life. Those houses need the time of birth, and so does any kite that passes through the Moon, which can leave its corner within a day. I [don't read a chart without the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) for exactly that reason: the kite's shape would be there, but not where it flies.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Follow the spine.** The opposition is the kite's working line. Ask what its two ends want from each other, and you'll find what the talent is being asked to do.
- **Use the wings.** The two sextiles are offers of help, from the planets on either side. Like every sextile, they work when you take them up.
- **Let the triangle rest sometimes.** The ease of the grand trine is still yours. Not every gift needs a project attached.
- **Expect some effort.** The opposition makes demands the trine alone never would. That's the price of direction, and usually worth it.

Its neighbours in this family share parts of it: the [boomerang yod](/astrology/boomerang-yod/) also hangs a planet opposite one point, and the [mystic rectangle](/astrology/mystic-rectangle/) keeps the mix of oppositions, trines and sextiles but doubles the opposition. A kite flies because something holds the string; the opposition is that string, and it's worth holding.
