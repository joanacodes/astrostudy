William Lilly was a farmer's son who walked to London to work as a servant and became the most important astrologer in England. He wrote astrology for people who had never read Latin, and astrologers still learn from the book he wrote.

## Who {#who}

He was born on 1 May 1602, by the old calendar, in the village of Diseworth in Leicestershire, the son of a yeoman farmer who struggled with the cost of his farm. His mother was determined he should be educated, and at the grammar school of Ashby-de-la-Zouch, under the schoolmaster John Brinsley, he became an excellent Latinist, which later served him well: nearly all the books of astrology were then in Latin. Cambridge was out of reach. “Only poor I, William Lilly, was not so happy,” he remembered, of the classmates who went up and he did not.

At eighteen he walked to London beside a carrier's cart, in “a very stormy week, cold and uncomfortable”, to become secretary and servant to Gilbert Wright, Master of the Salters' Company. When Wright died in 1627, Lilly married his widow, Ellen, and on her death she left him close to a thousand pounds, enough for the leisure to study astrology. He lived in a house on the Strand from 1627 to 1665, where a plaque now marks the site.

His almanacs and predictions made him a public figure in the Civil War. He dedicated *Christian Astrology* to Bulstrode Whitelocke, Lord Keeper of the Great Seal, counted members of Parliament among his supporters, and accused others of engineering charges against him in 1651; in his autobiography he credits Oliver Cromwell's friendly support while he faced prosecution for his political predictions. To his supporters he was an “English Merlin”; to his detractors, a “juggling wizard and imposter”. Samuel Pepys spent an evening at his house in October 1660 and met Elias Ashmole there, the patron at whose request Lilly wrote his life. He died at Hersham, in Surrey, on 9 June 1681.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He taught astrology in English. *[Christian Astrology](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/)* is a huge compendium of technique, and the historian Lauren Kassell says it tutored “a nation in crisis in the language of the stars”. Its third book, on nativities, keeps the rulers Ptolemy gave the water signs ([triplicity rulers](/astrology/triplicity-rulers/)).

He taught horary astrology by example, judging questions chart by chart in his second book ([horary astrology](/astrology/horary-astrology/)). It is a branch I don't practise: many of the questions brought to it are ones I won't read, and I read a birth chart for tendencies rather than answering what will happen ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

He carried the medieval masters across. His *Anima Astrologiae* (1676), “a Guide for Astrologers”, was “translated from Guido Bonatus” and from Cardano's “seaven Segments”, so that [Guido Bonatti](/astrology/guido-bonatti/) could be read in English four centuries after he wrote.

He showed what prediction costs. After the Great Fire of 1666, Lilly was brought before the committee investigating its cause, suspected because fifteen years earlier he had published images of a city in flames surrounded by coffins. The episode shows how quickly a forecast can become an accusation.

And he came back. A facsimile of the 1647 edition, published in 1985, helped set off the [traditional revival](/astrology/traditional-revival/), in which astrologers went back to his book and to the older ones behind it. His century and his book's place in it are told in [Renaissance astrology](/astrology/renaissance-astrology/).

He followed, in the trade of almanacs and prophecy, the most famous name of the century before, [Nostradamus](/astrology/nostradamus/). Two centuries after him, astrology in English took a very different turn with [Alan Leo](/astrology/alan-leo/), who wanted character read instead of events foretold.
