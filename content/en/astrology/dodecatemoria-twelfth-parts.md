The zodiac has twelve signs; the dodecatemoria put a whole zodiac inside each one. They are among the oldest ways of finding more in a single degree, and among the first to be called pointless, by Ptolemy himself.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Greek name means twelfth-parts, and that is what they are: each sign cut into twelve equal slices of 2½°. Each slice is given to a sign, starting with the sign you are in and going round in order. The first twelfth of Virgo belongs to Virgo, the second to Libra, and so on to the last, which belongs to Leo. Twelve signs of twelve parts make 144, a miniature [zodiac](/astrology/zodiac/) that historians call the micro-zodiac.

The word has a second use, which gives a single point rather than a slice. To find a planet's twelfth-part, take its degree within its sign, multiply by twelve, and count that many degrees on from the start of the sign. A Moon at 18° Gemini gives 216°, and 216° on from 0° Gemini lands at 6° Capricorn. The two uses agree: 18° falls in the eighth slice of Gemini, and the eighth sign counted from Gemini is Capricorn.

Manilius, whose *Astronomica* sets out the calculation, goes a step further and splits each 2½° slice into five half-degrees, each given to a planet.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The method is older than Greek astrology. Babylonian cuneiform tablets, possibly from the Persian period between 539 and 331 BC, already multiply by twelve to find the twelfth-part of a degree. Their version differs slightly: it adds twelve times the degree to the degree itself, not to the start of the sign, so the point moves thirteen times as fast as the planet. Greek astrologers took the idea over, and the Latin writers Manilius and [Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/) both describe the calculation; it became part of [Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/).

Why multiply by twelve? One suggestion is that it gave the astrologer a second position for every planet, and so twice as much to interpret. Another is that it imitates the Moon, which runs through all twelve signs in about the time the Sun crosses one; the Babylonian figures fit an imagined Moon moving 13° a day.

Ptolemy would have none of it. In the *Tetrabiblos* he notes the twelfth-parts and dismisses them as illogical: divisions manufactured out of numbers, with no basis in any natural cycle ([Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)). Abraham Ibn Ezra, in the 12th century, still described two versions, one dealing the 2½° slices out to the seven planets, the other giving each single degree a sign, so that the cycle runs two and a half times through every sign.

The twelfth-parts sit beside the other small divisions of a sign, the [terms (bounds)](/astrology/terms-bounds/) and the [faces](/astrology/faces/), and they even reached medicine: a Greek text gives each slice of a sign a part of the body ([The signs and the body, in old medicine](/astrology/signs-and-the-body-in-old-medicine/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't use twelfth-parts. They aren't in the system I built for my readings, and they weren't among the placements my notebook tested, which were the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign and each planet by sign and house ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

They do show, more sharply than anything else, why the birth time matters. A twelfth-part moves twelve times as fast as the point it comes from. The Ascendant takes about two hours to cross a sign, on average, so its twelfth-part crosses one in about ten minutes, and a time rounded to the quarter-hour can be seven minutes out, enough to move it most of a sign. Even the Moon's twelfth-part changes sign every four or five hours. There's always a birth time, or no reading, and a technique like this would want the minute ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Dodecatemoria are the same as decans.”** Decans cut a sign into three parts of 10°, from Egyptian star groups ([The Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)); twelfth-parts cut it into twelve of 2½°, from Babylonian arithmetic.
- **“There is one way to calculate them.”** The Babylonian method counts from the planet's own degree, the Greek one from the start of the sign, and Ibn Ezra knew two more.
- **“Ptolemy used them.”** He mentions them only to dismiss them.
- **“A planet's twelfth-part replaces its sign.”** Where it is used, it adds a second position beside the planet; the planet stays in its own sign.
