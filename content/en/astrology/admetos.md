Seven of Uranian astrology's eight points are named after gods. Admetos is named after a man: a king of Thessaly whom a god once served as a herdsman, and whose wife agreed to die in his place.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Friedrich Sieggrün proposed it in 1927, the second of his four points; [Vulkanus](/astrology/vulkanus/) and Poseidon came after it ([The Hamburg School (Uranian astrology)](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/)). The name just before it on his list is [Apollon](/astrology/apollon-uranian-point/), so the god and the king who employed him stand side by side, both beyond Neptune, where neither has ever been seen.

Admetus, whose name means the untameable, was king of Pherae in Thessaly, a city named after his father, Pheres. He sailed with the Argonauts, hunted the Calydonian boar, and was famous for his hospitality and his justice. When Apollo was sentenced to a year's service to a mortal, it was Admetus he served, as a cowherd, and afterwards the god helped him win his wife, Alcestis, by yoking a boar and a lion to a chariot, the task her father had set her suitors.

Apollo did more. He persuaded the Fates, in one telling by getting them drunk, to spare Admetus on his fated day if someone would die in his place. His aged parents refused. Alcestis went instead, and in Euripides' play *Alcestis* the king finds, once she is gone, that he no longer wants to live. Heracles, who had been his guest, wrestled Death for her and brought her back; in another version Persephone, queen of the underworld, let her go.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Uranian astrologers read Admetos as transformative power, close kin to [Hades](/astrology/hades/), with one difference: Admetos's power is held inside and is hard to release into the world, while Hades's can be released by helping others. They also read it for spiritual blockage, and for depth of knowledge.

The story and the reading pull different ways: the king was famous for an open house, and the point is read for power kept in.

The ancient writers didn't agree on why the god served him, either. In one telling it was a punishment; Callimachus wrote that Apollo was “fired with love” for Admetus, Plutarch counted the king among Apollo's lovers, and the Roman poet Tibullus called the god's year with the herds a slavery of love, chosen rather than imposed.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Admetos isn't in my readings. When someone tells me that something in them won't move, I don't go looking for a hidden point. I look at what is crossing their chart now, the slow planets above all, [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/) first among them, and at how long each contact lasts ([Transits](/astrology/transits/)). In a forecast I follow the transits of the planets from Jupiter out to Pluto, the social and generational ones ([Personal, social and generational planets](/astrology/personal-social-and-generational-planets/)), with Chiron and the true node, and every one of them arrives, builds and passes. A slow transit has a start and an end: a timetable, not a wall.

*Spiritual blockage* isn't a phrase I use in a reading either. A chart can show where things come slowly, or late; it can't weigh anyone's inner life, and it would be unkind to pretend it can ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Admetos is a god.”** He was a mortal king of Pherae; of the eight Uranian points, his is the one name that doesn't belong to a god.
- **“Admetos means you'll stay stuck.”** No point fixes a fate, and his own story brings two reprieves, one from a god and one from a hero.
- **“Admetos is another name for Hades.”** Uranian astrologers read the two as related powers, one released through helping others, the other held in; in myth, Admetus was the mortal whose wife the underworld gave back.
