Marc Edmund Jones taught American astrologers to step back from a chart before reading any of it. His seven chart shapes are still taught, and the study group he founded in 1923 still exists.

## Who {#who}

He was born in St. Louis, Missouri, on 1 October 1888. Early in life he wrote scenarios for silent films, and worked in that trade for a number of years. In 1923 he founded a special-studies group, the Sabian Assembly, which still exists. He was ordained a Presbyterian minister in 1934, later earned a doctorate at Columbia University, and spent years teaching and lecturing across the United States. His books range from *How to Learn Astrology* and *The Guide to Horoscope Interpretation* to *Horary Astrology*, *Mundane Perspectives in Astrology* and *The Counseling Manual in Astrology*, and outside astrology he wrote on the philosopher George Sylvester Morris. He died at Stanwood, Washington, on 5 March 1980, and his papers are now at the Harry Ransom Center of the University of Texas at Austin.

He was a teacher in the most direct sense. The theosophist Will Levington Comfort introduced a young French-born composer, [Dane Rudhyar](/astrology/dane-rudhyar/), to Jones, who introduced him to astrology, and Rudhyar learned it from Jones's mimeographed lessons. Rudhyar's first book on the subject, *[The Astrology of Personality](/astrology/dane-rudhyars-the-astrology-of-personality-1936/)*, followed in 1936.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He taught people to look at the whole chart first. Jones named seven shapes that the ten planets make round the wheel, before a single aspect is drawn or a single placement read. In a [bundle](/astrology/bundle-chart/) they fit within 120°, about four signs; in a [bowl](/astrology/bowl-chart/), within half the circle; a [bucket](/astrology/bucket-chart/) is a bowl with one planet out on the empty side as its handle; a [locomotive](/astrology/locomotive-chart/) leaves a third of the wheel empty; a [seesaw](/astrology/seesaw-chart/) splits the planets into two groups facing each other; a [splay](/astrology/splay-chart/) gathers them into irregular knots; and a [splash](/astrology/splash-chart/) spreads them all round. Each has a page on this site. The books that followed him often read the shapes with great confidence, the bundle as single-minded, the splash as scattered; I read a shape more modestly, as emphasis. Shapes weren't among what I tested in my notebook, which checked each planet in its sign and house one by one, so I have no count of my own to offer for them ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

The shape comes almost entirely from the planets, so most of it is there without a birth time. Which way it faces, and which houses its empty side covers between the Ascendant and the other angles, are questions only the time answers, and that's why I don't read a chart's shape, or anything else in it, without one ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

He gave each degree a picture. *The Sabian Symbols in Astrology* sets out an image for every one of the 360 degrees of the zodiac. Attention to the single degree is old, as old as [Vettius Valens](/astrology/vettius-valens/); what Jones added was a modern image for each one.

He worked in a different register from [Evangeline Adams](/astrology/evangeline-adams/). Where she had made astrology a public profession, in the courts and the newspapers, Jones made it a subject of study, taught in lessons, assemblies and manuals, and passed on through students. Through Rudhyar, his teaching fed into the astrology that set out to describe what a person might become ([humanistic astrology](/astrology/humanistic-astrology/)).

In Hamburg, in the same decade as his Sabian Assembly, [Alfred Witte](/astrology/alfred-witte/) was building a very different kind of school, one that worked by arithmetic between the planets rather than by images.
