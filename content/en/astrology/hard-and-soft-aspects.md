Astrologers sort the angles between planets into two families. The angles that make two planets rub are called hard, the ones that let them work together are called soft, and the conjunction sits apart from both. The words are useful shorthand, and very easy to misread as bad and good.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The hard aspects are the [square](/astrology/square/) and the [opposition](/astrology/opposition/). A square joins two planets 90° apart, usually three signs away from each other and in the same modality: two cardinal signs, say, which share a way of moving and little else. An opposition joins two planets 180° apart, in opposite signs, facing each other across the chart.

The soft aspects are the [trine](/astrology/trine/) and the [sextile](/astrology/sextile/). A trine joins two planets 120° apart, usually in signs of the same element; a sextile joins them 60° apart, usually fire with air or earth with water, elements that get along.

The [conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/), two planets together, belongs to neither family. It fuses whatever meets in it, so its nature is the nature of its planets.

The sorting is old. Ptolemy already called the trine and the sextile harmonious, and the square and the opposition disharmonious (*Tetrabiblos*, book I). The words hard and soft came much later, and they cover the minor aspects too, less tidily. Many astrologers count the [semi-square](/astrology/semi-square/) (45°) and the [sesquiquadrate](/astrology/sesquiquadrate/) (135°) as hard, because they come from cutting the square in half and adding the halves; Johannes Kepler had already added the sesquiquadrate to Ptolemy's list (*Harmonices Mundi*, 1619). The other minor aspects have no family everyone agrees on.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Each aspect relates two functions without changing either. A square brings them together through friction: what one planet wants gets in the way of what the other wants, at the same moment, and something has to move. An opposition puts them at two ends of a seesaw: each makes you aware of the other, and the work is balance rather than victory. A trine lets them reinforce each other, so they flow; a sextile gives them an opening, which only becomes something if you take it.

So a hard aspect tends to be felt, and a soft one tends to go unnoticed. You know where you're pushing against yourself; you rarely stop to notice where things simply work. That's the whole difference between the two families, and it says nothing about which life is better.

How loud an aspect is depends on how close it is to exact (its orb), on the planets themselves, and on whether the faster one is closing in or pulling away: [applying and separating aspects](/astrology/applying-and-separating-aspects/) describes that last difference. Chart programs list every aspect in [the aspect grid](/astrology/aspect-grid/), and many draw the lines across the wheel in two colours, often red for the hard family and blue for the soft, which makes the balance of a chart visible at a glance.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read both families in every chart, and I don't count them up to decide whether a life will be easy. The same two planets can meet both ways, and the comparison shows what each family does. [Mars square Saturn](/astrology/mars-square-saturn/) and [Mars trine Saturn](/astrology/mars-trine-saturn/) both join effort with structure. With the square, discipline is won against resistance, often slowly, and once won it's hard to shake. With the trine, discipline comes so naturally that it may never be tested, and the person may not know how much they rely on it.

A chart with few hard aspects isn't a charmed chart: it can lack the friction that makes someone move. A chart with many isn't doomed: it often belongs to someone who gets things done because something always needs settling. What matters is which planets are involved, in which signs and houses, and what the person does with them, and that's [how I read](/how-i-read/): tendencies, never verdicts.

The same holds in [transits](/astrology/transits/), where a hard aspect from a slow planet tends to feel like pressure and a soft one like a tailwind, both as weather rather than a forecast, and in [synastry](/astrology/synastry/), where a hard contact between two charts can be the spark of the relationship as much as its sore point.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Hard aspects are bad aspects.”** A square is friction, never failure; an opposition is a pull between two ends, never enmity. Both are where effort, and often growth, comes from.
- **“Soft aspects bring luck.”** A trine is flow and a sextile an opening; neither delivers anything by itself, and ease you never use stays potential.
- **“The conjunction is the easiest aspect.”** It's the most intense, because there's no distance between the planets, and it's as comfortable or as demanding as the two planets that meet.
- **“Count the aspects and you know the chart.”** A tally of hard and soft says little until you know which planets, how close, and in which signs and houses.
