At some point in Ptolemaic Egypt, astrology stopped looking only at the king and turned to the single person. Hellenistic astrology is where that happened, and most of what a birth chart contains today was first written down in Greek during its seven or eight centuries.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

Historians place its beginnings in the late second or early first century BC, around the Mediterranean and above all in Egypt, and it was practised until the sixth or seventh century AD. It drew on both traditions before it: the Babylonian zodiac with its exaltations and eclipses, and [the Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/) with their stress on whatever was rising. Greek had become the language of learning after Alexander's conquests, and around 280 BC a priest of Bel from Babylon, Berossus, settled on the island of Kos to teach astrology to Greeks.

The astrologers of the period told stories about their art's origins. Firmicus Maternus says the system was handed down to an Egyptian king, Nechepso, and his priest Petosiris. The authors whose own words survive are these:

- **[Dorotheus of Sidon](/astrology/dorotheus-of-sidon/)**, a first-century poet of astrology, wrote five books in verse, the *Pentateuch*, better known as *Carmen Astrologicum*. It survives in Greek fragments and an Arabic translation.
- **[Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/)** wrote the *[Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)* in Alexandria in the second century, after his astronomy, the *Almagest*, and explained astrology in the natural philosophy of his day: warmth, cold, moisture and dryness.
- **[Vettius Valens](/astrology/vettius-valens/)**, a second-century astrologer, compiled the *Anthology*. The classicist Mark Riley sets the two men side by side: Valens the practitioner, keeping techniques from older tradition, Ptolemy the theorist, building a consistent model.
- **[Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/)** wrote the *Mathesis* in Latin around 334 to 337, the longest text of Roman astrology to survive.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

The hour of birth, above all. A horoscopic chart is built around the degree rising on the eastern horizon, in Greek the *horoskopos*, the marker of the hour, which gave us the word horoscope, and around twelve places counted from it, the houses. That's the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/), and it moves about a degree every four minutes. My own rule was set the day astrology became personal: there's always a birth time, or no reading. Without it there's no rising degree and no houses ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

The houses were usually counted one whole sign each, [Whole Sign houses](/astrology/whole-sign-houses/), thought to be the oldest system of all. Valens also describes dividing each quarter of the chart between the angles into three by degree, the first of the quadrant methods. I use [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/), a much later quadrant system; Whole Sign astrologers are working with the older practice, and both have their reasons ([Which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/)).

Ptolemy's first book gives the grammar most charts still speak: the planets' natures, the day and night planets of [sect](/astrology/sect-day-and-night-charts/), the signs each planet rules, the exaltations, and the aspects, opposition, trine, square and sextile, which with the conjunction are still the five major aspects. Dorotheus and Valens add the lots, points measured from the Ascendant, such as the [Part of Fortune](/astrology/part-of-fortune/), although Ptolemy dismissed “lots and numbers of which no reasonable explanation can be given”.

Ptolemy was also frank about limits. He wrote that prediction is difficult and easily mistaken, and that the lesser cause always yields to the stronger, and he defended astrology only as far as this: “even if it be not entirely infallible, at least its possibilities have appeared worthy of the highest regard.” I'd put my own claim more modestly still, and [how I read](/how-i-read/) says how far it goes.

Its books travelled east, into Middle Persian and then into Arabic: [Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/).
