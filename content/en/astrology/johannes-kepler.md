Johannes Kepler found the laws by which the planets move, and paid many of his bills with horoscopes. He never pretended the two sat comfortably together, and he spent years trying to work out which parts of astrology deserved to survive.

## Who {#who}

He was born on 27 December 1571 in Weil der Stadt, in the Holy Roman Empire, and the sky caught him early. At six his mother took him to a high place to see the great comet of 1577; at nine he was called outdoors for an eclipse of the Moon, which “appeared quite red”. At the seminary in Tübingen he learned both the Ptolemaic and the Copernican systems from Michael Maestlin, became a Copernican, and earned a reputation as a skilful astrologer by casting horoscopes for fellow students.

In Graz, between 1594 and 1600, the calendars and prognostications he issued as mathematics teacher made his name as an astrologer. He then worked in Prague as assistant to [Tycho Brahe](/astrology/tycho-brahe/), and became imperial mathematician to Rudolf II and to his two successors, Matthias and Ferdinand II; he later taught in Linz and advised General Wallenstein. Sir Oliver Lodge summed up the bargain: Kepler was “continually attacking and throwing sarcasm at astrology, but it was the only thing for which people would pay him, and on it after a fashion he lived”. He died in Regensburg on 15 November 1630.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He looked for a physical reason. Like [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/), he treated astrology as the counterpart of astronomy, and in his treatise of 1601 on astrology's firmer foundations he gave the Earth a soul with a sense of geometry. As a shepherd enjoys the piping of a flute without knowing the theory of harmony, the Earth responds to the angles the planets make, without being conscious of them. In *Harmonices Mundi* (1619) he derived the aspects from harmony, and that is where the [quintile](/astrology/quintile/), the [biquintile](/astrology/biquintile/) and the [sesquiquadrate](/astrology/sesquiquadrate/) come from: angles Ptolemy never listed, which I read today as minor aspects. The fourth book of that work is about harmonic configurations in astrology; the fifth digresses on astrology at length before it states his third law of planetary motion.

He refused to throw it all out. After the supernova of 1604, Kepler traded published attacks over astrology with the astrologer Helisaeus Roeslin, while the physician Philip Feselius dismissed astrology altogether, Roeslin's above all. Kepler's answer, *Tertius Interveniens* (1610), the “third-party intervention”, was offered to the patron Roeslin and Feselius shared, and carried a long German title warning theologians, physicians and philosophers, Feselius above all, not to throw out the child with the bath while rightly condemning the star-gazers' superstition.

He asked for all the data. In his book on the new star of 1604, he faulted Roeslin for building his astrological forecasts on just two comets, those of 1556 and 1580, and later remarked that with a broader range of data Roeslin could have made a better argument. It is the objection now called [confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/), and it is why my own test counted every placement, misses included ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

He trusted the Moon with the sea. He wrote that moist things swell with the waxing [Moon](/astrology/moon/), and later that the Moon draws the Earth's waters by a force like magnetism. Galileo scorned him for lending his assent “to the moon's dominion over the waters, to occult properties, and to such puerilities”; Kepler pointed out that Galileo's own theory could not explain why the tides keep time with the Moon's phases.

He looked for the great conjunctions in history. Studying the conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 1603, he calculated a triple conjunction of the two in 7 BC and suggested it might have been the Star of Bethlehem ([Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/)).

And he left the tables. The Rudolphine Tables, built on Tycho's observations, were finished in 1623 and printed in 1627, and ephemerides were computed from them ([ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)).

His century's astrology is told in [Renaissance astrology](/astrology/renaissance-astrology/). The best-known name in prediction from the generation before him belonged to a man who worked very differently, and who refused the title of prophet: [Nostradamus](/astrology/nostradamus/).
