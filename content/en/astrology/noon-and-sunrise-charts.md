When the time of birth is unknown, a chart still has to be cast for some minute. Noon and sunrise are the usual stand-ins: one splits the difference, the other borrows the Sun.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A noon chart is cast for 12:00 on the day of birth. The reasoning is that whatever time you were born, noon is never more than twelve hours away, so the planets can't have travelled far from where the chart puts them.

A sunrise chart is cast for the moment the Sun rose on the day of birth, at the birthplace. At sunrise the Sun stands on the eastern horizon, which is exactly where the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) is measured, so the [Sun](/astrology/sun/) sits on the Ascendant and the rising sign is the Sun sign, by construction.

Neither is a birth chart. Both answer the question "what time shall we use?" with a convention, and the convention then appears in the chart as if it were a fact.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

Often without anyone deciding. Leave the time blank in many programs and they quietly use noon, or another default, and still draw a rising sign and twelve houses. Before reading anything into a chart's angles, check what it has assumed.

What a noon chart gets roughly right is what moves slowly. The Sun moves about a degree a day, so it lands within half a degree; the planets rarely cover more than two degrees a day, so their signs are usually right, wrong only on the days one of them changes sign. The [Moon](/astrology/moon/) is the difficulty. It covers between about 11¾° and 15⅓° a day, so a noon chart can put it nearly 8° from where it really was: enough to make or unmake its aspects, to move it across a phase boundary ([Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/)) and, on a little under half of all days, enough to put it in the wrong sign, since that's how often it changes sign between one midnight and the next ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)).

Everything that hangs on the time is filled in by the convention: the Ascendant, the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/), the twelve houses, the [Part of Fortune](/astrology/part-of-fortune/). In a sunrise chart they're built around the Sun, so they come out the same for everyone born that day in that place.

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

A noon chart shows, fairly well, where the planets were on the date of your birth: the sky of a day. A sunrise chart shows the same sky arranged around a convention. Astrologers who use them can describe what a date holds, the Sun's sign and the slow planets', as long as they say clearly what's missing.

I don't read from either. The parts of a chart that make it yours, the rising sign and the houses, are exactly the parts these charts invent, and a reading built on the planets alone would leave out the house each one speaks from, which is where a placement becomes personal. No birth time, no reading.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

A guess drawn as a chart looks as precise as any other: degrees, minutes, neat house cusps. Nothing on the page tells you that the angles were never measured.

The two conventions also disagree. For the same person, a noon chart and a sunrise chart give different angles and houses, and neither has a better claim than the other. Astrologers who want more than a convention turn to [Rectification](/astrology/rectification/), which estimates a time from the events of a life; that too yields a hypothesis rather than a record.

The better route, when it's open, is the record itself: the time is written down more often than people expect, on a full birth certificate in some countries, in a hospital file, in a parent's memory ([How to find your birth time](/astrology/how-to-find-your-birth-time/)).
