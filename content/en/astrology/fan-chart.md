Chart shapes look at the whole wheel at once: not which planets aspect which, but how the ten of them are spread. Fan is one of the names given to a shape, and it's the one on these pages I can say least about, for a reason worth explaining.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

[Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) named seven shapes: the bundle, the bowl, the bucket, the seesaw, the locomotive, the splay and the splash. The fan isn't one of them, and I haven't found a description of it in a source I could check. So I won't draw one. A picture I made up would look every bit as convincing as a real definition, and you'd have no way to tell the two apart.

What I can give you are the shapes that do have rules, because whatever a program calls a fan, your planets fit one of them or none. The count behind this site measures the smallest arc of the wheel that holds all ten planets. Within 120°, the chart is a [bundle](/astrology/bundle-chart/), about 1 person in 56; within 180°, a [bowl](/astrology/bowl-chart/), about 1 in 9; within 240°, a [locomotive](/astrology/locomotive-chart/), about 2 in 5. Nine planets within 180° and the tenth outside make a [bucket](/astrology/bucket-chart/), about 1 in 3. The other three of Jones's shapes, the seesaw, the [splay](/astrology/splay-chart/) and the splash, have no count, because authors draw them differently.

A shape needs no aspects at all. Two planets at the edges of a crowd may make an aspect or not; the shape is only about spacing, which is why it's read differently from figures like the grand trine.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Without a rule, there is nothing to count: a figure for the fan would only measure whatever definition I had chosen for it. So this page gives none, as for the seesaw, the splay and the splash.

How I read a shape doesn't depend on its name. The system I built for my readings treats patterns as emphasis that emerges from repetition, never as a fixed identity, and no single factor decides a chart. Where the planets crowd together is where attention gathers. The empty part of the wheel is what the chart keeps facing, the area of life it reaches towards rather than lives in. A planet standing apart from the rest earns a second look, never a verdict. None of this outweighs the planets themselves: a shape says how the ten are distributed, and their signs, houses and aspects say what each one does.

Most of a shape is there without the birth time, since the planets' signs need only the date, apart from the Moon's on the day it changes sign. Its houses are another matter. Whether the crowd sits above or below the horizon, east or west of the meridian, is [hemisphere emphasis](/astrology/hemisphere-emphasis/), and only the time places the horizon. The Moon, which moves about 13° a day, can also stretch the empty arc or close it. That's why [I wait for the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) before reading any shape, and why [how I read](/how-i-read/) starts with the planets.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Ask what your program means.** Its help pages or settings may define the fan. If they don't, treat the word as a nickname, not a finding.
- **Measure the arc.** Find the smallest stretch of the wheel that holds all ten planets. It tells you at once whether one of the counted shapes applies.
- **Find the gap.** Note where the empty part of the wheel lies, and which planets stand at its two edges.
- **Read the planets before the outline.** Ten placements, each in its sign and house, say far more than any silhouette.

The fan closes this family of shapes with a name and no rule I could check. The ten planets are still exactly where they are, and they're where any reading starts.
