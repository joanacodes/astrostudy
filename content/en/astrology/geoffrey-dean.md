Geoffrey Dean came to astrology's evidence from the inside: a former astrologer and a scientist, he spent decades collecting the tests of astrology and running his own. What he and the psychologist Ivan Kelly found is plain. Under controlled conditions, astrology did not hold up.

## Who {#who}

He is described as a scientist and former astrologer. In 1977 he published *Recent Advances in Natal Astrology*, a critical review of the research from 1900 to 1976. Papers followed, among them “Can astrology predict E and N?” in *Correlation* (1985), “Does astrology need to be true?” in the *Skeptical Inquirer* (1987) and, with A. Mather, “Is the scientific approach relevant to astrology?” (1994).

Much of his work is shared. With Ivan Kelly and D. H. Saklofske he wrote “Astrology: A Critical Review” for a philosophy-of-science textbook in 1990; with Kelly, “Is Astrology Relevant to Consciousness and Psi?” for the *Journal of Consciousness Studies* in 2003; with David Nias, a chapter on astrology and parapsychology for a book on the psychologist Hans Eysenck. His reviews reach beyond astrology: Barry and Dale Beyerstein's *The Write Stuff* (1992), on handwriting analysis, summarises his survey of some 200 studies of graphology and carries his chapter on effect size. He has also argued his case in public with astrologers, Dennis Elwell among them.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He tested the astrologer as well as the chart. In one study, 45 astrologers who rated themselves confident, with ten years' experience on average, judged 160 people chosen because they scored at the extremes of the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire. They did much worse than a simple judgement by age, and much worse than 45 people who used no charts. In a larger test, of more than a hundred cognitive, behavioural, physical and other variables, he and Kelly found no support for astrology at all. Their 2003 paper also pooled 40 studies, with 700 astrologers and more than 1,000 birth charts: where astrologers had to pick the right interpretation of a chart from several, none was preferred once the date and other obvious clues were taken away.

He measured agreement. Across 25 studies that he and Kelly documented, astrologers reading the same charts agreed with one another at a low 0.1, on a scale where 1 would mean complete agreement and 0 none. That finding matters as much as any other: before asking whether astrology is right, it asks whether astrologers say the same thing.

He looked for ordinary explanations of striking results. Faced with [the Mars effect (Gauquelin)](/astrology/mars-effect-gauquelin/), he proposed that some parents had reported birth times to suit their beliefs, which [Michel Gauquelin](/astrology/michel-gauquelin/)'s later samples, with birth times recorded by a nurse or doctor, would not show. With Kelly he studied people born within minutes of each other, using the astrologer John Addey's criteria ([Time twins: Dean and Kelly](/astrology/time-twins-dean-and-kelly/)), and the review he wrote with Kelly and Saklofske sets out Addey's harmonic analysis of Gauquelin's data in detail, with illustrations, and regrets that Addey's death cut it short ([Harmonic charts](/astrology/harmonic-charts/)). His tests are demanding, and they often start from astrologers' own claims and criteria.

The research stands, and I'm not going to argue it away ([Does astrology work?](/astrology/does-astrology-work/); [Carlson's double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/)). My notebook is a different and much smaller thing: a tick or a cross beside each placement, for my chart and, with their consent, for family, friends and strangers. About a third held, 30 to 40%; Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90%. It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). What it changed is how I read: when something rests on a placement that held less often, I say so.

[Chris Brennan](/astrology/chris-brennan/) asks what astrologers believed and practised. Dean asked whether it works, and the tests he gathered and ran answered no.
