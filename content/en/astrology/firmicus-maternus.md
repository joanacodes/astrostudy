Firmicus Maternus wrote the longest book of astrology the Roman world left in Latin, and about ten years later a book urging the emperors to stamp out the old religions. For a long time readers refused to believe one man had written both.

## Who {#who}

He lived under Constantine and his sons, in the fourth century, and the scholars who edited his Christian work describe a triple career: advocate in the courts, astrologer, and finally Christian apologist. He had a classical, pagan education that gave him Greek as well as Latin. In the opening of his astrology he says he was born in Sicily, and the manuscripts call him “the Sicilian” or “the younger”.

The *[Mathesis](/astrology/firmicus-maternus-mathesis/)*, eight books in Latin, was written around 334 to 337; Theodor Mommsen fixed it at 336, against an older date of 354. About 346 came *De errore profanarum religionum*, “On the error of profane religions”, addressed to Constantius II and Constans, two of Constantine's sons. In it he mocks the rites of the old cults and asks the emperors to put an end to them as a sacred duty.

In the nineteenth century the two books looked so unlike each other that most readers gave them to two different authors. In 1897 Clifford Herschel Moore compared their habits of vocabulary and syntax and showed that they shared one. Since the astrology came first, readers who hold astrology and Christianity to be incompatible conclude that he converted between the two books. I'd leave his inner life where the evidence leaves it: unknown. Whether astrology and faith can sit together is a question people still ask me, and it has a page of its own ([Is astrology a sin?](/astrology/is-astrology-a-sin/)).

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He gave the Latin West its handbook. What Greek authors had worked out, Firmicus set down in Latin at full length, and his modern editors count the *Mathesis* among the last extensive handbooks of astrology circulating in the West before Arabic texts arrived in the twelfth century. Between him and those translations, Latin astrology had little else on that scale ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)).

He kept older authors' names alive. It's through him that we know [Dorotheus of Sidon](/astrology/dorotheus-of-sidon/) came from Sidon: he says so in his second book. And Wolfgang Hübner has argued that some of the material attributed to Teucer of Babylon, the Hellenistic astrologer of the constellations rising with the zodiac, had already been used by Firmicus.

His century also produced the best-known form of an old objection. Augustine, who consulted astrologers in his youth and later turned against them, objected on two grounds: astrologers treated the planets as divinities, and, on reason alone, twins born almost together so often lead different lives. Two charts drawn minutes apart differ at the edges, on the horizon and the house cusps, since four minutes move the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) by about a degree; whether that is enough to account for two lives is a question my notebook can't settle, and the modern version has been tested ([Time twins: Dean and Kelly](/astrology/time-twins-dean-and-kelly/)). It is one more reason I start from an exact birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

The *Mathesis* is still read: W. Kroll and F. Skutsch edited the Latin for the Teubner series, and it has had two English translations, in 1975 and 2011.

He also stands at the start of a long wait. After him, the Latin West had to wait centuries for new books on the subject; when they came, in the twelfth century, they were translations from Arabic, by astrologers such as [Masha’allah](/astrology/mashaallah/) who had carried the Greek tradition into that language. Firmicus belongs to the Latin end of [Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/).
