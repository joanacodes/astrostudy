Somewhere between your eighteenth and nineteenth birthdays, the Moon's nodes arrive back at the exact degrees they held when you were born. They will do it again in your late thirties, your mid-fifties and your seventies. That arrival is the nodal return.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The North and South Nodes are the two points where the Moon's path crosses the Sun's, always exactly opposite each other: the [nodal axis](/astrology/nodal-axis/). They slide backwards through the zodiac and take about 18.6 years to go all the way round. Each time they complete the circle, the transiting [North Node](/astrology/north-node/) sits on your natal North Node and the transiting South Node on your natal South Node. The two return together, because they never part.

Halfway round, between two returns, the axis lies the other way, the North Node over your natal South Node. That is the [nodal reversal](/astrology/nodal-reversal/), at about 9, 28 and 46. Read together, the returns and reversals make a slow rhythm through a life, one beat about every nine years.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The ages come straight from the nodes' speed. Computed from the ephemeris for 160 birth dates, the first return fell between 18.6 and 18.8 years of age in every one of them. For someone born in 1965, the returns came at 18.6, 37.2, 55.7 and 74.3: about 18½, 37, 56 and 74, as everywhere on this site.

Which node you follow changes the shape of the return, not its age ([mean node and true node](/astrology/mean-node-and-true-node/)):

- **The mean node** moves backwards at an even pace, so it crosses your natal degree once, on a single date.
- **The true node** wobbles around the mean one, so it can pass your natal degree, pull back, and pass it again. The return then reads as a stretch of time with more than one exact crossing.

I look at both: the mean node's date says when the return is exact on average, and the true node's passes say how long the season runs.

The nodes also carry the eclipses. An eclipse happens when a new or full moon falls near the nodes, so in the season of a return the eclipses fall in the same pair of signs as around your birth, and in the same pair of your houses. That is part of why the return is read as the chart lining up with itself.

The age needs nothing but the birth date. The houses, the angles the nodes cross on their way back, and the houses the eclipses light up all depend on the birth time; [without it](/blog/unknown-birth-time/), there's nothing in the chart to set the return against, and I don't read a chart that way.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In a forecast, the true node is one of the bodies whose transits I follow, so its return is part of the timetable I read. I read it as weather: a season in which the question your nodes describe, between what comes easily and what you're still learning, tends to come back to the surface.

What I look at:

- **The houses of your natal nodes:** the areas of life where the familiar and the new pull against each other, and so where the question tends to show.
- **What else is moving:** a return that coincides with a slower transit is read with it, never on its own.
- **Where you were at the last one:** eighteen and a half years is long enough to compare. Many people find it useful to look back at the season of their previous return and ask what has changed since.

The first return falls close to the start of adult life, the second in the middle years, the third and fourth later on; I read each in the life of the adult in front of me, never as a stage everyone must pass in the same way. A return describes a season. It doesn't promise a move, a meeting or a break, and I don't read it as one. [How I read](/how-i-read/) explains the rest of the forecast.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A nodal return is a fated turning point.”** The nodes describe direction, not destiny. The return asks the question again; it doesn't answer it for you.
- **“It happens on one exact day.”** With the mean node, the crossing has a date. With the true node, it can come in more than one pass, which is why I read a season.
- **“Everyone your age has it at once.”** The age is nearly the same for everyone, about 18.6 years, but the dates follow each person's birth date.
- **“The second return repeats the first.”** The sky lines up the same way. You don't: the same question meets a different life.
