In the ancient world, Medusa's face was put on shields and roofs to turn harm away. The asteroid named after her is a small, quiet rock that once held two records without anyone knowing.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Asteroid 149 Medusa is a stony body about 20 km across, near the inner edge of the main belt, a little over twice the Earth's distance from the Sun. It goes round in about 3.2 years, on an orbit that is nearly round and almost flat to the Sun's path, and it turns slowly, once in about 26 hours. Henri Joseph Perrotin found it on 21 September 1875.

It held two records at once. It was by far the smallest asteroid yet found, though no one could measure that at the time, and it was the closest to the Sun, a place it took from Flora and kept until 1898, when [Eros](/astrology/eros/) and Hungaria were found closer in.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Medusa was one of three Gorgon sisters, daughters of the sea powers Phorcys and Ceto, and the only one who could die. Living snakes grew in place of her hair, and whoever looked at her was turned to stone. Perseus, sent for her head by a king who wanted to marry his mother, was equipped by the gods: a polished shield from Athena, winged sandals from Hermes, the helmet of invisibility from Hades. He beheaded her while watching her reflection, and from her body sprang the winged horse Pegasus and the giant Chrysaor. Her head kept its power, and Athena set it on her shield; the goddess's asteroid is [Pallas](/astrology/pallas/).

Her name means guardian, and her face did a guardian's work: as the gorgoneion it was painted and carved to keep evil away. In the fifth century BC artists began to show her beautiful as well as terrible, and Pindar was already writing of "fair-cheeked Medusa".

The version most people know is Ovid's, and it's late. In it Medusa is a beautiful young woman; Neptune takes her in Minerva's temple, and it is Medusa the goddess punishes, turning her hair to snakes. Ovid tells [Arachne](/astrology/arachne/)'s story two books later, and has her weave Neptune's disguises into her tapestry, Medusa's among them. In the twentieth century feminist writers read Medusa again, as a woman punished for what was done to her, and made her face an image of women's anger. In ordinary speech her name had gone the way of [Kassandra](/astrology/kassandra/)'s, into a word: a Medusa is a monster.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read Medusa, and with a myth like hers I'm glad not to have to. It holds a violation, a punishment that fell on the wrong person, and a killing presented as a hero's errand, and none of it should be pointed at a reader. A chart can't say that something was done to you, or what you ought to feel about it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). If you're carrying harm that someone caused, a qualified professional is the person to talk to ([When to see a professional instead](/astrology/when-to-see-a-professional-instead/)).

Her oldest use is the one I'd keep: a face on a shield that holds fear at a distance. A reading should leave you less afraid than it found you.

The asteroid also has no record with me. My notebook checked the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith, and Medusa never appeared in it ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)); the system behind my readings doesn't compute her either. If you place her yourself, keep her to tight contacts with the planets and read her last ([How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Medusa was always a monster.”** Early art made her monstrous, but by the fifth century BC she was also shown beautiful; the beautiful girl turned Gorgon is Ovid's, centuries later.
- **“Medusa was immortal.”** She was the only mortal Gorgon. Her sisters, Stheno and Euryale, could not die.
- **“The asteroid Medusa is a big, menacing rock.”** It's one of the smaller named asteroids, about 20 km across, on a quiet, nearly round orbit inside the main belt.
