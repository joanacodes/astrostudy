For several centuries Latin Europe had little astrology to work with. Then the Arabic books crossed the Pyrenees, and within a hundred years astrology was being taught, consulted by princes, used by doctors and argued over by theologians.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

Early medieval writers knew the subject mostly at second hand. In the seventh century Isidore of Seville split it in two: the part that describes the movements of the Sun, Moon and stars, which he accepted, and the part that predicts, which he judged theologically wrong. Historians describe the Latin astrology of those centuries as fragmented, partly through the loss of Greek astronomy and partly through the Church's condemnations.

The change came through Spain. After Toledo was taken by Christian forces in 1085, its cathedral library became a centre for translating Arabic into Latin. John of Seville put Abu Ma'shar's *Great Introduction* into Latin in 1133, and Herman of Carinthia made a freer, shorter version in 1140. In Barcelona in 1138, Plato of Tivoli translated [Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/) from its Arabic version, the first complete introduction of Ptolemy's astrology to medieval Europe. Gerard of Cremona, the most productive of the Toledo translators, added the *Almagest* in 1175, among 87 books.

Astrologers found employers. Michael Scot, who had learned Arabic at Toledo, served the emperor Frederick II as court astrologer in Palermo. [Guido Bonatti](/astrology/guido-bonatti/), the most celebrated astrologer of the thirteenth century, advised Frederick II and the governments of Florence, Siena and Forlì, and his *Liber astronomiae*, written around 1277, stayed a standard textbook for two centuries ([Guido Bonatti’s Book of Astronomy](/astrology/guido-bonattis-book-of-astronomy/)). Dante put both men in hell: Bonatti walks among the diviners, his head turned backwards.

The theologians drew a line. Thomas Aquinas, following Aristotle, held that the stars ruled the imperfect body while God ruled the soul. The *Speculum astronomiae*, written after 1260 and usually attributed to Albertus Magnus, though its authorship is debated, went further: knowing the stars helps a person resist the bodily impulses they stir, so astrology perfects free will rather than denying it. In Paris in 1277, Bishop Étienne Tempier condemned 219 propositions, a good number of them aimed at astrology, because foreknowledge seemed to leave no room for choice. In 1322 Cecco d'Ascoli became professor of astrology at Bologna; in 1327 he was burned at Florence, condemned for heresy. In the fourteenth century Nicole Oresme granted that predicting plagues, wars and weather was a valid field of inquiry, however badly practised, but rejected elections and questions as wholly false.

Medicine was where astrology lived every day. By the thirteenth century it was part of ordinary medical practice, and the Zodiac Man, a body with Aries at the head and Pisces at the feet, filled calendars and books of hours; physicians avoided letting blood from a part of the body whose sign held the Moon ([the signs and the body, in old medicine](/astrology/signs-and-the-body-in-old-medicine/)). When the Black Death came, the medical faculty of Paris blamed a conjunction of planets for “a great pestilence in the air”.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

It gave astrology a Latin vocabulary and a place in the universities, where Ptolemy was taught alongside medicine. It put the Arabic techniques into Latin, the judging of questions among them ([horary astrology](/astrology/horary-astrology/)). It produced a house system of its own, named after Campanus of Novara, although the method was known earlier in the East ([Campanus](/astrology/campanus/)).

Its most lasting gift was the line between body and will. A chart that inclines without compelling is still how I read: tendencies, not fate, and you stay in charge of your choices ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

What I leave with the physicians is their diagnosis. Old texts gave the [6th house](/astrology/6th-house/) to illness and the signs to parts of the body, and the history is worth knowing ([medical astrology, in history](/astrology/medical-astrology-in-history/)). I don't read a chart for health, and a medical question belongs with a doctor ([can astrology predict health problems?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-health-problems/)).

In fifteenth-century Florence the debate took a new turn, with a priest who prescribed by the planets and a young nobleman who wrote against them: [Renaissance astrology](/astrology/renaissance-astrology/). The books behind all of it had come from Baghdad ([Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/)).
