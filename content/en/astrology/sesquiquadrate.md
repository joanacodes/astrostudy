The sesquiquadrate is a square and a half: two planets 135° apart, three-eighths of the way round. It describes a tension with two layers to it, which rarely gives way to a quick fix; what it asks for is a rebuild.

## What it means {#what-it-means}

Add a semi-square of 45° to a [square](/astrology/square/) of 90° and you reach 135°, three-eighths of the circle. That's four and a half signs, so the pair of signs changes halfway through a sign: a planet in its first fifteen degrees makes the aspect to the sign four on, one of the same element; a planet in its second half, to the sign five on, which has nothing in common with its own. Either way, it's the angle you read, not the signs.

I read it as tension compounded, and constructive. Two planets press on each other in a way that stacks one difficulty on another, so the obvious adjustment doesn't hold, and the work is to restructure: to change the arrangement around both planets rather than a single habit. Two layers of tension still don't add up to a failure, a punishment or a crisis; they add up to work.

It's the odd one out of the trio that Johannes Kepler brought in alongside Ptolemy's aspects, in *Harmonices Mundi* (Linz, 1619): the other two, the [quintile](/astrology/quintile/) and the [biquintile](/astrology/biquintile/), divide the circle by five, while the sesquiquadrate divides it by eight. Its orb stays small, a degree or two either side (see [orbs](/astrology/orbs/)).

Two planets moving apart from a [conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/) pass the [sextile](/astrology/sextile/), the square and the [trine](/astrology/trine/) before they reach 135°, and the [opposition](/astrology/opposition/) still lies 45° ahead. Its nearest major neighbour, 15° back, is the trine, the most fluent of them; the sesquiquadrate is anything but. As I read it, a square meets you head on; a sesquiquadrate comes at an odd angle, from further round, which is partly why it's harder to see coming.

## Between which planets {#between-which-planets}

No pair of planets has a sesquiquadrate page of its own here; what tells me most is how fast the two move.

When both are personal planets, the Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus or Mars, it shows in arrangements you can change: a routine, a way of working, a habit of speech that keeps tying the same knot until you rebuild it. With a slow planet on one side, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune or Pluto, the personal planet is asked over years to reorganise itself around a larger force, and that's the version I read most carefully. Between two slow planets it belongs to a whole generation, and the houses show where that generation's structures want rebuilding in your own life.

The [semi-square](/astrology/semi-square/), at 45°, is the same tension in a single layer, a local rub that a small correction settles; the [quincunx](/astrology/quincunx/), 15° further on at 150°, has no friction in it at all, only a mismatch to adjust to.

Two planets in square that both make a sesquiquadrate to a third, 135° from each, form the pattern astrologers call [Thor's hammer](/astrology/thors-hammer/). The third planet carries much of the rebuilding: demanding work, and never a blow of fate.

## The sesquiquadrate in synastry {#in-synastry}

Across two charts, the sesquiquadrate rarely sparks or clashes in the open; more often it's an arrangement that keeps not quite working: whose space is whose, whose timetable wins, whose way of doing things becomes the default. Patching it in the moment seldom holds; changing the arrangement itself, together, is what it asks for. In my [synastry readings](/synastry/) it's a place where two people may rebuild something, read from both sides, and never a reason to part.

## The sesquiquadrate in transits {#in-transits}

Transiting sesquiquadrates from the fast planets pass in hours or days and rarely register. From Jupiter and Saturn they stay for weeks; from Uranus, Neptune and Pluto, for months, often in three passes as those planets turn retrograde and direct again. They tend to come with the sense that an arrangement has stopped fitting: a structure to rebuild rather than a decision to take. Weather, not a forecast: a sesquiquadrate transit names the pressure to rebuild, not what you'll build.
