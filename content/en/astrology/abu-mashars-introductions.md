[Abu Ma’shar](/astrology/abu-mashar/) wrote his introduction to astrology twice: once at full length, as the *Great Introduction*, around 848, and once in brief. They are manuals for training astrologers rather than books of new ideas, and that is what made them travel, through translations, from the Islamic world to Byzantium and western Europe.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The long book is the *Kitāb al-madkhal al-kabīr*, the *Great Introduction to the Science of Astrology*, written around 848, some fifteen years after Abu Ma’shar had turned to astrology. Latin readers got it twice within seven years: a close translation by John of Seville in 1133, under the title *Introductorium in astronomiam*, and a looser abridgement by Herman of Carinthia in 1140. Nothing on its scale had circulated in Latin since [Firmicus Maternus’ Mathesis](/astrology/firmicus-maternus-mathesis/), eight centuries earlier ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)). Byzantium had it in Greek, and when it was printed at Venice in 1506 the Latin ran to eight books.

The short book is the *Kitāb mukhtaṣar al-madkhal*, the *Abbreviation of the Introduction*, Abu Ma’shar's own abridgement of the long one, which Adelard of Bath put into Latin; its Latin title is *Ysagoga minor*, the lesser introduction.

Both now have modern editions. Richard Lemay published the Arabic of the long book with both Latin versions in nine volumes, in Naples, in 1995 and 1996; Keiji Yamamoto and Charles Burnett followed in 2019 with the Arabic and an English translation. The *Abbreviation* appeared in Arabic and Latin in 1994, edited by Burnett, Yamamoto and Michio Yano, and Burnett translated it into English the same year. The world the books came from, the Abbasid court and its Persian learning, is described in [Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/).

## What it says {#what-it-says}

Neither book travels light. The *Abbreviation* alone sets out no fewer than fifty-five lots, points calculated from three parts of the chart, and these are only the ones Abu Ma’shar counted as significant ([Doctrine of the lots](/astrology/doctrine-of-the-lots/)). Most lots are counted from the Ascendant, so a lot shifts with the minute of birth just as the Ascendant does, which is why the time comes before any of them ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

The long book sets traditions side by side. Its sixth book gives the images of the decans, the thirds of each sign, as Greek, Indian and Persian authorities described them, and some of the Persian figures still carry Persian names ([The Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)).

The *Great Introduction* also sets out the [firdaria](/astrology/firdaria/), the periods of life ruled in turn by the planets and then by the Moon's nodes, 75 years in all. The nodes, Abu Ma’shar explains, are not divided into sub-periods as the planets are, because they have no signs of their own; in his fuller account they come last, at seventy to seventy-five. Four centuries later Guido Bonatti described the same periods less explicitly, and his later readers disagreed over where the nodes go in a night chart ([Guido Bonatti’s Book of Astronomy](/astrology/guido-bonattis-book-of-astronomy/)).

The nodes need that care in any chart. They are calculated points, not bodies, and two ways of calculating them, the mean and the true, can disagree by a degree or two. I look at both ([Mean node and true node](/astrology/mean-node-and-true-node/)).

The introductions also carry a view of the stars that I don't share. In the historian J. R. Veenstra's account, the *Introductorium* and Abu Ma’shar's book on conjunctions argued that both individual actions and the larger course of history are determined by the stars ([Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/)). In 1411 the friar Laurens Pignon wrote his *Contre les devineurs* in opposition to that tradition. I'd put it differently from both of them: a chart shows how you tend to move, not where you must end up, and the choices stay yours ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).
