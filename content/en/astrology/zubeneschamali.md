Zubeneschamali is the northern claw of the old Scorpion and the brightest star of Libra, though Bayer lettered it Beta. It is also the star that older observers called green, the only one they could see so without a telescope. In the zodiac it stands at 19°44′ Scorpio.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Zubeneschamali is Beta Librae, magnitude 2.61 against 2.75 for [Zubenelgenubi](/astrology/zubenelgenubi/), the constellation's Alpha. It is a hot star about 185 light-years away, some 410 times as luminous as the Sun and spinning over a hundred times faster. Its tropical position was 18°23′ [Scorpio](/astrology/scorpio/) for a birth in 1930, 19°22′ in 2000 and 19°44′ in 2026.

The zodiac's shuffle of names shows well here. The stars of the constellation Libra now sit in the sign of Scorpio, while [Vindemiatrix](/astrology/vindemiatrix/), a star of Virgo, sits in Libra at 10°18′ ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).

Its latitude is 8.5° north of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), just outside the belt of about 8° either side that the zodiac was drawn to cover. The Moon never strays that far, so it never passes in front of Zubeneschamali as it can in front of its southern partner ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

In Arabic it is al-zubānā al-šamāliyy, the northern claw, from the time when these stars were the Scorpion's claws; reading them as a balance gave it Kiffa Borealis, the northern pan, and the Latin Lanx Borealis. Babylonian astronomers already knew the constellation both ways, as the Claws of the Scorpion and as the Scales, which were held sacred to Shamash, the sun god and patron of truth and justice. With Zubenelgenubi, the star belongs to the Arabic mansion of the Two Claws and to Vishakha in India, and Chinese astronomy counts it as the fourth star of the Root.

Two puzzles come with it. Eratosthenes recorded it as brighter than [Antares](/astrology/antares/); Ptolemy, some 350 years later, called it as bright. Antares may have brightened since, or the difference may lie in Zubeneschamali's own slight variability: nobody knows. And while modern eyes usually see it as white or bluish, earlier observers often called it the only greenish star visible to the naked eye, a green no one has explained to general agreement.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

By the tradition's rule, Zubeneschamali touches a planet or angle within about a degree of its longitude: 18°44′ to 20°44′ Scorpio for a birth in 2026, 17°23′ to 19°23′ for one in 1930 ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). The Sun crosses that span on about 11 and 12 November. Neptune held it for births in parts of 1964–66, and [Pluto in Scorpio](/astrology/pluto-in-scorpio/) at intervals from late November 1990 to August 1992, so the contact belongs to whole stretches of birth years. For the Moon, which passes south of the star in a few hours each month, the answer is in the birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Stars don't enter my own readings ([How I read](/how-i-read/)), so from Zubeneschamali I pass on its two puzzles, an old brightness and a green no one can account for, rather than a meaning.
