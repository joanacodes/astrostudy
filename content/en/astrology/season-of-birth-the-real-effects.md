Here, for once, the research does find something. The time of year you were born leaves small traces that show up across thousands of people. They just don't follow the signs.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Yes, there are real effects, and they are small and collective. The clearest is the relative age effect: in a school year or a sports age group, the oldest children tend to be selected more, and to do better, than the youngest. Others are statistical links between birth month and some health conditions, found in very large medical records. None of them is a Sun-sign effect: they move when the calendar moves, and they say nothing certain about any one person.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**The relative age effect.** Children are grouped by year, so a cut-off date decides who is oldest. The IOC, FIFA and the football confederations use 1 January; in the UK, 1 September is common. In the upper levels of youth sport and in academic selection, those born soon after the cut-off are over-represented. The best evidence that this comes from the calendar, not the sky, is what happens when the date moves: Werner Helsen, Janet Starkes and Jan Van Winckel studied male football players across a change in the selection year (2000), and research on the effect finds that the advantage shifts with the selection date. Researchers keep it apart from season of birth proper, which looks at conditions during pregnancy and around birth, such as sunlight, temperature or infections.

**At school.** In Britain, where the school year begins in September, children born in the autumn term have been found to do better than their classmates born in spring, who in turn outperform those born in summer, as Russell and Startup reported in 1986. The gap narrows with age: the effect fades after the middle school years, and at university those born later in the year do better.

**In medical records.** Birth month shows up faintly in health too. A 2015 study of 1.7 million patients at Columbia University in New York linked it with neurological, respiratory, reproductive and cardiovascular conditions. A 2018 study of 10.5 million patients in the United States, South Korea and Taiwan looked for causes, and found links with school cut-off dates and, for atrial fibrillation, with exposure to fine air pollution in the first trimester. Season of birth has been linked with some psychiatric conditions as well, while a 2022 study of older adults found no effect on symptoms of depression or anxiety. These are differences in rates across populations, not forecasts for anyone ([Astrology and mental health](/astrology/astrology-and-mental-health/)), and I don't read a chart for health ([Can astrology predict health problems?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-health-problems/)).

**Why this isn't astrology.** Three things set these effects apart from the zodiac. They move with calendars: change the cut-off and the favoured months change. The ones that come from climate follow the seasons, and south of the equator the seasons are reversed: the start of [Aries](/astrology/aries/), at the March equinox, is spring in London and autumn in Sydney, while the sign is the same ([Tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/)). And they are averages over thousands of people, far too small to describe any one of them. Whether Sun signs go with personality is a separate question, with studies of its own ([Sun signs and personality: the studies](/astrology/sun-signs-and-personality-the-studies/)), and so is whether astrologers can tell people apart from their whole charts ([Vernon Clark's matching tests](/astrology/vernon-clarks-matching-tests/)).

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My notebook didn't count seasons; it went placement by placement. The [Sun](/astrology/sun/) sign, the placement that follows the calendar most closely, held about a third of the time, the same as my checks overall and close to what a flattering horoscope scores. Saturn, Lilith and Chiron, whose positions have nothing to do with the season you were born in, held 75 to 90% of the time ([The three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/)). It's a notebook, not a study: it proves nothing about astrology in general, and it can't tell a seasonal effect from a sign ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).
