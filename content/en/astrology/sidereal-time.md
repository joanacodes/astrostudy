A clock follows the Sun. To find the angles of a chart, an astrologer needs a second clock that follows the stars, and that clock runs about four minutes a day ahead of the first: sidereal time.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Earth turns once against the stars in 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. A solar day is longer because the Earth moves on along its orbit as it spins, about a degree a day, and has to turn a little further before the Sun comes back to the meridian. Counted against the stars, a year holds about 366¼ turns; counted against the Sun, 365¼.

Strictly, sidereal time is measured from the March equinox rather than from any star. It is the angle, along the [celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/), between your meridian and that point, written in hours, minutes and seconds, and it reads 0h when the equinox crosses your meridian. At any moment, then, it equals the [right ascension](/astrology/right-ascension/) on your meridian. The name comes from the Latin *sidus*, a star, though its zero is the very point the tropical zodiac starts from ([solstices and equinoxes](/astrology/solstices-and-equinoxes/)).

Each meridian has its own local sidereal time. Tables give it for Greenwich; going east, you add four minutes for each degree of longitude, and going west, you take them away.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The two clocks agree once a year. Around the September equinox, sidereal time reads close to 0h at local midnight; every day after, it is about four minutes further ahead, until by the March equinox it reads 12h at midnight. Two people born at the same clock time in the same town, one in March and one in September, have sidereal times twelve hours apart, and nearly opposite Ascendants.

Working a chart out by hand takes three steps:

1. **Convert the birth time to Universal Time,** with the time zone and any summer time in force that day ([time zones and daylight saving in charts](/astrology/time-zones-and-daylight-saving-in-charts/)).
2. **Find the sidereal time at Greenwich** for that moment. An ephemeris lists it for 0h or noon each day ([Ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)); add the time elapsed since then, plus about ten seconds an hour, the sidereal clock's gain.
3. **Correct for the longitude:** four minutes for every degree east of Greenwich, added, or west of it, taken away. The result is local sidereal time.

From local sidereal time, the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) follows directly. Add the latitude of the birthplace, and a table of houses gives the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) and the cusps ([Calculating a chart by hand](/astrology/calculating-a-chart-by-hand/)). The [East Point](/astrology/east-point/) needs sidereal time alone.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

You'll never see sidereal time in a reading, since software works it out in a moment, but it explains two things I care about. It turns about a degree every four minutes, so a birth time rounded to the half-hour can shift the angles by several degrees, and every house cusp with them. And it depends on where you were as much as on when: the same minute, a hundred kilometres further east or west, is a different sidereal time and a different set of houses.

That's why I ask for the town of birth along with the time, and why the time is something I can't do without ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). The planets would hardly notice a wrong hour. The angles and the houses, built on sidereal time, would.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Sidereal time belongs to sidereal astrology.”** It's used to calculate every chart, tropical ones included, and it is counted from the March equinox, the tropical zodiac's own zero ([sidereal zodiac](/astrology/sidereal-zodiac/)).
- **“A sidereal day lasts 24 hours.”** It lasts about 23 hours 56 minutes; the solar day is longer because the Earth moves on along its orbit while it spins.
- **“Sidereal time is an astrologers' invention.”** Astronomers long timed stars crossing the meridian by it, and almanacs still print it.
- **“Software has made it irrelevant.”** Software calculates it for you. The birth time and the place you give it still decide the result.
