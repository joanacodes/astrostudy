Proserpina is Persephone's Latin name, and the asteroid that bears it was found first, in 1853, decades before her Greek twin, [Persephone (asteroid 399)](/astrology/persephone-asteroid/). The word also turns up in some chart programs for something that isn't an asteroid at all, which is the first thing worth knowing about it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

26 Proserpina is a stony asteroid about 90 km across, in the main belt between Mars and Jupiter. Robert Luther found it on 5 May 1853. Its orbit is close to round, between 2.4 and 2.9 times the Earth's distance from the Sun, and one circuit takes about 4.3 years. Even its spin was hard to pin down: estimates published between 1979 and 2001 ranged from under 7 hours to over 12, before a study of 2008 settled on just over 13.

Like several asteroids of its time, it was given a symbol as well as a name, and the names then came mostly from Greek and Roman myth, with a preference for women's. Proserpina's symbol was a star inside a pomegranate, the fruit that tied her to the underworld. A year later, asteroid number 30 took the name of a muse: [Urania](/astrology/urania/), muse of astronomy.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Roman Proserpina came in two layers. An older Italic goddess, Libera, shared a temple on the Aventine Hill with [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/) and Liber from about 493 BC. Around 205 BC, near the end of the war with Carthage, Rome brought in the Greek cult of Mother and Daughter, with Greek priestesses to serve it, and Libera was identified with Proserpina. Her name is the Greek one reshaped in Latin, perhaps under the influence of *proserpere*, to creep forth, as young grain does.

The Latin poets retold her abduction in their own way. Ovid told it twice, in the *Metamorphoses* and in the *Fasti*. In Claudian's late version, Dis drives his chariot up out of Etna and seizes her by Lake Pergusa, near Enna in Sicily. In the Latin tellings she stays below for half of each year, and in Virgil's *Georgics* she is the one who, charmed by Orpheus's music, lets him lead Eurydice back to the living, as long as he doesn't look behind him.

The name causes a practical problem. Some astrologers also use Proserpina for a fictitious planet with a symbol of its own, unrelated to the asteroid; astrologers in Poland and the astrology software Urania give it a second symbol and identify it with the dwarf planet [Eris](/astrology/eris/). So a "Proserpina" on a printout may be asteroid 26, computed from its observed orbit, or a point that no telescope tracks. The program's list of bodies, or the number 26 beside the name, tells you which.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The underworld already has its seat in my readings, and it's a planet: [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/), named after the god who carried Proserpina off. Pluto is one of the planets I read, and it was on my invoice like all of them. In my notebook it wasn't one of [the three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/) far more often than the rest, Saturn, Chiron and Lilith, at 75 to 90%; overall, about a third of what I checked held.

Proserpina was never on that invoice. The notebook covered the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith, and the system I built for my readings computes no asteroid, so there is no figure to give, and none to borrow from Pluto: sharing a myth with a planet doesn't give an asteroid the planet's record ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

If you use Proserpina anyway, first make sure your program means the asteroid, then hold it to the usual discipline: read it only in a tight contact with a planet or an angle, give it a house only with a birth time ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)), and choose it before you look at the chart rather than because it lands somewhere striking ([How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Proserpina is a goddess with her own separate myth.”** She is Persephone under a Latin name, fused at Rome with an older goddess, Libera.
- **“The Proserpina in my chart must be the asteroid.”** It may be a fictitious planet that shares the name. Check the symbol and the number.
- **“She spent a third of the year below.”** That is the Greek hymn's count. The Latin versions, Ovid's among them, give half.
- **“Proserpina is a small Pluto.”** They share a myth and nothing more: Pluto had a line in my notebook and keeps a place in my readings; the asteroid never had either.
