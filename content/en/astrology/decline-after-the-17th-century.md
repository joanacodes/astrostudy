Astrology wasn't so much disproved at the end of the seventeenth century as left behind. Educated Europe stopped picturing the heavens and the earth as one interlocking whole, and astrology, which depended on that picture, found itself outside the walls.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

The new astronomy and physics, heliocentrism and Newton's mechanics among them, called astrology into question, and it lost its academic standing. Ptolemy's astrology had still been taught at some European universities into the seventeenth century, but by the middle of it the subject was struggling to keep its rank among the respected sciences. The historian Keith Thomas puts the change in a sentence: the world “was now a mechanism of infinite dimensions, from which the hierarchical subordination of earth to heaven had irrefutably disappeared”. He also notes how quietly it happened. Scarcely anyone attempted a serious refutation; by the eighteenth century the intellectual investment had simply been withdrawn. Another historian, Darrel Rutkin, adds that the precise contours of the story remain obscure.

Astrologers did try to hold on. In London, the Society of Astrologers, active from about 1647 to 1684, held feasts and paid for sermons presenting astrology as a lawful Christian study. The historian Michelle Pfeffer judges that the campaign ultimately failed.

France made the break in print. The *Connaissance des temps*, the first national astronomical ephemeris, appeared in 1679 and left astrology out, and Pierre Bayle's *Dictionnaire* of 1697 called the subject puerile. In England, even an almanac compiler, Richard Saunders, printed a derisive discourse on the invalidity of astrology.

Then came the joke everyone remembered. In January 1708 Jonathan Swift, writing as Isaac Bickerstaff, predicted that the almanac maker John Partridge would die on 29 March; on that date he published a letter confirming the death. Partridge, very much alive, protested in print, and the hoax followed him for the rest of his life.

What remained was a popular following, supported by cheap almanacs, and a handful of astrologers who kept the old books alive. John Whalley translated Ptolemy's *[Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)* into English in 1701, and Ebenezer Sibly and his brother published a corrected edition in 1786.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

A new planet, first. In 1781 William Herschel discovered [Uranus](/astrology/uranus/), the first planet no ancient astrologer had seen; [Neptune](/astrology/neptune/) followed in 1846 and [Pluto](/astrology/pluto/) in 1930. Astrologers later gave each of them rulership of a sign. Critics such as the astronomer Philippe Zarka point out that astrology claimed to work before these planets were known and added them after the fact, and the objection deserves a straight answer. Mine is to keep both sets of rulers: for Scorpio, Aquarius and Pisces I read both, the traditional ruler and the modern one ([traditional and modern rulers](/astrology/traditional-and-modern-rulers/)). How the newcomers got their names has a page of its own ([How the outer planets got their names](/astrology/how-the-outer-planets-got-their-names/)).

Then, the printed tables. Out of the almanac trade came the ephemerides astrologers still use ([ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)). From 1827 Robert Cross Smith, who wrote as Raphael, edited *The Prophetic Messenger*, which helped revive interest in astrology in the nineteenth century, and the ephemeris published under his name spread [Placidus](/astrology/placidus/) houses through the English-speaking world. That's one reason the house system I use is so widespread in English.

And a question that never went away: what does astrology rest on? Swift's satire and Bayle's contempt asked it rudely; my own answer began with a notebook ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

Astrology had left the universities with Lilly's generation ([Renaissance astrology](/astrology/renaissance-astrology/)). Its return came from an unexpected direction, a religious movement founded in New York in 1875: [the Theosophical revival](/astrology/theosophical-revival/).
