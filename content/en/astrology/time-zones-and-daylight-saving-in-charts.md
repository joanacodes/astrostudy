The time on a birth record is the time the town's clocks showed that day. Before a chart program can place a single planet, it has to turn that into Universal Time, and to do it, it needs to know what the clocks were doing then, which isn't always what they do now.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A time zone is a region that keeps one clock time, set as an offset from Universal Time, the old Greenwich Mean Time counted from midnight: Paris is an hour ahead of it in winter, New York five hours behind. One instant can therefore carry two dates, which is why the day the Sun changes sign can differ from one country to the next ([sign dates, and why they move](/astrology/sign-dates-and-why-they-move/)). Most offsets are whole hours, from twelve behind to fourteen ahead, but India keeps five and a half hours ahead and Nepal, since 1986, five and three quarters. Daylight saving, or summer time, moves the clocks on for part of the year, usually by an hour.

Zones follow politics as much as the Sun. Spain and France both lie west of the meridian their clocks are set to, so their clocks run ahead of the Sun: in summer, solar noon in Vigo comes at 14:41. China keeps one time from its east coast to its far west, where the Sun can cross the meridian as late as three in the afternoon. None of that troubles a chart, which needs only the offset, to the minute. Before standard time, towns kept their own local time, which calls for a different correction ([local mean time for old births](/astrology/local-mean-time-for-old-births/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The program takes off the offset in force at the birthplace on the birth date, summer hour included, to reach Universal Time, the time ephemerides are printed in ([Ephemeris, and how to read one](/astrology/ephemeris-and-how-to-read-one/)); from there it works out [sidereal time](/astrology/sidereal-time/), and from sidereal time the angles and the houses. The difficulty is history. Summer time began as a wartime measure: Germany and Austria-Hungary moved their clocks on 30 April 1916, Britain followed on 21 May, the United States in 1918. The rules have changed again and again since, and your chart needs the ones in force when you were born:

- **Britain** spent the summers of 1941 to 1945 two hours ahead of Greenwich, on double summer time, did it again in 1947, and kept its clocks an hour ahead all year from 27 October 1968 to 31 October 1971.
- **France and Spain** moved onto Central European Time during the Second World War and never moved back, which puts France an hour ahead of Greenwich in winter and two in summer.
- **The United States** kept summer time all year during the Second World War, as War Time. Today Arizona, outside the Navajo Nation, and Hawaii don't change their clocks at all.
- **South of the equator,** where summer time is kept, the clocks go forward in the southern spring, around October, and back around April.

Even ordinary years have two awkward nights. When the clocks go back in autumn, the last hour before the change is lived twice, from 2:00 to 2:59 in Europe and from 1:00 to 1:59 in North America, so a birth recorded inside it could be either of two moments an hour apart. When they go forward in spring, an hour is skipped, and a time written inside it can't be right as it stands.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

An hour's error is not a detail. The [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) moves about a degree every four minutes on average, so an hour moves it by around 15°, often into the next sign, and every [house cusp](/astrology/house-cusps/) moves with it; on a day the Moon changes sign, it can change the Moon's sign as well ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)).

When a birth time reaches me, I keep it exactly as the record gives it, with the town, and look at the offset the program applied rather than adjusting anything by hand ([How to find your rising sign](/astrology/how-to-find-your-rising-sign/)). If you were born on a night the clocks changed, the record, a hospital note or a parent's memory can usually settle which hour it was ([How to find your birth time](/astrology/how-to-find-your-birth-time/)), and I'd rather we settle it together than pick one for you. There's always a birth time. Or no reading.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Summer time is always one hour.”** Britain ran two hours ahead of Greenwich in the wartime summers, and from 1968 to 1971 kept its summer hour all through the winter.
- **“My software knows every clock in history.”** It knows a great deal, but its tables are only as good as their record of each town and year. For a wartime birth, or any birth long ago, check the offset it used.
- **“A place's time zone never changes.”** France and Spain changed theirs in the war and kept the new one; Nepal settled on its present offset only in 1986.
- **“Universal Time and GMT give different charts.”** For a birth chart they're the same hour: the two now differ by less than a second.
