Pandora was the fifty-fifth asteroid ever found, and its name came out of a quarrel. In 1858 the Dudley Observatory in Albany was the scene of a bitter dispute, and the name of the asteroid found there that September was chosen, apparently, by one side and found apt by the other. Some astrologers set Pandora in the birth chart. This page says what it is, where its story comes from, and why it forecasts nothing.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

George Mary Searle, an American astronomer who was also a Catholic priest, found it on 10 September 1858. It was the only asteroid he ever discovered. The name was apparently chosen by Blandina Dudley, the widow of the observatory's founder, who was then locked in a bitter dispute with its director, Benjamin Apthorp Gould; he thought the name had an "apt significance". Six years earlier, Gould had redrawn the symbol of [Hygiea](/astrology/hygiea/) as the rod of Asclepius. Ten years after Pandora came the hundredth asteroid, [Hekate](/astrology/hekate/).

It's a fairly large and bright asteroid, about 70 to 85 km across, which turns in under five hours. It circles the Sun every 4.6 years at an average distance of 2.76 times the Earth's, almost exactly the distance of [Ceres](/astrology/ceres/). In one classification it sits with [Psyche](/astrology/psyche/) among the M-type asteroids. A moon of Saturn carries the same name.

Its namesake is the first woman of Greek myth. In Hesiod, around 700 BC, Zeus has her made as a punishment for the theft of fire, and each god gives her a gift; her name has been read as "all-gifted" or as "all-giving". She opens a jar left in her care, and out come harsh toil, grievous sicknesses and other evils. She closes it in time to keep one thing inside: hope, or, in a darker reading of the Greek word, deceptive expectation.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A body named in 1858 brings no older tradition, so what astrologers say about Pandora starts from the myth: the gifts, the curiosity, the jar, the hope. Two details are worth knowing before reading anything into it.

The first is the box. Hesiod's Pandora opens a large storage jar, a *pithos*. The box is usually traced to 1508, when the humanist Erasmus, retelling the story in his collection of proverbs, turned the Greek jar into a *pyxis*, a box. The idiom of opening Pandora's box grew from that slip.

The second is the name itself. It was chosen in the middle of a quarrel and approved for its "apt significance" by the man on the other side. A name tells you about the people who gave it, not about anyone born with the asteroid in a particular sign ([personal-name asteroids](/astrology/personal-name-asteroids/)). That's worth remembering with every asteroid, and especially with names that sound like an omen.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read Pandora. The system behind my readings calculates no asteroids, and Pandora wasn't among the placements I [counted, tick or cross](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), so I can't tell you how often it holds.

What I can tell you is that no placement forecasts trouble. I read the planets' movement over a chart, the [transits](/astrology/transits/), as weather, not a forecast, and a myth about evils let loose isn't a prediction about your life. If someone has used a Pandora placement to frighten you, or to sell you a remedy, that tells you about them, not about your chart ([curse removal scams](/astrology/curse-removal-scams/)). And if you add Pandora to your own chart, keep it in proportion: close contacts only, read as a detail beside the planets ([How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Pandora in your chart means trouble is coming.”** No asteroid forecasts anything, and a myth is not a timetable.
- **“Pandora opened a box.”** It was a jar; the box is Erasmus's, from 1508.
- **“Pandora is all about curiosity punished.”** Her name is about gifts, and in the story hope stays with us.
- **“Pandora was named for its astrological meaning.”** It was named in the middle of an observatory quarrel; any meaning came long after.
