Alan Leo moved astrology from foretelling events to describing people, and he printed it for anyone who could afford a book. Much of the way astrology is written about today, as character rather than fate, goes back to him.

## Who {#who}

He was born William Frederick Allan in Westminster on 7 August 1860, and became Alan Leo: astrologer, author, publisher and collector of astrological data. He was a devout theosophist, and with his wife Bessie he belonged to Lodge Hermes no. 20 of Le Droit Humain, an order of Freemasonry that admits men and women. He was a non-smoker, a teetotaller and a vegetarian.

His output was enormous. He edited *The Astrologer's Magazine* in the 1890s, and his Modern Astrology Office brought out his own books one after another: *How to Judge a Nativity* (1904), *Horary Astrology* (1909), *The Key to Your Own Nativity* (1910) and *Casting the Horoscope* (1912) among them, and after his death a *Dictionary of Astrology*, completed by Vivian Robson. He also collected charts. His *1001 Notable Nativities*, subtitled *The Astrologer's Who's Who*, reached a second edition in 1917. That is a practice I keep away from: a public figure's chart is still a person's chart, and without their agreement I leave it closed ([Reading public figures](/astrology/reading-public-figures/)).

Some astrologers now call him the father of modern astrology. He died at Bude, in Cornwall, on 30 August 1917, a few weeks after a court had fined him for fortune-telling. Bessie had warned him that he “needed rest badly after the worry and anxiety of the law case”; he spent the holiday rewriting his books instead. In 1919 she and Annie Besant published *The Life and Work of Alan Leo*.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He moved astrology from events to character. In the usual account he was the first to argue that a chart shows trends that may or may not unfold, not events fixed in advance, and the later habit of reading charts psychologically starts with him ([psychological astrology](/astrology/psychological-astrology/)). After his first prosecution he asked his colleagues to leave behind the astrologer “who prides himself on his predictions and who is ever seeking to convince the world that in the predictive side of Astrology alone shall we find its value”.

That is the line I work on. I read a chart for inclinations and seasons, never verdicts, and the things people most want predicted, health, money, a court case, are the ones I won't read at all ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). The laws he fell foul of haven't all gone, either: I don't offer readings to people in Pennsylvania, where state law makes predicting the future for money an offence. Leo came to it partly under pressure from the law, and the unfinished rewriting of his books along those lines was completed after his death by his friend and colleague H. S. Greene.

He wrote about timing too. *The Progressed Horoscope* (1906) dealt with the slow unfolding of a chart after birth, and [secondary progressions](/astrology/secondary-progressions/) are among the timing methods I use, read as seasons growing from inside rather than events arriving from outside. He didn't abandon every older branch, though: among his titles is a *Horary Astrology* (1909), on judging questions ([horary astrology](/astrology/horary-astrology/)).

He looked east. He and Bessie went to India twice, in 1909 and again in 1911, to study the astrology practised there ([Vedic astrology (Jyotish)](/astrology/vedic-astrology-jyotish/)). Back in London, the Astrological Lodge of London dates from 1915, his foundation.

The movement he belonged to has its own page: [the Theosophical revival](/astrology/theosophical-revival/). Two and a half centuries before him, [William Lilly](/astrology/william-lilly/) had faced prosecution for political predictions; in New York, in Leo's own years, [Evangeline Adams](/astrology/evangeline-adams/) faced fortune-telling charges too, and the courts there treated her differently.
