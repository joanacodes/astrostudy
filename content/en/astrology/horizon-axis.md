Stand outside at the moment you were born and face south: the horizon runs from your left, in the east, to your right, in the west. The horizon axis is that line, drawn across your chart, with the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) at one end and the [Descendant](/astrology/descendant/) at the other.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

At every moment, one degree of the zodiac is rising over the eastern horizon and the opposite degree is setting in the west. At your birth, the rising degree became your Ascendant — the cusp of the [1st house](/astrology/1st-house/) in Placidus and the other quadrant systems — and the setting one your Descendant, the cusp of the [7th house](/astrology/7th-house/). They are always exactly opposite, the same degree of opposite signs: Aries rising always comes with a Libra Descendant.

Together they divide the chart in two. Everything drawn above the line — houses 7 to 12 — was above the horizon when you were born, up in the sky; everything below it — houses 1 to 6 — was under the earth, out of sight.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Earth turns once a day, so the whole zodiac rises over the horizon in a day, and the Ascendant changes sign about every two hours on average — faster or slower with the sign, and with how far north or south you were born. Four minutes turn the sky by a degree. That's why the horizon axis needs a birth time and place, and why I don't guess it ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

It's one of two axes. The other, the [meridian axis](/astrology/meridian-axis/), runs from the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) at the top of the chart to the [Imum Coeli](/astrology/imum-coeli/) at the bottom. The two lines cross to make the four angles, which tradition counts among the strongest points of a chart: a planet close to one of them tends to speak loudly ([angular planets](/astrology/angular-planets/)).

Because the horizon splits the chart into a half that was visible and a half that was hidden, astrologers also read the balance between the two. Planets gathered above the horizon are often read as a life lived in the open, with and through other people; gathered below, as a life built in private, from the inside out ([hemisphere emphasis](/astrology/hemisphere-emphasis/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read the horizon as the line between you and other people. The Ascendant end is how you come through the door: the first impression, the instinctive way you start things. The Descendant end is what you meet in others — the people you're drawn to, and often the qualities you find easier to admire in a partner than to own yourself. Each end explains the other. Capricorn rising, with a Cancer Descendant, often meets the world composed and capable, and looks for tenderness in the people closest to them; the work of the axis is to let both belong to the same person.

Planets near the axis matter most: one close to the Ascendant colours how you come across, one close to the Descendant how your partnerships go. And I'm open about the evidence. In [my notebook](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), the rising sign held about a third of the time, like the Sun sign, Venus and Mercury, while Saturn, Chiron and Lilith held 75 to 90%. So I read the horizon with care, as one strand among several, and I tell you when a conclusion leans on it: that's [how I read](/how-i-read/).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“You can work out your rising sign from your birthday.”** It changes about every two hours on average; only the time and place of birth can settle it.
- **“Your rising sign is a mask, not the real you.”** It's how you meet the world, and that's real too: the first way you reach for, not a disguise.
- **“The Descendant tells you your partner's sign.”** It describes what you look for and meet in others, not the Sun sign of anyone you'll end up with.
- **“Planets above the horizon are lucky, planets below unlucky.”** The two halves describe where life tends to be lived — in the open or in private — not good or bad fortune.
