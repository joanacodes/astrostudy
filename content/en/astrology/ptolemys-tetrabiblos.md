The *Tetrabiblos* is the book in which [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/) explained astrology: four books in Greek, written in second-century Alexandria as the companion to his astronomy. F. E. Robbins, who translated it for the Loeb series, said it “enjoyed almost the authority of a Bible among the astrological writers of a thousand years or more”. It is a book of reasons more than recipes: every rule comes with the physics Ptolemy thought explained it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The name is a nickname. *Tetrabiblos*, like the Latin *Quadripartitum*, means “four books”. Some Greek manuscripts call it a “Mathematical treatise in four books”, others “The prognostics addressed to Syrus”, after the unidentified Syrus to whom Ptolemy dedicated all his works, and the latest critical edition, Wolfgang Hübner's of 1998, prints it as *Apotelesmatika*, “effects”.

No copy from Ptolemy's century survives. The oldest manuscript is an Arabic translation made in the ninth century by Hunayn ibn Ishaq ([Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/)); the oldest fairly complete Greek one dates from the thirteenth. Deborah Houlding traces the text's disagreements to three streams: copies that passed through Arabic, copies of a simplified Greek paraphrase, and late Greek manuscripts that never passed through translation.

The paraphrase, doubtfully attributed to Proclus, had the stranger career. Around 1630 the Vatican scholar Leo Allatius put it into Latin “for his own private gratification”; printed at Leiden in 1635, apparently without his consent, his Latin became the source of every English *Tetrabiblos* before 1940. The Greek itself had been printed at Nuremberg in 1535 by Joachim Camerarius, with a Latin translation made, for the first time, from the Greek rather than the Arabic.

Two editions appeared in 1940: Robbins's Loeb, Greek and English face to face, and the Teubner Greek text begun by Franz Boll and finished by his student Emilie Boer, later translated by Robert Schmidt for Project Hindsight. Hübner's edition, built on 33 complete and 14 partial manuscripts, is now the authoritative Greek; a reviewer in *The Classical Review* found progress over its predecessors “on virtually every page”. I quote Robbins, the translation most readers can find.

## What it says {#what-it-says}

**Book I** is the theory. Ptolemy separates two studies of the sky: one works out its movements, the other asks what those movements change on earth, and he admits the second is the less certain. His best witness is the Moon: “the rivers increase and diminish their streams with her light, the seas turn their own tides with her rising and setting, and plants and animals in whole or in some part wax and wane with her.” Because he holds astrology to be natural, he asks no oath of secrecy of his readers, as some astrologers of his day did ([Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/)); his near contemporary Valens wanted his own teaching kept from the ignorant ([Vettius Valens’ Anthology](/astrology/vettius-valens-anthology/)).

Then every planet gets a temperament. Jupiter, moderately warm and moist, is temperate and fertile; Mars, excessively dry, is destructive. That is where the tradition's [benefics and malefics](/astrology/benefics-and-malefics/) come from: a theory of heat and moisture, not a verdict on anyone's chart. He also tells the astrologer to take account of a person's nationality, country and upbringing, advice I'd still give. A chart is read beside a life, never instead of it.

**Book II** turns from persons to peoples ([mundane astrology](/astrology/mundane-astrology/)). It ties 73 nations to signs and planets, a geography of character that reads today as stereotype, then judges eclipses by where they were seen, comets by their shapes, “beams”, “trumpets”, “jars”, and the year's weather from the new or full Moon before the Sun enters Aries.

**Book III** begins the birth chart. Ptolemy prefers the moment of birth to that of conception, as “more perfect in potentiality”, then takes the topics in the order a life meets them: parents and siblings, the body, injuries and illness, and the length of life, which Bouché-Leclercq called “the chief task of astrology” and on which, Jim Tester notes, Ptolemy is unusually detailed. I read a chart for neither illness nor death ([Can astrology predict health problems?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-health-problems/), [Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)). The book ends with the soul, the part I recognise most: [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/) for the reasoning mind, the [Moon](/astrology/moon/) for the “sensory and irrational part”.

**Book IV** covers what Ptolemy calls “external accidentals”: wealth, rank, work, marriage, children, friends, travel, the manner of death. For wealth he uses the “so-called Lot of Fortune”, measured from the Ascendant as the Moon is from the Sun “in both diurnal and nocturnal nativities”. Most Hellenistic astrologers reversed it by night, but his authority carried the unreversed version into medieval and Renaissance books, [William Lilly’s Christian Astrology (1647)](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/) among them. Astrologers still differ, and software with them ([Part of Fortune](/astrology/part-of-fortune/)).

For marriage he compares two charts: “Marriages for the most part are lasting when in both the genitures the luminaries happen to be in harmonious aspect.” It is one of the oldest descriptions of [synastry](/astrology/synastry/). I compare two charts too, only with both people's consent ([Synastry and consent](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)), and never to say whether a relationship will last.

The last chapter gives the seven ages of life, in the order of the planetary spheres: the Moon for the first four years, Mercury the next ten, Venus eight, the Sun nineteen, Mars fifteen, Jupiter twelve, and Saturn all the rest. Even the ending is disputed: the manuscripts give two, and Robbins, printing both, sided with the one that came down through Arabic.
