In English, a nemesis is an arch-enemy. In Greek myth Nemesis was something more exact: the goddess who saw that everyone received what was due to them, and no more. Asteroid 128 carries her name.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Nemesis is a large asteroid of the main belt, about 163 km across, dark and carbonaceous, a primitive kind of rock. James Craig Watson found it on 25 November 1872. It goes round the Sun in about 4.6 years, between 2.4 and 3.1 times the Earth's distance, and turns very slowly: one rotation takes nearly 39 hours, the second-longest known for an asteroid more than 150 km across. It is the largest member of a family of asteroids that astronomers name after it, and between 2005 and 2021 it was seen passing in front of eight stars.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Her name comes from the Greek *némein*, to give what is due. At first she was the distributor of fortune, neither good nor bad, handed out in proportion to what each person deserved. In Herodotus and Pindar the word came to mean the resentment that follows when that proportion is broken, and in Greek tragedy she is the avenger of crime and the punisher of hubris, arrogance before the gods. In time she was paired with Tyche, the Greek [Fortuna](/astrology/fortuna/), as the one who keeps chance in balance.

Her images say the same. She is winged and carries a measuring rod, a bridle, scales, a sword or a whip; a poet of the second century AD called her the "winged balancer of life". At Rhamnous, in Attica, her statue was carved by Pheidias after the battle of Marathon, from a block of Parian marble the Persians had brought to make a monument to the victory they expected.

Her best-known act is in Ovid. Narcissus has scorned the nymph Echo; Nemesis leads him to a pool, where he falls in love with his own reflection and wastes away, and the nymphs find a flower where his body had been.

She also belongs to an older story about justice leaving the world. Hesiod foresaw that Nemesis and Aidos, humility, would abandon the earth as people grew worse, and later poets told how justice itself, [Astraea](/astrology/astraea/), went up to the stars; Nemesis was said to be justice in her fiercer form.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I don't read Nemesis, and it's one name I'd be wary of even if I did. The modern sense of the word invites a question no chart can answer, who is my enemy, and the old sense invites a harder one, what am I being punished for. A placement can't name an enemy, and reading someone else's chart to find one would need their agreement anyway ([Synastry and consent](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/)). Nor can a placement show that a difficult stretch of life is deserved. Reading misfortune as a verdict is the opposite of what I do ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). If a hard time feels like punishment, that's worth talking through with someone qualified, not with a chart ([Astrology and mental health](/astrology/astrology-and-mental-health/)).

It also has no record with me. The system behind my readings doesn't compute it, and my notebook, which went through the Sun, the Moon, the rising sign, each planet, Chiron and Lilith, never reached it ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

If you use it, the goddess herself suggests the rule: proportion. One asteroid in a tight contact with a planet ([Orbs](/astrology/orbs/)) is a detail; it never outweighs the planets, and a dozen asteroids read at once outweigh everything ([How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Nemesis means your enemy.”** In myth she was proportion and retribution, not a rival. The arch-enemy sense is modern English.
- **“The asteroid Nemesis is the star blamed for mass extinctions.”** That Nemesis is a hypothetical companion of the Sun, proposed in 1984; surveys such as IRAS in the 1980s and 2MASS from 1997 to 2001 didn't find it. Asteroid 128 orbits between Mars and Jupiter.
- **“Nemesis punished people at random.”** In the myths she answers hubris. Even her name is about measure: giving each what is due.
