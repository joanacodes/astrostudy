In an essay published in 1952, Carl Jung, the founder of analytical psychology, described a statistical test of astrology made with the horoscopes of married couples. He was testing an idea of his own, synchronicity, and what he made of his figures is the most interesting part of the story.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Jung's experiment is no evidence that the planets act on us, and Jung himself didn't claim it was. Accounts of his figures differ: one says he found what astrologers predicted, more than once; another says the results were not statistically significant, and that he knew it. Both agree on what he did next. Rather than speak of a cause, he called the link synchronicity, a meaningful coincidence with no discoverable cause. Critics consider that idea neither testable nor falsifiable, and they faulted the study itself for its sample, its statistics and its astrology.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**The essay.** Jung's essay on synchronicity first appeared in 1952, in German, in *Naturerklärung und Psyche*, the book he shared with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli; in English it is *The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche*. The essay is now also a book of its own, *Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle*. Synchronicity is Jung's name for events that coincide in time and seem meaningfully related, yet have no discoverable causal connection; he called it “a falling together in time, a kind of simultaneity”.

**The experiment.** For it, Jung was offered a collection of over 400 pairs of horoscopes of married couples. One account, drawing on his essay, says he found correlations among the married couples that matched astrological prediction; astonished, he questioned his own influence on the study and repeated it, with the same results, then changed his methods and again arrived at the results astrologers predicted. Unable to find a causal relationship, he called the link acausal. Another account, in *The Skeptic Encyclopedia of Pseudoscience*, says there was no statistical significance in the results, that Jung was aware of it, and that he invoked synchronicity to explain the lack.

**The criticism.** The study was later heavily criticised: for not using a truly random sample, for dubious statistics and astrological material, and for its lack of consistency with astrology itself. Even Pauli, his collaborator, objected to Jung's experiments with astrology. And as an explanation, synchronicity has a problem of its own: it is considered neither testable nor falsifiable, and looking for coincidences after the fact is of very dubious value.

**Beside it.** Three years before Jung's essay, the psychologist Bertram Forer had published a classroom demonstration of why a personality sketch can feel true without being about you, the subject of [The Barnum (Forer) effect](/astrology/barnum-forer-effect/). A few years after it, Michel Gauquelin published statistics of his own: [The Mars effect (Gauquelin)](/astrology/mars-effect-gauquelin/).

Jung was looking, in effect, for a signature of marriage in two charts. That's not what I do with two charts. A [synastry reading](/synastry/) isn't a score, there's no percentage, and both people know it's happening: [why I won't read a synastry behind someone's back](/blog/synastry-behind-their-back/).

## What I found {#what-i-found}

Jung worked with pairs of horoscopes; my notebook worked with single charts, placement by placement. I checked every placement of my own chart, Sun, Moon, rising, each planet in its sign and house, with a tick or a cross, then did the same for family, friends and strangers, with their consent.

Overall, about a third held: 30 to 40%. The Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury, the placements everyone reads first, are among those. Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90% of the time.

It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general, about marriage or about anything else. It's simply why, when something in a reading rests on a placement that held less often in my tests, I tell you so. The method is in [my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).
