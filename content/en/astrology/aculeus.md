The Butterfly Cluster, Messier 6, goes by Aculeus in astrology, which counts this loose spray of young stars near the Scorpion's sting among the fixed stars. Its centre works out at about 26°06′ Sagittarius in 2026, a little over a degree past Shaula.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Aculeus is Messier 6, catalogued as NGC 6405 and called the Butterfly Cluster for its shape: an open cluster of about 120 stars, some 1,590 light-years away and about 94 million years old. Their combined light comes to magnitude 4.2, spread over about 25′, a little less than the full Moon's width. Most of its bright stars are hot and blue; the brightest, BM Scorpii, is an orange giant whose light wavers between magnitude 5.5 and 7.0.

Its degree, about 26°06′ [Sagittarius](/astrology/sagittarius/) in 2026 and 25°44′ in 2000, is taken from the cluster's centre, which astronomers give only roughly, to a tenth of a minute of right ascension. Its latitude, 8.8° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), keeps it just outside the planets' band of about 8° either side, and well beyond the Moon's 5°.

It is a different kind of cluster from [Facies](/astrology/facies/), twelve degrees further along the zodiac. Facies is a dense ball of stars about 12 billion years old; Aculeus is a loose group not yet 100 million years old, and about seven times nearer.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Aculeus is Latin for a sting, the word biologists still use for a scorpion's stinger, and the cluster sits a few degrees from the stars of the Scorpion's sting, [Shaula](/astrology/shaula/) and Lesath.

Its recorded history begins with Giovanni Battista Hodierna, in 1654. The astronomer Robert Burnham Jr. suggested that Ptolemy may already have seen it with the naked eye while he was observing its neighbour, the larger cluster astrologers call [Acumen](/astrology/acumen/). Credit for the discovery usually goes to Jean-Philippe Loys de Chéseaux, in 1746, and Charles Messier observed it on 23 May 1764 and gave it the sixth place in his list.

Its two names show two ways of looking at the same patch of sky. Astrology's Aculeus places it in a figure, near a scorpion's sting; the astronomers' Butterfly describes what a telescope shows.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Birthdays on about 17 and 18 December put the Sun on the cluster's centre, three or four days before the solstice ([Sun in Sagittarius](/astrology/sun-in-sagittarius/)). Its stars spread only about 12′ either side of that centre, so the whole cluster sits well inside its own one-degree window. Because Aculeus sits 1°09′ past Shaula, the stretch from 25°06′ to 25°57′ Sagittarius in a 2026 chart is within a degree of both ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)).

Pluto spent much of late 2005 to the autumn of 2007 near its degree, crossing it three times ([Pluto in Sagittarius](/astrology/pluto-in-sagittarius/)); Uranus came by in 1987, Neptune between 1981 and 1983.
