Arachne's name is simply the Greek word for spider, and her story was told to explain why spiders weave. It also asks an uncomfortable question about art and power, which may be why it has lasted. Asteroid 407 carries her name.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Arachne is dark and carbonaceous, about 97 km wide, and keeps to the main belt, 2.4 to 2.8 times the Earth's distance from the Sun. One circuit takes about 4.3 years and one rotation nearly a full day, about 22.6 hours. Between 2002 and 2023 it was seen to pass in front of nine stars.

Max Wolf found it at Heidelberg on 13 October 1895, the year he also found [Persephone (asteroid 399)](/astrology/persephone-asteroid/), and he found another, 408 Fama, the same night. Wolf pioneered the search for asteroids on long-exposure photographs, where they show as short streaks among the stars, instead of hunting them by eye; once his method took hold, the rate of discoveries rose sharply.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Ovid tells the story in book 6 of the *Metamorphoses*. Arachne was a young woman of Lydia, daughter of a dyer famous for his purple, and so fine a weaver that she would not admit her skill came from Minerva. The goddess came to her disguised as an old woman and told her to ask pardon; Arachne answered that if Minerva wanted to stop her, she could come herself. The goddess threw off the disguise, and they wove.

Minerva's tapestry showed, in its four corners, the gods punishing mortals who had set themselves up as their equals, around her own contest with Neptune for Athens. Arachne's showed the gods deceiving mortal women: Jupiter as a bull for Europa and a swan for Leda, Neptune in his disguises, [Medusa](/astrology/medusa/)'s among them. Her work had no flaw, and that was the trouble. Minerva tore it apart and struck her with the shuttle. Arachne, in shame, hanged herself, and Minerva, moved to pity, sprinkled her with the juice of Hecate's herb ([Hekate](/astrology/hekate/)) and turned her into a spider, to go on weaving for ever.

The story has been read two ways. It explains spiders and warns against pride. It can also be read as Ovid's own case: he wrote under Augustus, who later exiled him, at a time when weaving was a common image for poetry, and in his telling the work that shows the powerful at their worst is the one destroyed. Velázquez painted the contest as *The Spinners*, now in the Prado.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I leave Arachne out, and I'd ask anyone who uses it to be careful which half of the story they reach for. One half says that talent which won't bow to authority gets punished; the other, that the punishment was the injustice. Neither belongs on a person. A placement can't tell you that you're too proud, or that you'll pay for your gifts, and the idea of a chart showing what someone deserves is the popular notion of karma, not something a chart can measure ([Karma](/astrology/karma/)).

Her story also includes a death by her own hand. If that touches something in your own life, please talk to someone now: my Disclaimers page, linked at the foot of every page, lists helplines, and [Astrology and mental health](/astrology/astrology-and-mental-health/) says what a chart can and can't do for you.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Arachne lost the contest.”** In Ovid her tapestry is flawless and the goddess destroys it in anger. It is a later mythographer, the first of the so-called Vatican Mythographers, who has her lose.
- **“Arachne was a goddess.”** She was a mortal, a dyer's daughter from Lydia, in Asia Minor.
- **“It's an old Greek legend.”** The full story is known mainly from Ovid, a Roman poet. A Corinthian flask of about 580 to 560 BC may show the contest, but that reading is disputed.
