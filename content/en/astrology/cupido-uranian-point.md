Cupido is the point Uranian astrology gives to love. Alfred Witte placed it beyond Neptune, where he expected a planet to turn up; none ever did, and the school he founded kept the point all the same.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Witte, an astrologer and amateur astronomer in Hamburg, proposed four planets out past Neptune: Cupido, [Hades](/astrology/hades/), Zeus and Kronos. The four predate Pluto, found in 1930, and in 1927 Friedrich Sieggrün, one of his first collaborators, added four more ([The Hamburg School (Uranian astrology)](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/)). Witte treated Cupido as more than a set of figures: he described its colour, as he did Hades's, in articles later gathered in the anthology *Der Mensch – eine Empfangsstation kosmischer Suggestionen* ([Alfred Witte](/astrology/alfred-witte/)).

The name is the Latin one of the god the Greeks called Eros, and *Cupido* means passionate desire. In myth he is a winged archer whose arrow leaves anyone it strikes, god or mortal, in the grip of a desire they can't master. He was often shown as the son of [Venus](/astrology/venus/) and [Mars](/astrology/mars/), and in the one long story told about him, the tale of Cupid and Psyche, he is wounded by his own weapons and goes through the ordeal of love himself.

Astrology has a second Cupido, and that one is real: [Cupido (asteroid 763)](/astrology/cupido-asteroid/), a small rock of the main belt found in 1913. It goes round the Sun in less than four years. Anything orbiting beyond Neptune would need longer than Neptune's 165.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Uranian astrologers read Cupido for romantic love, and for marriage, which they look for in its transits to the planets of love and marriage in a birth chart. They give it other kinds of partnership as well, and artistic gifts.

It rarely speaks alone. The school's unit of meaning is the planetary picture, in which a point combines with two or more other factors, as when it stands at the [midpoint](/astrology/midpoints/) of two planets. Cupido enters those pictures as a planet would, and the dial the school devised for the purpose, a circle of 90°, puts its hard aspects and midpoints in line ([The Uranian 90° dial](/astrology/uranian-90-dial/)).

Its position comes from calculation, not observation. Nobody has ever pointed a telescope at Cupido; its place on any date is worked out from the orbit Witte's school gave it, and that is all a chart program can show you.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Cupido isn't in my readings. Love comes into them through Venus, by sign, house and aspect, and through [the 7th house](/astrology/7th-house/), whose cusp is the Descendant. That house depends on the birth time, as every house does, which is one reason I don't read a chart without one ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Between two people, the case against it is plainer still. When I compare charts, I count only contacts that involve at least one personal body: the Sun, the Moon, Mercury, Venus or Mars. A point beyond Neptune moves so slowly that partners born a few years apart have their Cupidos close together, whatever else is true of them, so a Cupido contact says more about their birth years than about the two of them ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

Some Uranian astrologers read a coming marriage in Cupido's transits. I don't time weddings by any point: a transit is weather, not a forecast.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Cupido is a planet still waiting to be found.”** No telescope has shown it, and some of Witte's successors now treat his points as the centres of clusters of small bodies, not as planets.
- **“Cupido is about romance and nothing else.”** Uranian astrologers read it for other partnerships and for artistic gifts as well.
- **“Cupido is Venus by another name.”** Venus is the brightest planet in the sky; Cupido is a point no one has seen, worked out for a region far beyond Neptune.
