Unlike the other biases on this site, this one isn't about how you hear a reading. It's about what you might do afterwards.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Self-fulfilling prophecies are real: a belief can bring about what it predicts, because people act on it. In classrooms, later research found the effects real but usually small and temporary, smaller than the first famous study claimed. For astrology, the nearest finding is that people who know what their sign is said to mean tend to describe themselves that way. So a reading can help produce what it seems to confirm, and that's why, in mine, a chart describes tendencies, never a fate to live up to.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

The idea is older than the phrase. In 1928 the sociologists W. I. Thomas and Dorothy Swaine Thomas wrote, in *The Child in America*: "If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences." Twenty years later Robert K. Merton gave the self-fulfilling prophecy its classic definition, in an essay of that name in *The Antioch Review*: "a false definition of the situation evoking a new behavior, which makes the originally false conception come true". His best-known example is a bank. A rumour spreads that it will fail; its customers rush to withdraw their money; and since no bank keeps all its deposits in cash, it does fail. "The prophecy of collapse led to its own fulfillment." Merton is credited with coining the phrase, although the Oxford English Dictionary quotes it from the *Morning Post* in 1832.

The best-known test came from schools. In 1968 Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson reported that when teachers were told that a random selection of their pupils could be expected to do exceptionally well, those pupils' test scores rose significantly by the end of the year. Their book, *Pygmalion in the Classroom*, took its title from the Greek myth that gives the effect its name. It was criticised at once. Robert L. Thorndike, reviewing it in 1968, found the test used to measure the children so seriously flawed that he judged the findings worthless. In 1984 Stephen Raudenbush, putting together eighteen experiments, found the effect supported in general, but much stronger when the expectation was planted at the start of the school year, and virtually nil once teachers had known their pupils for more than two weeks. In 2005, looking back over 35 years of research, Lee Jussim and Kent Harber concluded that self-fulfilling prophecies in the classroom do occur, but that the effects are usually small and temporary, and that what looks like a prophecy fulfilled may often be an accurate expectation.

For astrology, the nearest finding is what researchers call self-attribution. In a 2003 paper, Edgar Wunder lists the studies: people who know what their sun sign is supposed to mean have repeatedly been found to describe their own personality in terms of its traits; the effect is stronger when they're aware the study is about astrology; and in studies of people unfamiliar with sun-sign symbolism, no effect was found. The broader research on signs and character has a page of its own, [Sun signs and personality: the studies](/astrology/sun-signs-and-personality-the-studies/). Self-attribution isn't proof that a reading changes a life. It does show that people can come to describe themselves in a chart's terms.

[Cold reading](/astrology/cold-reading/) works on what a reader picks up from you in the moment. A self-fulfilling prophecy works on what you do after the reading, which is why it matters even when a reading is written from the chart alone.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My notebook can't tell these apart. I checked every placement with a tick or a cross, my own chart first, then family, friends and strangers, with their consent. Overall about a third held, 30 to 40%, and [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/), [Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/) and [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/) held 75 to 90% of the time. But a tick can't show whether a placement described someone or whether they had already taken the description on. It's [a notebook, not a study](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), and it proves nothing about astrology in general.

What I can do is read in a way that gives a prophecy as little to work with as I can. A chart describes tendencies, not permissions: how you're inclined to move, not where you must end up. There are no doomed placements in my readings, and I read your patterns and your seasons, not next Tuesday. "You're a Scorpio, so…" is not a reading; it's an excuse with a vocabulary. It's also why I don't read for anyone under 18: a chart read at the wrong age can become a label you grow into.

You stay in charge of what you do with a reading. It's the last rule of [how I read](/how-i-read/), and with this bias it's the one that matters most.
