Some words in astrology come from far away. Circumambulations is one: a word for walking around a sacred place, used for one of the old ways of timing a life. I'll tell you what I can stand behind, and where I stop.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The word comes from the Latin *circum*, around, and *ambulare*, to walk. Its everyday sense is religious: the ritual circling of a sacred object or place, part of Hindu and Buddhist devotion, of the pilgrimage around the Kaaba, of some Christian and Jewish rites. That's why a search for it turns up temples and pilgrims before it turns up a chart.

In astrology, circumambulations belong to the time-lord techniques, the methods that put one planet at a time in charge of a stretch of life. Their neighbours on this site are [decennials](/astrology/decennials/), [firdaria](/astrology/firdaria/), [zodiacal releasing](/astrology/zodiacal-releasing/) and [annual profections](/astrology/annual-profections/).

Old astrologers timed a life in two broad ways. Some methods worked from fixed tables: the firdaria give each planet the same number of years in every chart, and only the starting point changes. Others moved points of the birth chart by calculation, as [primary directions](/astrology/primary-directions/) do, so that the timing differs from one chart to the next.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

Here the honest answer is short. I haven't found a source I could check that sets out how circumambulations are worked out, and the name doesn't tell you. Rather than describe a procedure I can't verify, I leave the mechanics to the translations of the texts that teach it.

What I can describe is the setting such techniques were used in. In Sasanian Iran, Greek works by Dorotheus of Sidon and Vettius Valens were put into Middle Persian, and the astrologer Zādānfarrukh is reported to have particularly admired Valens's books. His own writings survive only through later Arabic quotations and adaptations. They read a person's year through an anniversary chart, cast for the Sun's return to its birth position, together with profections, directions and planetary periods. Each method gave roles to different planets, and the year was judged from all of them together ([Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/)).

Whatever its rules, a technique that times a life from the birth chart is only as good as the chart behind it, and that means a reliable birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

What any time-lord shows: which planet's function is in front for a while, read through where that planet sits in your birth chart. Nothing in the method can show what will happen in that time.

One of the roles in Zādānfarrukh's material was the lord of the year, a planet chosen by the rules of the technique in use and given particular weight for those twelve months. The idea is still in use: it is the lord of the year I read in profections.

My own forecasts work in the spirit of that Persian practice, though with other techniques: several methods read together, the [solar return](/astrology/solar-return/) among them, and weight given only where they agree. Circumambulations aren't among those methods. The directions I use are [solar arc directions](/astrology/solar-arc-directions/), which move every point of the chart at the progressed Sun's pace.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

The first limit is mine: a page that can't give you the procedure can't help you check a reading built on it. If you're offered one, ask which author's method it follows and how it is worked out, and expect a precise answer.

The second is one the Persian material already shows. When several methods are read together, each naming its own planet, the result depends on how they are weighed against each other, and that weighing is a judgement, never a calculation. A reading should tell you which methods it leaned on, and why ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).
