Of all the Liliths, this is the only one you could, in principle, photograph: a lump of metal-rich rock in the asteroid belt, catalogued as number 1181. Its name is a coincidence of the kind the catalogue of minor planets is full of. It honours a composer, not the figure of legend.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

On 11 February 1927, Benjamin Jekhowsky, a Russian–French astronomer at the Algiers Observatory, found a new minor planet, one that had first been observed at the Simeiz Observatory in 1914 under another designation. It became number 1181, and Jekhowsky named it Lilith, after Marie Juliette Boulanger, the French composer known as Lili (1893–1918), younger sister of Nadia Boulanger.

It is about 23 km across and metallic, and it keeps to the middle of the main asteroid belt, between 2.1 and 3.2 times the Earth's distance from the Sun. It goes round the Sun in about 4 years and 4 months, on an orbit tilted about 6° to the Earth's, and turns on its axis about every 15 hours.

That makes it unlike the other Liliths. The Black Moon, with its three calculations, mean, true and interpolated, is a point of the Moon's orbit, not an object ([Black Moon Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/)). The [Dark Moon Lilith](/astrology/dark-moon-lilith/) was meant to be an object, a second moon of the Earth, and was never found. The asteroid is real, and it goes round the Sun.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

In a chart, asteroid Lilith is placed from its orbit like any other asteroid. It moves on its own clock, independently of the Black Moon, so the two can sit anywhere in relation to each other; when they happen to share a sign, it is a coincidence of timing, not a sign that they are one point. A chart usually tells them apart with the word *asteroid* or the number 1181 beside the name.

A name, in the asteroid catalogue, says little about the body that carries it. More than a million minor planets are known, and many are named after people and places, as this one was after a composer. Whatever a writer reads into asteroid Lilith comes from its position in a chart, not from its namesake, and certainly not from the legend that gave Black Moon Lilith its name.

The family of points filed under Lilith on this site runs on beyond this one: [Priapus](/astrology/priapus/) and the [White Moon Selena](/astrology/white-moon-selena/) have pages of their own, and [which Lilith is mine?](/astrology/which-lilith-is-mine/) sets them side by side.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The system I built for my readings computes no asteroids, so asteroid Lilith isn't part of them. The Lilith I read is the mean Black Moon, and it's the one my notebook counted: with Saturn and Chiron, one of [the three placements that held best](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/), 75 to 90% of the time, where placements overall held about a third. The asteroid wasn't in the notebook at all ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

If you add asteroids to your own chart, the advice on [using asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/) applies here too: read the planets first, keep to a few asteroids, and give weight only to close contacts. And keep the asteroid apart from the Black Moon. A description written for one won't fit the other. What I read, and what I leave out, is in [how I read](/how-i-read/).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Asteroid Lilith is named after Adam's first wife.”** It's named after Lili Boulanger, the French composer.
- **“Asteroid Lilith is the Dark Moon.”** The asteroid goes round the Sun and has been observed since 1927; the Dark Moon was supposed to go round the Earth and was never found.
- **“If my asteroid Lilith and my Black Moon share a sign, they're the same point.”** They move independently; a shared sign is a coincidence.
- **“The asteroid is the real Lilith, so it's the one to use.”** It's the only Lilith that is an object, but the Black Moon is a real point of a real orbit, and it's the Lilith most astrologers mean.
