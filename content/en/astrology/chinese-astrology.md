Chinese astrology counts in years where mine counts in degrees. It grew from the Chinese calendar, and it reads a birth through that calendar's cycles rather than through the Sun's path among the signs.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

Its frame is a count of sixty. Ten Heavenly Stems pair with twelve Earthly Branches in a cycle of sixty, used first to name days: almost every inscription on the Shang oracle bones carries such a date, from about 1100 BCE. Counting years with it came much later. The earliest documents that do so are silk manuscripts from tomb 3 at Mawangdui, sealed in 168 BCE; the practice spread under the Western Han, and the count of years has run without a break ever since. Each branch has its animal, Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon, Snake, Horse, Goat, Monkey, Rooster, Dog and Pig, so the year you were born in gives you yours, and that year turns at the lunar New Year, not on 1 January.

Astrology flourished under the Han dynasty, when yin and yang, the five phases and the ideas of heaven and earth were brought together into the principles behind Chinese medicine, divination, astrology and alchemy. The planets belong to that scheme: in Chinese, Mercury is the water star, Venus the metal star, Mars the fire star, Jupiter the wood star and Saturn the earth star. The sky was mapped in its own way, along 28 lunar lodges, the *xiu* ([The 28 lunar mansions](/astrology/28-lunar-mansions/)). Their names are written on the lid of a lacquer box from the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng, proof that the system was in use before 433 BCE. India divides the Moon's path in a similar way, into nakshatras ([Vedic astrology (Jyotish)](/astrology/vedic-astrology-jyotish/)).

Reading the sky was also the business of the state. Under the Ming, the Astronomical Bureau made the calendar, reported anything abnormal in the sky to the emperor and chose auspicious days for events from military parades to marriages, though most of its work was simply recording the movements of the stars and planets.

Personal fate calculation took its classic forms later. Under the Tang, Li Xuzhong (c. 761–813) is credited with reading a life from the stem-branch pairs of the year, month and day of birth; under the Song, Xu Ziping added the hour, giving the Four Pillars, or *bazi*, ‘eight characters’. A Song manual revised under the Ming, the *Yuanhai Ziping*, is still in use, and its months follow the solar terms, not the lunar months. The Purple Star system, Zi Wei Dou Shu, is traditionally attributed to the Daoist Chen Tuan (c. 871–989), an attribution no one can verify; it places more than a hundred named stars in twelve palaces, and the birth month and hour decide where the Life Palace falls. Under the Ming and Qing, the Imperial Astronomical Bureau used the Four Pillars for court divination. In the twentieth century such practices were classed as superstition in mainland China, and they revived from its last decades, in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore and Malaysia above all.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

The astrology I practise didn't come through China, and I don't read Chinese charts. What this tradition gives me is a set of comparisons that make my own tools clearer.

A sign can be a year. The animal of your year is shared with everyone born in the same Chinese year, much as a [Sun](/astrology/sun/) sign is shared with everyone born within about a month of you. Chinese astrology also pairs the animals for love and marriage, as Sun-sign astrology pairs signs ([Sun-sign compatibility, and why it isn't enough](/astrology/sun-sign-compatibility-and-why-it-isnt-enough/)). In my notebook the Sun sign, one of the placements everyone reads first, held about a third of the time, and I hold it lightly for that reason ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)). I never tested a year animal, so I have no figure for it.

The hour counts there too. The Four Pillars gives the hour a pillar of its own, and the Purple Star needs it to place the Life Palace, much as my charts need the time for the [rising sign](/astrology/ascendant/) and the houses. I don't read a chart without it ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

Five phases are not four elements. Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water are usually translated ‘elements’, but in 1987 the sinologist Nathan Sivin proposed ‘five phases’, because they describe process and change rather than substance. The fire, earth, air and water of a Western chart are another scheme, and five can't be laid one for one over four ([The four elements](/astrology/four-elements/)).

I don't draw Chinese charts, so the reading of yours belongs with someone trained in that calendar. What I read is the tropical chart for the same minute: another picture of the same birth, made with other tools ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).
