You may meet the word in a chart program or a forum: an envelope, listed among the aspect patterns. It's one of the least settled names in this family. The authors who use it don't describe the same figure, so this page can't show you one drawing, or give you a count.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

I can't give you a single geometry. Different authors attach the name to different sets of planets and aspects, and a description I couldn't check would be a guess dressed up as a fact. What I can describe is what every aspect figure has in common, and how to read the one in front of you.

An aspect pattern is a set of planets joined by aspects, drawn as lines across the chart. Every aspect pattern on these pages with an agreed definition is closed in one sense: each of its planets aspects at least two of the others, so the lines make a shape rather than a stray line. When a program draws an envelope, the shape is there to look at. Note which planets sit at its corners, which aspect each line stands for, and how close to exact each one is; the [aspect grid](/astrology/aspect-grid/) of your chart gives you all three. Those are the facts of your chart. The name is a label someone chose for them.

The [trapeze](/astrology/trapeze/), the figure before this one in the family, has a similar problem, softened by geometry: its outline at least follows a rule. The envelope's name carries no rule I can verify, which makes reading the lines themselves all the more important.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The count behind this site tests every chart against a single rule for each figure, with the orbs it states: 8° when the Sun or Moon is involved, 6° otherwise, 3° for the quincunx and the sesquiquadrate. The envelope has no single rule, so it isn't counted. A figure that one author's definition finds and another's misses would produce a number that described nothing in particular.

That doesn't make the planets in it less real. If a program draws an envelope in your chart, its planets still make every aspect it shows, and each aspect means what it always means in the system I built for my readings: openings, flows, frictions, polarities, adjustments, according to the angle. I read those first, one by one, in their signs. Then I ask what binding them together adds. A figure is emphasis: it says that these functions tend to move together, not who you are, and it earns attention when it repeats what the rest of the chart already says.

The houses of its planets decide which areas of life the figure joins, and they need the time of birth. If the Moon is one of its corners, the figure may hold for only part of the day. That's why I [don't read a chart without the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/), whatever its patterns are called.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Find the definition.** A program's help pages or settings usually say what it means by each pattern. If they don't, treat the label as a suggestion.
- **Check the orbs.** A wide figure is a loose one. The [orbs](/astrology/orbs/) your program uses decide whether the shape is tight enough to matter.
- **Read the planets, not the label.** Each planet keeps its function, each aspect its meaning, whatever the figure is called.
- **Compare with fixed figures.** If the same planets also make a grand trine, a T-square or a yod, start there: those have one definition everybody shares.

The figure after this one, the [grand quintile](/astrology/grand-quintile/), is built on a single rule: five planets, each a fifth of the circle from the next. When a pattern name is this unsettled, the honest reading is the one built from what's actually drawn.
