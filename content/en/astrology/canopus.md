Canopus is the second-brightest star of the night sky, and from London or Paris it is never seen: it doesn't rise north of about 37° latitude. Its zodiac degree is 15°19′ Cancer, but the star itself lies 76° south of the Sun's path.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Canopus is α Carinae, magnitude −0.72 in the Yale catalogue, about 310 light-years away. Carina, the keel, is one of three pieces of the old constellation Argo Navis, the ship of Jason and the Argonauts, which the astronomer Lacaille broke up in 1763; Canopus marked the ship's steering oar.

[Sirius](/astrology/sirius/) sits less than a degree before it in the zodiac: 14°26′ [Cancer](/astrology/cancer/) against Canopus's 15°19′ for 2026, so a planet at 15° Cancer is within a degree of both. Canopus was at 14°57′ in 2000 and 13°59′ in 1930.

Its latitude, 75.8° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), puts it about 14° from the ecliptic's south pole ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)). Its brightness and its distance from the planets' paths made it a guide for spacecraft: in 1964 Mariner 4 was the first to steady itself on a star, and the star was Canopus.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

The name is a ship's pilot's: Canopus steered the ship of Menelaus when he sailed to bring Helen back from Troy. [Ptolemy](/astrology/ptolemy/), observing from Alexandria, recorded the star as Kanôbos, while the mainland Greeks and Romans never saw it. In Ptolemaic Egypt its rising at nightfall set the date of the Ptolemaia festival, held every four years from 262 to 145 BCE. In 1153 Ibn Rushd travelled to Marrakesh to observe it, since it never rose over his native Córdoba, and used the difference as an argument that the Earth is round.

Its Arabic name is Suhayl, and Persian keeps the name for anything rarely seen: to become the star Soheil is to make yourself scarce. In China it was Shou Xing, the Star of Longevity, named in the *Records of the Grand Historian* of 94 BCE as the southern counterpart of Sirius, and also the Old Man of the South Pole; under the Han dynasty its appearance was taken as a sign of peace. Vedic literature links it with the sage Agastya.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

By the tradition's one-degree rule, a planet or angle between about 14°19′ and 16°19′ Cancer, for a birth in 2026, is conjunct Canopus ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)), and a birthday around 6 or 7 July puts the Sun there.

Plainly, though, no planet ever comes near Canopus. Draw the great circle from the ecliptic's south pole through the star and it meets the Sun's path at 15° Cancer: that is the whole conjunction. From most of Europe the star is below the horizon even when the planet on its degree is high in the sky.

[Polaris](/astrology/polaris/), its partner in Bedouin star lore, is the same case in the north, and [Achernar](/astrology/achernar/), next on this site, another in the south. A longevity star is a lovely name and nothing more for a chart; no star can say how long anyone will live ([Can astrology predict death?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-death/)).
