A chart wheel shows where each planet stands along the zodiac, but the sky has a second dimension the wheel leaves out: how far north or south a planet sits. That second measure, taken from the celestial equator, is its declination.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Declination is the angular distance of a body north or south of the [celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/), the Earth's equator projected out onto the sky. It's counted in degrees, positive to the north and negative to the south: 0° on the equator itself, +90° at the north celestial pole, −90° at the south. Astronomers compare it to latitude on a map, set on the sky instead of the ground, and the word comes from the Latin *declinatio*, a bending away.

Paired with [right ascension](/astrology/right-ascension/), which runs east along the equator, declination gives the equatorial coordinates astronomers use to point a telescope. The zodiac belongs to a different frame, built on the ecliptic, the Sun's yearly path, which is tilted against the equator. So one planet has a zodiac position and a declination, two separate answers to two separate questions.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Sun's declination changes with the seasons, swinging north and south of the equator over the year and crossing it at the equinoxes. The planets travel close to the Sun's path, so they swing north and south as well, each at its own rhythm, and the Moon fastest of all. Compared with the stars, whose declinations creep slowly, the bodies of the Solar System change theirs quickly.

Astrologers use declination in two main ways. Two planets at the same declination, on the same side of the equator, are said to be in [parallel](/astrology/parallel/), which many read like a conjunction; at the same declination on opposite sides, they're in [contra-parallel](/astrology/contra-parallel/), often read like an opposition. And a planet whose declination goes beyond the furthest north or south the Sun ever reaches is called [out of bounds](/astrology/out-of-bounds-planets/): outside the Sun's range, and read by those who use the idea as working outside the usual rules.

The values are calculated for the moment of birth. The Moon's declination moves noticeably within a day, so a parallel to the Moon, or the Moon being out of bounds near its limit, can depend on the birth time, just as the houses and angles do. That's one more piece of a chart I wouldn't want to guess at ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Declination never changes a planet's sign or house; it adds a layer to the reading, never a new foundation. When I look at the column of declinations that most chart software prints beside the planets, I'm looking for two things: pairs at matching values, which tie planets together even when the wheel shows no aspect between them, and anything beyond the Sun's range.

A parallel can explain a link the zodiac misses. Two planets with no aspect on the wheel but the same declination can behave, in a life, as if they were in conversation, and I'd mention that link rather than leave it out. An out-of-bounds planet I describe as one that goes further than usual, in its own way: independent, unconventional, hard to fit to a template. It isn't a flaw and it isn't a talent by decree.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Declination is the same as zodiac position.”** They're measured from different circles, the equator and the ecliptic, and they answer different questions.
- **“A negative declination is a bad sign.”** The minus only means south of the equator.
- **“Out of bounds means out of control.”** It means beyond the Sun's range. Astrologers who use it read independence, not chaos.
- **“Declination replaces aspects.”** It adds parallels to them; the aspects on the wheel still stand.
