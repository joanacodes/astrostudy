Two things in astrology are called Cupido, and they have almost nothing in common but the name. One is an asteroid, number 763, a small rock you could photograph. The other is a point that a Hamburg astrologer proposed in the 1920s, beyond Neptune, where no planet has ever been seen.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Franz Kaiser found the asteroid at the Heidelberg-Königstuhl observatory on 25 September 1913. It's a small stony body, about 7 km across, in the inner part of the main belt, which it shares with [Sappho](/astrology/sappho/), found half a century earlier. It goes round the Sun in about 3 years and 4 months, between 1.9 and 2.6 times the Earth's distance from the Sun. Some astronomers count it in the Flora family of asteroids; others treat it as unattached.

It also spins oddly. Observations in 2017 showed it turning very slowly, once in about 151 hours, more than six days, and tumbling rather than rotating cleanly about one axis.

Its name is the god's Latin one. *Cupido* means passionate desire; Cupid's other Latin name, *Amor*, love, went to a later asteroid ([Amor](/astrology/amor/)), and his Greek one to the first near-Earth asteroid, [Eros](/astrology/eros/). The name was probably chosen by the Swedish astronomer Bror Asplind, because the asteroid comes relatively close to the Sun. It carries a Roman god's name the way [Bacchus](/astrology/bacchus/), found in 1977, carries the name under which the Romans worshipped Dionysus.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The god gives the asteroid its themes: desire, attraction, affection. In Ovid, Cupid carries two kinds of arrow, a golden one that fills its target with desire and a blunt leaden one that makes it flee, and both sides of that story, wanting and refusing, belong to the name.

The confusion starts with Hamburg. Alfred Witte, whose teachings founded the Hamburg School, proposed four planets beyond Neptune, which he named Cupido, Hades, Zeus and Kronos; his collaborator Friedrich Sieggrün added four more in 1927. Their rules were published in 1928, and Uranian astrologers still use these points, reading their Cupido for love, partnership and the arts ([the Hamburg School](/astrology/hamburg-school-uranian-astrology/); [Cupido (Uranian point)](/astrology/cupido-uranian-point/)). None of them has been found as a planet: their positions come from orbits worked out on paper.

So a chart that shows "Cupido" may be showing a rock in the asteroid belt or a hypothetical point far beyond Neptune. The two move at completely different speeds and land in different places. The label, or the program's settings, will tell you which one you're looking at.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The asteroid Cupido and the Uranian Cupido are the same.”** The asteroid was found in 1913 and can be observed; Witte's Cupido has never been seen by anyone.
- **“Cupido shows when you'll marry.”** No point, real or hypothetical, puts a wedding on the calendar.
- **“Astronomers named Cupido for its astrological meaning.”** It was named, probably by Bror Asplind, for its relative closeness to the Sun; what astrologers read into it came later.
- **“Cupido is only about falling in love.”** In Ovid, Cupid's arrows make people flee as well as desire.
