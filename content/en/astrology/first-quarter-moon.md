About a week after the new moon, the Moon looks as if someone had cut it neatly down the middle. Half lit, it hangs high in the sky at sunset. That half moon is the first quarter, and it is the month’s first turning point.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The first quarter is an instant: the moment the Moon is exactly 90° ahead of the Sun, as astronomers time it. On average it comes about 7.4 days after the new moon, closing the [waxing crescent](/astrology/waxing-crescent/) and opening the [waxing gibbous moon](/astrology/waxing-gibbous-moon/). The name comes from the cycle, not the shape: the Moon has gone a quarter of the way round, and what you see is half of its face lit.

Because it sits 90° from the Sun, a first quarter moon always falls three signs on from the Sun’s sign, at the same degree. With the Sun at 10° Aries, the first quarter Moon is at 10° Cancer; with the Sun at 10° Libra, at 10° Capricorn. The two lights are then in signs of the same modality, both cardinal, both fixed or both mutable, and of different elements.

Astrologers who use Dane Rudhyar’s eight phases, from *The Lunation Cycle* (1967), treat the first quarter as a phase rather than an instant: from 90° to 135°, about three and three-quarter days. It begins at the square and takes in, two or three days later, the trine at 120°, before Rudhyar’s gibbous phase begins at 135°.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A first quarter moon rises around midday, is at its highest around sunset and sets around midnight. That makes it the easiest phase to see in daylight: look for a pale half disc in the afternoon sky, away to the east of the Sun. From the northern hemisphere its right half is lit; from the southern hemisphere, its left. Through a small telescope, or even binoculars, the line between light and shadow is the best place to see craters, because the Sun there is low on the lunar landscape and the shadows are long.

The square at its heart is, in the system I built for my readings, constructive friction: two functions that rub against each other in a way that drives adaptation and effort. Friction isn’t failure. Between the Sun, which organises purpose, and the Moon, which regulates need, the waxing square is the moment the month’s growing light stops being easy and starts asking for something.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Born at a first quarter moon, you have the Sun square the Moon, waxing, and [Sun square Moon](/astrology/sun-square-moon/) describes that aspect in full. What the phase adds is direction. I read the first quarter as friction that pushes outward: the Moon has moved far enough from the Sun that what you need and what you aim for pull apart, and the way you tend to resolve it is by doing something, by acting rather than waiting. It isn’t a verdict on your character; the signs and houses of your Sun and Moon give it its colour.

The time can decide which side of the line you fall. The Moon gains about 12° a day on the Sun, so on the day of a quarter, a few hours move it from Rudhyar’s crescent phase into his first quarter ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)). [Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/) shows how to find yours.

In the sky, I don’t give the quarter moons any weight of their own in a forecast. They belong to the month’s rhythm; the new and full moons are the markers I use.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A half moon is halfway through the month.”** Halfway is the full moon. The first quarter comes a quarter of the way through.
- **“You can only see it at night.”** It rises around midday, so on clear afternoons it is already up, pale against the blue.
- **“The first quarter is a crisis.”** It is friction, and friction isn’t failure. A square asks for adjustment and effort, which is how most things grow.
- **“At the first quarter, you have to act.”** The sky doesn’t set deadlines. The phase describes a mood of the month, never an obligation on a given day.
