Dane Rudhyar was a composer before he was an astrologer. He came to both from Paris with the conviction that all existence is cyclical, and he spent his astrological life rewriting an old craft for the psychology of his own century.

## Who {#who}

He was born Daniel Chennevière in Paris on 23 March 1895. He studied at the Sorbonne and at the Paris Conservatoire, and his book *Claude Debussy et son œuvre* came out in 1913. Music took him to New York in November 1916, and on 4 April 1917 his orchestral arrangements and his own compositions were performed in a production of *Métachorie* by the Metropolitan Opera, one of the first polytonal pieces heard in the United States. Between 1917 and 1919 he took the name Rudhyar, a cognate of several Sanskrit words, among them the name of the god Rudra, and in 1926 he became an American citizen.

He kept all his trades. He set out a theory of music in *Dissonant Harmony* (1928), and he painted: one of his transcendental drawings appears in Raymond and Lila Piper's *Cosmic Art* (1975). Late in his life other composers rediscovered his music, and in 1983 Glenn Branca dedicated his Symphony No. 3, *Gloria*, to him.

Astrology reached him through the mimeographed lessons of [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/), at a time when he was also reading [Carl Jung](/astrology/carl-jung/). In the preface he wrote for the 1970 edition of his first astrological book, he recalled the thought that astrology “could be used in close connection with depth-psychology” if many of its concepts were “reformulated” for the men and women of his own time. That book, *[The Astrology of Personality](/astrology/dane-rudhyars-the-astrology-of-personality-1936/)*, appeared in 1936. From 1969 he was producing several books a year, among them *Person-centered Astrology* (1972), *An Astrological Mandala* (1973), subtitled *The Cycle of Transformations and Its 360 Symbolic Phases*, and *From Humanistic to Transpersonal Astrology* (1975), and by the mid-1970s he had moved from humanistic to what he called transpersonal astrology. He died in 1985.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He moved the chart from events to the person. In his account the planets didn't cause what happened; the chart pictured forces working in a person, who remained free to answer them. He called the result humanistic astrology ([Humanistic astrology](/astrology/humanistic-astrology/)). It's how I hold a chart too: it describes how you're inclined to move, not where you have to end up ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

He turned the Moon's month into a cycle with a meaning. *The Lunation Cycle* (1967) reads the phase of the Moon at birth as a key to personality, and some of its phase names, the balsamic moon among them, are still widely used by astrologers ([Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/); [Balsamic moon](/astrology/balsamic-moon/)). I give the phase less weight than he did, because both its ingredients, the Sun and the Moon, held less often in my notebook than Saturn, Chiron and Lilith ([my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

He gave the houses a psychology. *The Astrological Houses* (1972), subtitled *The Spectrum of Individual Experience*, reads the twelve houses as stages of a life's experience: the 1st as the emergence of consciousness, the 8th as regeneration, the 12th as transcendence. The houses are also the part of a chart that depends entirely on the birth time, which is why I don't read a chart without one ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

His book appeared four years before [Reinhold Ebertin](/astrology/reinhold-ebertin/)'s handbook of cosmobiology, and the two could hardly be further apart: Ebertin listed combinations of planets, Rudhyar wrote a philosophy of the person. Within a few decades his questions had a school of their own: astrologers such as Stephen Arroyo, whose work the scholar Kieron Le Grice traces in part to Rudhyar, made the psyche the subject of the chart ([Psychological astrology](/astrology/psychological-astrology/)).
