A planet low in the east before dawn and a planet low in the west after sunset are on opposite sides of the Sun. Which side a planet is on, the morning one or the evening one, is what oriental and occidental describe, and it changes every time the planet meets the Sun.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A planet is oriental of the Sun when it rises before the Sun, and sets before it too; it is occidental when it rises after the Sun and sets after it. Put simply, an oriental planet belongs to the morning sky and an occidental one to the evening sky, whenever either is far enough from the Sun to be seen.

In a chart, you can read it straight off the degrees. An oriental planet sits behind the Sun in the order of the signs, anywhere up to 180°; an occidental planet sits ahead of it. With the Sun at 15° Leo, a Venus at 20° Cancer rises first and is oriental, the morning star; a Venus at 10° Virgo sets last and is occidental, the evening star.

The words mean eastern and western, but here they have nothing to do with geography, and a planet can be oriental in any house. They come from what an observer saw close to the Sun: an oriental planet low in the east at dawn, an occidental one low in the west at dusk. A planet's first dawn appearance after a spell lost in the Sun's light is its [heliacal rising](/astrology/heliacal-rising/).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A planet changes sides at the moment it meets the Sun, the moment of [cazimi](/astrology/cazimi/), and, for the Moon and for the planets from Mars outwards, again at the opposition. How that plays out depends on the planet.

- **Mercury and Venus** never get far from the Sun, so they swing from side to side. After passing behind the Sun they appear as evening planets, occidental; after passing between us and the Sun, in the middle of a retrograde, they appear in the morning, oriental.
- **Mars and the planets beyond** are oriental from their conjunction with the Sun until their opposition, rising a little earlier each night, then occidental from the opposition back to the next conjunction. Since a retrograde loop is centred on the opposition, its first half falls on the oriental side and its second half on the occidental one.
- **The Moon** is occidental from new moon to full, while it waxes in the evening sky, and oriental from full moon back to new, while it wanes in the morning sky ([Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/)).

The tradition put the distinction to work in [sect](/astrology/sect-day-and-night-charts/): Mercury, which belongs to neither the day team nor the night team on its own, joined the day planets when oriental and the night planets when occidental. Like [besieging](/astrology/besieging/), which looks at a planet caught between Mars and Saturn, it judges a planet by the company it keeps rather than by its sign.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read oriental and occidental as a phase, the way I read the Moon's shape at birth: a moment in a planet's cycle with the Sun. A morning planet rises ahead of the Sun, as if announcing the day; an evening planet follows the Sun down and lingers after it has gone. Because I read the Sun as identity and purpose, I take a morning planet's function as one that tends to run ahead of your sense of self, and an evening planet's as one that tends to follow it and reflect on it. That is an interpretation, offered as a tendency, never a rule ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

For the Moon, the hour of birth decides the side only on the day of a new or full moon; for the planets, only on the day one passes the Sun or, from Mars outwards, stands opposite it. On every other day the date alone settles it ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Oriental planets sit on the left of the chart.”** The eastern half of a chart is a different idea, measured from the Ascendant ([Hemisphere emphasis](/astrology/hemisphere-emphasis/)). Oriental and occidental are measured from the Sun.
- **“An oriental planet is at its eastern elongation.”** It's the reverse. Astronomers name an elongation by where the planet stands from the Sun in the sky, so a morning planet, seen west of the Sun, is at a western elongation; astrology calls the same planet oriental because it rises first, in the east.
- **“The morning star and the evening star are two different bodies.”** Both are Venus, seen months apart on either side of the Sun.
- **“A planet is oriental only when it can be seen at dawn.”** The side is a matter of position. A planet [under the beams](/astrology/under-the-beams/) is already on one side or the other, though no one can see it.
