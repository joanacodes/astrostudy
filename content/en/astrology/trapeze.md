Some pattern names stand for one figure that everybody draws the same way. The trapeze isn't one of them. The name is used for four-planet shapes that different authors build from different aspects, and that's why this site gives no count for it.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

What the versions share is the outline the name describes: four planets whose connecting lines make a four-sided figure with one pair of parallel sides, like the trapezium of a geometry lesson. On a circle, that outline follows a simple rule. Two sides are parallel when the gaps on either side of them are equal. Go round the wheel from planet to planet: if the gaps run a, b, a, c, the first and third gaps the same size, the four planets always draw a trapeze, with the two equal gaps as its slanting sides.

Fill the gaps with aspects and you get the astrological versions, and there are many ways to do it. Three gaps of 60° and one of 180° give the [cradle](/astrology/cradle/), which has exactly this outline. Other mixes of sextiles, trines, squares and quincunxes give others, each with its own character. An author who calls one of them the trapeze isn't wrong; another who calls a different one by the same name isn't wrong either. The name fits the drawing, and the drawing fits many charts.

So when a program or a book labels a trapeze, the label tells you about the outline, not about the aspects. Look at what is actually drawn: which four planets, which aspects between them, how tight each one is. The [aspect grid](/astrology/aspect-grid/) of your chart lists them all.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The count behind this site covers figures with one agreed definition: the [minor grand trine](/astrology/minor-grand-trine/), the cradle, the grand trine and the others in this family. The trapeze is left out on purpose. A figure needs a single definition before it can be counted honestly; counted under several, it would give a number nobody could use.

I read whatever a chart shows under this name the way I'd read it without one: aspect by aspect, planet by planet. Each side of the figure is an aspect with its own meaning in the system I built for my readings: a sextile an opening to take up, a trine a flow, a square a friction that builds, an opposition a polarity to integrate, a quincunx an adjustment. The figure adds one thing to those meanings. It binds four functions together, so that what happens along one side tends to draw in the others. That's emphasis, not identity: patterns emerge from repetition, and one outline on a chart doesn't decide who you are.

Four planets mean four houses, and the time of birth decides which. A corner held by the Moon can decide whether the figure exists at all, since the Moon moves about a sign every two and a half days. That's why I [wait for the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) before reading any pattern, named or not.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Ask what the label means.** If a program shows you a trapeze, find out which definition it uses. Two programs may mean two different figures.
- **List the aspects.** Write down the four planets and every aspect between them, with its orb. That list is what you read.
- **Start with the tightest link.** The aspect closest to exact usually speaks loudest, and it's a steadier place to begin than the outline.
- **Let the name go if it doesn't help.** You lose nothing by reading the four planets directly.

The next figure in this family, the [envelope](/astrology/envelope/), shares this difficulty. With the trapeze, at least, the geometry gives you something firm to hold on to: equal gaps, parallel sides, and four planets to read.
