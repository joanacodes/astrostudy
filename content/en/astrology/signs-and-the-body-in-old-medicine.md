Open a medieval almanac and you'll often find him: a man standing with the twelve signs laid over his body, a ram at his head and two fishes at his feet. He is the Zodiac Man, and for centuries he told physicians when not to cut.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The idea that each sign governs a part of the body is called melothesia, and its best-known picture is the Zodiac Man, *homo signorum* in Latin, the man of signs. The signs run down him in their usual order:

- **[Aries](/astrology/aries/):** the head
- **Taurus:** the neck
- **Gemini:** the shoulders
- **Cancer:** the chest
- **Leo:** the sides
- **Virgo:** the belly
- **Libra:** the buttocks
- **Scorpio:** the genitals
- **Sagittarius:** the thighs
- **Capricorn:** the knees
- **Aquarius:** the lower legs
- **[Pisces](/astrology/pisces/):** the feet

That list is the oldest and the most common. Its first full account is in Manilius's *Astronomica*, a Latin poem of the 1st century AD, but a cuneiform tablet of unknown date gives almost the same list and may be older, and the idea of a body in step with the heavens has been traced back to Babylonia. Medicine went on to match planets as well as signs with parts of the body, illnesses and drugs, and the whole art came to be called iatromathematics.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The scheme's main use was timing. Medieval physicians, who saw the Moon raise the tides, feared that letting blood from a part of the body while the Moon stood in that part's sign could bring on a tide of blood no one could stop. The rule, attributed to Ptolemy, ran: don't strike a limb with iron while the Moon holds the sign that rules it. So before bleeding, surgery or a dose of medicine, someone looked up the Moon's sign, in an almanac or on a volvelle, a paper calendar with turning discs.

The figure sat inside a larger theory. Greek medicine already watched for critical days in the course of an illness, and medieval physicians worked with the four humours, blood, yellow bile, black bile and phlegm; the stars were one more frame for the same body. The Zodiac Man appeared in calendars, Books of Hours and treatises on medicine and astrology, from a 15th-century Welsh manuscript to Persian medical books, and he was still being printed in almanacs in 1702. Physicians grew wary of him long before the public did.

A Greek text even cut each sign into twelve slices and gave each slice a part of the body, joining the Zodiac Man to the twelfth-parts of the signs ([Dodecatemoria (twelfth-parts)](/astrology/dodecatemoria-twelfth-parts/)). Nicholas Culpeper published his *Astrological Judgement of Diseases* in 1655; after that century, astrological medicine declined ([Medical astrology, in history](/astrology/medical-astrology-in-history/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

This page is history, and it stops there. I don't read health in a chart, for anyone: a sign can't tell you which part of you will ail, and no position of the Moon decides when a treatment is safe ([Can astrology predict health problems?](/astrology/can-astrology-predict-health-problems/)). Symptoms, operations and medicines belong with a doctor, and the timing of a procedure with the people carrying it out.

What I keep from the signs is their manner. Aries in a chart is a way of moving, quick to begin and to test; it says nothing about headaches. Libra is a way of weighing and balancing, and its relation to Aries, across the circle, is one of the [six sign axes](/astrology/six-sign-axes/): a question with two answers, not a pair of body parts. The [6th house](/astrology/6th-house/), which tradition gave to illness, I read as daily work and daily care.

The Zodiac Man is worth knowing all the same. He shows how completely astrology once ran through ordinary life, and how far the modern practice has moved from it ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Your Sun sign shows your weak spot.”** The Zodiac Man was a calendar for timing treatment by the Moon's sign, not a diagnosis by birth sign, and no chart diagnoses anything.
- **“The scheme is medieval.”** Its first full account is Roman, in Manilius, and a cuneiform tablet suggests older roots.
- **“Each sign rules a list of organs.”** The oldest and most common list names regions of the body, head to feet. Longer lists of glands and organs are not the old scheme.
