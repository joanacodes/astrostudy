A matching test is the plainest fair question you can put to astrology: here are some charts and some people, so which goes with which? This page is about the tests that carry Vernon Clark's name, and about the limits of what I could check.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

I can't give you Clark's results from a source I could open, so I won't repeat them. Quoting figures I can't check, on a page about checking, would be the wrong way round. What I can say plainly is what the matching tests I could check found: astrologers did no better than chance once the obvious clues were taken away.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**What a matching test asks.** Astrologers receive charts and descriptions, such as personality profiles, life histories or the people's own choices, and pair them without knowing the answer. In a double-blind test, the people running it don't know the answer either, so nobody can steer the result. Chance sets the bar: with five options, one guess in five is right by luck alone.

**Clark's own tests.** None of the sources I can open describes them: who took part, what was matched, how the results were counted. A summary from memory, or copied from someone else's unsourced retelling, is exactly what a page like this should avoid. So their details are left out, and I'd rather tell you that than fill the gap.

**The matching tests I could check.** In 1985, *Nature* published Shawn Carlson's double-blind test. Twenty-eight astrologers tried to match more than 100 natal charts to personality profiles from the California Psychological Inventory, under a protocol agreed beforehand by a group of physicists and a group of astrologers. They did no better than chance ([Carlson's double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/)).

In 2003, Geoffrey Dean and Ivan Kelly pooled 40 studies, with 700 astrologers and more than 1,000 birth charts. In ten of them, with 300 participants in all, astrologers had to pick the right interpretation of a chart from among several others, usually three to five. Once the date and other obvious clues were removed, no significant result pointed to the right one. In ten further studies, people picked the horoscope they felt described them, one of which was really theirs, and again the results were no better than chance. Dean and Kelly also followed people born minutes apart, a question with its own page: [Time twins: Dean and Kelly](/astrology/time-twins-dean-and-kelly/).

**Why the clues matter.** A chart carries its date, and a date can give away a person's age or the season they were born in; a description can hint at a job or a stage of life. A test that removes those clues tests the chart; one that leaves them in may only test the clues. What birth dates do show, apart from astrology, is the subject of [Season of birth: the real effects](/astrology/season-of-birth-the-real-effects/).

None of this says astrologers are dishonest, or that the people who feel described by a chart are fooled. It says that when the question is put fairly, matching hasn't come out above chance in the studies I could read.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My notebook is not a matching test. I started with my own chart, giving each placement a tick or a cross: Sun, Moon, rising, and each planet in its sign and house. Then I did the same, with their consent, for family, friends and strangers, who marked the lines of their own charts.

About a third of what I checked held overall, 30 to 40%, and the placements everyone reads first, the Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury, are among those. The exceptions were Saturn, Lilith and Chiron, which held 75 to 90% of the time.

Everyone in it knew whose chart they were looking at, so the notebook can't answer a matching test, and it doesn't try. It's a notebook rather than a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general. What it changes is how I work: when part of a reading rests on a placement that held less often, I say so ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). The tally, line by line, is in [my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).
