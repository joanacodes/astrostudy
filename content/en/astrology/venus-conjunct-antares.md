Venus conjunct Antares is the rarest of the five contacts the Scorpion's heart receives: about 1 person in 212 born between 1930 and 2009 has Venus within a degree of it. It belongs to births between mid-October and mid-January, and to a Venus in Sagittarius.

## What it means {#what-it-means}

I read [Venus](/astrology/venus/) as how you value and how you relate: what you choose, what you find beautiful, how affection passes between you and other people. [Antares](/astrology/antares/) owes its name to another planet. In Greek it is the rival of Ares, red enough to be taken for [Mars](/astrology/mars/), so with Venus on it the tradition brings Mars's colour into the planet of attraction: affection with heat in it, and a little contest. The medieval Behenian list, which pairs Antares with Mars and Jupiter, leaves Venus out of the star's nature altogether. In the old vocabulary, this is the lesser benefic sitting on a star named for the lesser malefic ([benefics and malefics](/astrology/benefics-and-malefics/)).

I keep that as the old books' colour. Harmony, as I read Venus, was never the absence of friction, so a Venus with some Mars in its picture isn't a damaged one. Venus still does its own work in a chart, through its sign, house and aspects, and for births from 1930 to 2009 the sign is Sagittarius, at around 9° or 10° ([Venus in Sagittarius](/astrology/venus-in-sagittarius/)). The system I read with places no stars, so this page describes the contact for anyone who meets it in another astrologer's chart ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

Venus never strays more than about 47° from the Sun, so it can sit on Antares only for births between about 16 October and 17 January, with the Sun somewhere between late Libra and late Capricorn. Born before early December, you had Venus on the star as an evening star, following the Sun down after sunset; born after, as a morning star, rising ahead of it. In these eighty years it never turned round on the star: every pass was a single forward crossing, quick and unrepeated, which goes a long way to explaining why this contact is the rarest of the five.

In the sky the two stayed apart. Most planets pass north of Antares, which lies 4.6° below the ecliptic, and from 1930 to 2009 Venus did too, by between about one and a half and nine degrees. Its path is shifting, though: from 2117 to 2157, every eight years, Venus will slip just south of the star.

The star's other contacts: the [Sun conjunct Antares](/astrology/sun-conjunct-antares/), on 1–2 December, never coincided with Venus on the same degree in these eighty years, though Venus is never far from the Sun; [Mars conjunct Antares](/astrology/mars-conjunct-antares/), the planet the star is named against, is nearly as rare as this one, about 1 in 210; the [Moon conjunct Antares](/astrology/moon-conjunct-antares/) is the most frequent, about 1 in 171; and [Saturn conjunct Antares](/astrology/saturn-conjunct-antares/) belongs to two stretches, in 1956–57 and 1986. [Conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/) explains the one-degree rule they share, and [fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/) the tradition it comes from.
