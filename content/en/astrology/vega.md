Vega marked the north around 12,000 BCE, when the Earth's axis pointed close to it, and the slow wobble of that axis will bring the pole back near it in about twelve thousand years. Astrology gives it a quieter address: a degree in mid-Capricorn, and a place on a medieval list of fifteen stars used for talismans.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Vega is α Lyrae, the brightest star of the Lyre, a small constellation of the northern sky. Its magnitude is 0.03, and on that scale a lower number means a brighter star: Vega is the fifth-brightest of the night, so close to zero that astronomers once averaged it with a few similar stars to set magnitude zero itself. It sits at the right angle of the Summer Triangle, the asterism it makes with [Altair](/astrology/altair/) and [Deneb Adige](/astrology/deneb-adige/).

Its tropical degree for 2026 is 15°40′ [Capricorn](/astrology/capricorn/). Charts from 2000 put it at 15°18′ and charts from 1930 at 14°20′, because the [precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/) moves every star on by about a degree in 72 years; at that pace Vega has roughly a thousand years of Capricorn ahead of it.

The figure that matters most for a chart is its latitude, the distance north or south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), the Sun's path. Vega's is 61.7° north. The Moon never strays much more than 5° from that path and the planets stray less, so nothing in a birth chart ever comes near Vega in the sky. A planet "on" Vega shares its zodiac degree, and only that ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

The name is Arabic: an-nasr al-wāqi', the falling eagle, from a picture of the Lyre as a bird diving with its wings folded. Latin made it Vultur cadens, the name under which Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa printed Vega's sign in his *De occulta philosophia* (1533).

Agrippa's stars are the Behenian stars, fifteen bright stars that the medieval astrology of Europe and the Arab world singled out for magic. Each was thought a source of power for one or two planets, and each had a gemstone and a plant used to draw its influence into a talisman, an influence thought strongest when a planet came within six degrees of the star. Vega's are chrysolite and winter savory, and its planets [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/) and [Venus](/astrology/venus/). [Alphecca](/astrology/alphecca/) and [Deneb Algedi](/astrology/deneb-algedi/) are on the same list.

Further east Vega carries a love story rather than a talisman. In China it is the Weaving Girl, kept from the Cowherd by the Milky Way except on the seventh night of the seventh month, when magpies make a bridge: the Qixi festival, and Tanabata in Japan. In India it stands for Abhijit, a lunar mansion of [Vedic astrology (Jyotish)](/astrology/vedic-astrology-jyotish/).

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

A star enters a birth chart through a conjunction: a planet or angle within about a degree of it, by zodiac position ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). For a birth in 2026, Vega's window runs from 14°40′ to 16°40′ Capricorn; for one in 1930, from 13°20′ to 15°20′.

The Sun crosses it on about 5 and 6 January. Early January is when the Sun moves fastest, near the Earth's closest approach to it, so the window goes by a little sooner than a summer star's: about 1 person in 185 born from 1930 to 2009 has the Sun on Vega, against nearer 1 in 175 for the stars the Sun meets in June and July. Astrologers who work with stars would lay Vega's two planets, Mercury and Venus, over that Sun, which keeps its own meaning all the same: a [Sun in Capricorn](/astrology/sun-in-capricorn/) first.
