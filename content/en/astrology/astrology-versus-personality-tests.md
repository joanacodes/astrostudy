Astrology and personality tests often turn up in the same mood: curious about yourself, a little sceptical, hoping for a few words that fit. People lump them together, from both directions, and that isn't quite fair to either. Here's how each has fared when researchers checked it.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

They aren't the same kind of thing. A personality test is a questionnaire about how you see yourself, and psychologists check it in two main ways: does it give the same result twice, and does it predict anything outside itself? On those checks, the Big Five does well and the MBTI noticeably less well. A birth chart is read from your date, time and place of birth, with no questions asked, and when astrologers tried to match charts to personality profiles in a controlled test, they did no better than chance.

Whatever their merits, I don't think any of them should decide who gets a job, and I never read anyone for hiring, firing or promotion.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**The Big Five.** The five-factor model describes personality with five traits, each a continuous scale rather than a box: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. It grew out of research into the words people use to describe themselves, grouped by statistics into clusters. Its structure has been replicated across cultures and over time, and it predicts things beyond the questionnaire: conscientiousness, for instance, predicts job performance and academic success. Its critics have a point too. One psychologist called it a "psychology of the stranger": it captures what is easy to see in someone you've just met, and leaves out the more private, context-dependent sides of a person. I haven't found retest figures for it that I could check, so I don't quote any.

**The MBTI.** The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator, built by Katharine Cook Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers from Carl Jung's Psychological Types, sorts people into sixteen types from four either-or preferences. Reliability is its weak point: between 39% and 76% of people get a different type when they retake it after only five weeks. Most people's scores also sit near the middle of each scale, where a cut-off at the centre can give two almost identical scores two different letters, and critics have argued its type descriptions are general enough to fit almost anyone. Not every study is so harsh: a 2002 meta-analysis of reliability studies found strong internal consistency and test-retest estimates, though with variation. The researchers disagree, and you should know that they do.

**Astrology, tested against them.** In Shawn Carlson's double-blind test, published in Nature in 1985, astrologers tried to match more than a hundred birth charts to profiles from a standard personality questionnaire, the California Psychological Inventory, and did no better than chance ([Carlson's double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/)). A questionnaire can be wrong about you too; the difference is that it can be checked this way, again and again, by people who didn't write it.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

My notebook was a test of a sort, so it's worth being clear about which sort. I went through my own chart [like an invoice](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), then the charts of family, friends and strangers, with their consent: a tick if a placement described the person in a way I couldn't have written about anyone else, a cross if it was vague, wrong, or would have fitted their neighbour just as well. Overall, about a third held, **30 to 40%**, which is close to what a flattering horoscope scores, so I hold those placements lightly ([The Barnum (Forer) effect](/astrology/barnum-forer-effect/)). **Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90% of the time.**

Next to the checks a questionnaire like the Big Five has been through, my notebook is nothing of the kind. It's a hand count in a notebook, not audited, mostly on people I knew: a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general. What it does is decide where I spend time in a reading, and why I tell you when something rests on a placement that held less often.

A personality test and a reading can sit side by side, as two ways of thinking about yourself, but neither is a verdict on who you are.
