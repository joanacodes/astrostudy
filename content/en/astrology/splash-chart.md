Some charts refuse to be summed up at a glance. Their planets sit all round the wheel, in many signs and many houses, with no obvious crowd and no great empty space. Marc Edmund Jones called that a splash.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

The splash is the shape with the least shape. In the description Wikipedia gives from its sources, the planets are distributed round the chart in a splash, as the name suggests: spread out rather than grouped, a pattern "or rather lack of pattern". Where the other shapes [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) named are defined by an empty stretch, a cluster or a gap, this one is defined by having none of them to speak of.

Its neighbours mark its edges. When the planets settle into two groups facing each other across empty space, the chart moves towards the [seesaw](/astrology/seesaw-chart/); when they gather into a few irregular clusters with gaps between, towards the [splay](/astrology/splay-chart/). Authors draw those edges in different places, and the planets in a splash still make aspects of every kind to each other, so the shape tells you about spacing and nothing else.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

There's no figure for this one. Authors define the splash too differently, and the count behind this site only measures shapes with one agreed rule: the bundle, the bowl, the bucket and the locomotive. A number for the splash would depend on where I chose to draw its edges, which is no help to you.

Astrologers who use the shape tend to read breadth in it: wide interests, many connections, an outlook that takes in a great deal, and a risk of spreading energy thin. I'd keep the breadth and hold the warning lightly. The system I built for my readings holds that no single factor determines what dominates a chart, and a splash makes that plain: when the shape itself gives no emphasis, the emphasis has to come from the planets. So I look first for what organises a splash. The tightest aspects, the planets close to the angles, the [chart ruler](/astrology/chart-ruler/) and where it sits: those usually show what a spread-out chart is about. A splash also changes how I read the [empty houses](/astrology/empty-houses/): more houses hold a planet and fewer stand empty, so the chart reads like a set of short chapters rather than one long one.

Two of those need the time of birth: the angles, and with them the chart ruler, the ruler of the rising sign (Scorpio, Aquarius and Pisces have two, and I read both). A splash also touches many houses, and the time decides which are filled and which stay empty. Without it, a splash is a scattering of signs with nothing to organise it, which is why I [don't read a chart without the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/).

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Choose a few.** Breadth is a resource; it becomes a strain when everything gets equal attention at once. Pick the areas that matter this year.
- **Find the organising planets.** The chart ruler, the planets on the angles and the tightest aspects give a splash its centre. Lean on them.
- **Count the range as a skill.** Being able to move between many worlds is useful, at work, in friendship, in a crisis.
- **Look for repetition.** A theme that turns up in two or three separate places matters more than any single planet in a spread-out chart.
- **Let some things wait.** Not every interest needs feeding at the same time. Rotating them is a way of keeping them all.

A splash has a little of everything, and the art of reading one is deciding where to look first.
