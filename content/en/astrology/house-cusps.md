Every house of a birth chart begins somewhere, at a precise degree of the zodiac, and that starting line is its cusp. It's where the house takes its sign, and so where its story starts.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A cusp is a single degree: the line on the chart wheel where one house ends and the next begins. The house runs from its own cusp to the next one. In Placidus, the house system I use, and the other quadrant systems, four of the twelve cusps are the angles: the [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/) opens the 1st house, the IC the 4th, the Descendant the 7th and the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/) the 10th, which is why those four are called the [angular houses](/astrology/angular-succedent-and-cadent-houses/). The other eight cusps are calculated between them, and that calculation is where house systems part ways ([which house system should I use?](/astrology/which-house-system-should-i-use/)).

The word comes from the Latin *cuspis*, a point. Astrology also uses *cusp* loosely for the edge between two signs, as in [born on the cusp](/astrology/born-on-the-cusp/): that's a different line, and it has nothing to do with the houses.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The sign on a cusp gives the house its style, and that sign's ruler carries the house's story to wherever the planet sits ([house rulers](/astrology/house-rulers/)). So a house can be read even when no planet is in it: its cusp and its ruler already say a great deal.

In Placidus, the houses are rarely 30° each. Placidus divides the sky by time: each house corresponds to a third of the time a degree of the zodiac takes to travel between the horizon and the meridian ([Placidus](/astrology/placidus/)). Because the zodiac rises at a slant that changes through the day, steeper or gentler depending on your latitude, some houses come out wide and others narrow. One sign can then open two houses while another opens none, sitting entirely inside a house ([intercepted signs](/astrology/intercepted-signs/)).

Opposite cusps always mirror each other: the 7th cusp is the same degree as the 1st in the opposite sign, the 8th mirrors the 2nd, and so on round. And the whole set turns with the sky, through the entire zodiac in about a day. Four minutes move the horizon by a degree.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

I read each house from its cusp, in Placidus: the sign there first, then its ruler, then any planets inside. The angles carry the most weight among the cusps, and the rising sign most of all.

Cusps are also where the birth time shows how much it matters. A planet a degree or two from a cusp can change house with a few minutes' difference, and a time rounded to the hour can shift every cusp by several degrees. So when a planet sits close to one, I check how exact the time is before leaning on its house ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). Some astrologers count a planet a few degrees before a cusp as already in the next house; others keep it strictly where it falls. Either way, a planet near a cusp is one to read with care ([how I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Every house is 30°.”** Only in equal and Whole Sign houses. In Placidus, a house can be much wider or narrower, depending on where and when you were born.
- **“A cusp and being born on the cusp are the same thing.”** One is the start of a house; the other is a birthday close to the Sun's change of sign.
- **“Each sign sits on exactly one cusp.”** In Placidus a sign can open two houses, or none at all when it's intercepted.
- **“A planet on a cusp is in both houses.”** It's in one or the other, at its exact degree. Near the line, the birth time decides which, and the neighbouring house's themes are never far away.
