Every planet that passes the Sun crosses, for somewhere between an hour and a few days, a sliver of sky no wider than the Sun itself. That sliver is cazimi, and what tradition makes of it runs opposite to everything around it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A planet is cazimi when it stands within 17 minutes of arc of the Sun, about 0.28°. The Sun's disc is about 32 minutes across, so a cazimi planet sits, along the zodiac, roughly within the width of the disc. The old term means in the heart of the Sun.

Around that heart, the tradition saw only weakness. Within about 15° a planet is [under the beams](/astrology/under-the-beams/), hidden by the Sun's light; closer in it is [combust](/astrology/combustion/), more deeply lost. At the centre the judgement turns over, and the conjunction is read as one of great strength ([Conjunction](/astrology/conjunction/)). A planet that close is no longer hidden by the Sun; it is with it.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The window is tiny, 34 minutes of arc from edge to edge, so no planet stays in it long. The Moon, which gains about half a degree an hour on the Sun, crosses it in about an hour at every [new moon](/astrology/new-moon/). The planets take longer, from a few hours to a few days, depending on how fast each one moves against the Sun at that moment ([Planetary speed](/astrology/planetary-speed/)).

The 17 minutes are a distance in longitude. Up in the sky, a planet usually slips a little above or below the Sun's disc rather than across it. Only now and then does the line-up hold in latitude too. [Mercury](/astrology/mercury/) then crosses the face of the Sun as a tiny black dot, visible only through a protected telescope: 13 or 14 times a century, most recently on 11 November 2019, next on 13 November 2032. When the Moon does it, near one of its nodes, the result is a [solar eclipse](/astrology/solar-eclipses/).

The moment of cazimi is also the moment a planet changes sides. Just before it, the planet is on one side of the Sun; just after, on the other, moving from the evening sky to the morning sky or the reverse ([Oriental and occidental](/astrology/oriental-and-occidental/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In the system I built for my readings, the Sun organises identity, purpose and coherence, so a planet at its heart is a function at the centre of who you are. A cazimi Mercury puts your way of thinking right inside your sense of self; a cazimi Venus does the same with what you value and love; a cazimi Mars with how you act. Tradition called that a strength, and I agree that it can be a defining one: the function isn't drowned out by your identity, it is part of its core.

It is also rare: a planet spends hours or days in that window, once or twice in a cycle of months or years. When a chart has one, I still read it through its sign, its house and its aspects. Sitting at the heart of the Sun says where the function lives in you, not everything it does.

Timing matters more here than with almost anything else in a chart. A cazimi Moon lasts about an hour, so only an exact birth time can confirm it; a cazimi planet near the edge of its 17 minutes can depend on the hour as well ([The Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Cazimi is just a very tight combustion.”** Tradition reads it the other way: the one place near the Sun where a planet is strengthened rather than hidden.
- **“Any planet conjunct the Sun is cazimi.”** Only within 17 minutes of arc. A conjunction of a few degrees is combust or under the beams.
- **“A cazimi planet is visible against the Sun.”** Almost never. It usually passes above or below the disc, and the rare transits need a protected telescope.
- **“A cazimi planet rules the whole chart.”** It is central to the self, which is not the same as dominating every other part of the chart.
