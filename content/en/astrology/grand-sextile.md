Of all the patterns in the count behind this site, this is the one you're least likely to find in your own chart. Six planets placed evenly round the wheel, each a sixth of the circle from the next, draw a six-pointed star.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

A grand sextile needs six planets, each about 60° from the next, all the way round. Every planet is then [sextile](/astrology/sextile/) its two neighbours, [trine](/astrology/trine/) the two beyond them and opposite the one across the chart: six sextiles, six trines and three [oppositions](/astrology/opposition/), fifteen aspects among six planets. Join the neighbours and you draw a hexagon; join every second planet and two triangles appear, laid over each other into a star.

That star is built from other figures. Its two triangles are [grand trines](/astrology/grand-trine/), each in its own element. Any two of the oppositions, with the sextiles and trines between their ends, make a [mystic rectangle](/astrology/mystic-rectangle/), three in all. And each grand trine, with a planet of the other triangle opposite one of its corners, makes a kite: six of those. In signs, all six planets usually share a polarity, fire and air or earth and water, because every aspect in the figure joins signs an even number apart: Aries, Gemini, Leo, Libra, Sagittarius and Aquarius, for instance.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The count found about 1 person in 7,305 born from 1930 to 2009 with a grand sextile among the ten planets, with orbs of 8° when the Sun or Moon is involved and 6° otherwise: by a long way the rarest pattern it counted. The reason is plain. It needs six of the ten planets in six evenly spaced places at once, and the slow planets, which hold their positions for years, have to be in the right ones. A wider orb would find a few more loose stars. A real chart can also complete one with a point the count ignores, the Ascendant, the Midheaven, Chiron, Lilith or a node.

Rare isn't the same as special, and I'm careful about the difference. A grand sextile joins six functions in a web of flow, the trines, openings, the sextiles, and polarity, the oppositions. I read it as a chart in which much connects to much: what moves in one part tends to travel to the others. The oppositions keep it from being only easy, and the soft links keep the oppositions from being only strain. But six planets in one figure are still six functions, each in its own sign, with four more planets outside the star. Patterns come from repetition, and no single factor decides a chart, however rare it is.

Six planets usually mean six houses, and which six depends on the time of birth. If the Moon is one of the points, the star may only exist for a day or so, since the Moon moves about a sign every two and a half days. The houses and the Moon are why the [birth time comes first](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) in any reading I do, this one included.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Read it piece by piece.** Take the two grand trines, then the three oppositions, then the whole. The figure is easier to live with once you know its parts.
- **Let the oppositions work.** They're the star's tension, and its use. Without them, so much ease could simply circle.
- **Look outside the star.** The four planets that aren't in it often carry what the figure can't: friction, distance, a reason to change.
- **Hold the rarity lightly.** A rare pattern is a curiosity to understand, not a credential to live up to.

Its neighbour in this family, [Thor’s hammer](/astrology/thors-hammer/), is the opposite in almost every way: three planets instead of six, harder aspects, and far more common. A six-pointed star is a lovely thing to draw on a chart; the reading is in the six planets that draw it.
