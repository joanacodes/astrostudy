A chart wheel sets the planets out along the zodiac. A declination chart sets them out by how far north or south of the celestial equator they stand, and lines up a set of contacts the wheel can't show.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

[Declination](/astrology/declination/) is a planet's distance north or south of the [celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/). A declination chart is a scale of it: the equator as the zero line, north above and south below, and a line on each side at about 23°26′, the furthest the Sun ever goes ([Solstices and equinoxes](/astrology/solstices-and-equinoxes/)). Each planet sits at its own height.

Read across, the scale gives three things. Planets at the same height on the same side are in [parallel](/astrology/parallel/); at the same height on opposite sides, in [contra-parallel](/astrology/contra-parallel/); beyond either of the Sun's lines, [out of bounds](/astrology/out-of-bounds-planets/). Set out over weeks or months instead of a single moment, the same scale turns each body into a wave: the Sun's rises and falls once a year, the Moon's every 27.3 days.

Equal declinations mattered long ago in India. The *Sūrya Siddhānta*, an astronomical treatise from around the 4th or 5th century, revised until about the 10th, names two moments when the Sun and the Moon stand at the same declination: *vyatipāta*, on the same side of the equator, and *vaidhṛti*, on opposite sides ([Vedic astrology (Jyotish)](/astrology/vedic-astrology-jyotish/)). *Pāta* means a fall, or a calamity, and both were held unlucky; why, notes the treatise's 1860 English translation, "is not easy to discover".

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

Most chart software prints a column of declinations beside the planets; the chart is those numbers drawn to scale. For a point on the zodiac itself, the declination follows from the longitude alone: its sine is the sine of the Earth's tilt times the sine of the longitude. The *Sūrya Siddhānta* already gives that rule, with a greatest declination of about 24°. Along the zodiac, it comes to:

- **0° Aries and 0° Libra:** 0°, on the equator.
- **10° Aries:** 3°58′ north; **10° Libra:** 3°58′ south.
- **15° Taurus:** 16°20′ north.
- **0° Gemini:** 20°09′ north.
- **0° Cancer:** 23°26′ north, the Sun's limit, and **0° Capricorn** the same to the south.

Aries to Virgo lie north of the equator, Libra to Pisces south. Most bodies, though, travel a little off the zodiac's line, and their latitude adds or subtracts. A Moon at 15° Taurus, 5° north of the ecliptic, stands at 21°07′ north; 5° south of it, at 11°33′. Between 1900 and 2100, by The Astro Study's count, the Moon strays up to about 5° from the ecliptic, Venus about 9° and Pluto about 18°, so a sign and degree alone can't give a planet's declination.

The Moon is the one that needs the birth time: its declination changes by up to about a third of a degree an hour ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

Contacts the wheel misses. Two planets with no aspect between them can stand at the same height, and the scale shows the match at a glance. On the zodiac's own line, equal declinations fall in mirror pairs ([Antiscia and contra-antiscia](/astrology/antiscia-and-contra-antiscia/)). Drawn over time, the waves show when a moving planet reaches the height a natal one holds, and where each planet turns at its furthest north or south.

I don't draw declination charts. I check the column of numbers for matching values and for anything beyond the Sun's range, and whatever I find sits on top of the signs, houses and aspects ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). The notebook in which I tested my own chart checked planets by sign and house, so I have no record of how declination holds up ([The invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

## Its limits {#its-limits}

Its contacts are a minority interest: parallels and contra-parallels are not widely used by astrologers.

The scale is uneven. Near the equinoxes, declination changes by about 0.4° for every degree along the zodiac; near the solstices it hardly changes at all. Every point from 15° Gemini to 15° Cancer lies between 22°36′ and 23°26′ north, so any two points of the zodiac on that stretch of 30° are within a degree of parallel. Matches come cheap there and dear near the equinoxes.

It depends on where you stand. A chart, wheel or scale, is measured from the centre of the Earth. From your birthplace, the Moon's declination can differ by up to about 0.95°, enough to make or break a close parallel.

And it gives up the zodiac. A declination chart shows how high or low each planet stands, not where it is along the zodiac or which way it is moving there. Other frames trade differently: [harmonic charts](/astrology/harmonic-charts/) multiply the zodiac, and the [Uranian 90° dial](/astrology/uranian-90-dial/) folds it into quarters.
