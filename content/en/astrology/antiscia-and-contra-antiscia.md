Fold the zodiac along the line of the solstices and every degree lands on another. That partner is its antiscion. Fold it along the equinoxes instead and you get the contra-antiscion. Both are old ways of seeing a link between two planets that no aspect joins.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The axis of the solstices runs from 0° [Cancer](/astrology/cancer/), where the Sun stands at midsummer in the northern hemisphere, to 0° [Capricorn](/astrology/capricorn/), at midwinter ([solstices and equinoxes](/astrology/solstices-and-equinoxes/)). An antiscion is a degree's reflection across that line, the same distance from it on the other side. The signs pair off accordingly:

- Aries with Virgo, and Taurus with Leo;
- Gemini with Cancer, the two signs either side of midsummer;
- Libra with Pisces, and Scorpio with Aquarius;
- Sagittarius with Capricorn, either side of midwinter.

The degree mirrors too: take it away from 30. Venus at 10° Taurus has its antiscion at 20° Leo; a Mars at 5°15′ Aries, at 24°45′ Virgo.

The contra-antiscion is the reflection across the other axis, the equinoxes, 0° Aries to 0° Libra. It always falls exactly opposite the antiscion, so Venus at 10° Taurus has its contra-antiscion at 20° Aquarius.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The mirror isn't arbitrary: it follows the Sun. Degrees in antiscia sit at the same [declination](/astrology/declination/), the same distance north or south of the celestial equator. When the Sun stands on either of them, it rises at the same point of the horizon and the day lasts as long. Around 5 June the Sun is near 15° Gemini; around 7 July, near 15° Cancer: two days of the same length, one each side of the longest. That's why antiscia are close kin of the [parallel](/astrology/parallel/), which joins two planets at the same declination.

Contra-antiscia work the other way round. Their declinations are equal and opposite, one north, one south, so the day at one matches the night at the other: early June's daylight is as long as early January's night, with the Sun near 15° Capricorn. Their partner among the declination contacts is the [contra-parallel](/astrology/contra-parallel/).

Hellenistic astrologers saw the same symmetry from the horizon ([Hellenistic astrology](/astrology/hellenistic-astrology/)). Signs equally far from the equinoxes, Virgo and Libra for instance, take the same time to rise, and they called such signs like-ascending. They read a working link between signs paired either way, by antiscia or by contra-antiscia, though probably a weaker one than an aspect gives. Centuries later, in his *Astrologia Gallica* (published in 1661), [Morinus](/astrology/morinus/) counted the antiscia of planets among the points that [primary directions](/astrology/primary-directions/) bring to the natal places.

Antiscia need tight [orbs](/astrology/orbs/): a degree or so. A planet's mirror point moves only as fast as the planet, a degree or two a day at most, the Moon's about thirteen; those of the Ascendant and Midheaven change by the minute, like the angles themselves ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Antiscia aren't among the contacts my readings count. The system I read with works with the planets in their signs, houses and aspects ([how I read](/how-i-read/)), and two planets in antiscia, with no aspect between them, don't come into it. Nor did my notebook test them: it went placement by placement, a tick or a cross for each sign and house.

If you work with them, a few habits help. Keep the orb to a degree or so. Check whether the two planets are also in parallel, since that's the same idea measured on the sky rather than the zodiac. And read a planet on another's antiscion as a quiet link between two functions, never as a verdict on either.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The antiscion is just the opposite degree.”** The opposite of 10° Taurus is 10° Scorpio. Its antiscion is 20° Leo; its contra-antiscion, 20° Aquarius.
- **“Antiscia are a modern invention.”** The mirror pairs go back to Hellenistic astrology, and Morinus was using them in the 17th century.
- **“Contra-antiscia are bad and antiscia good.”** They're two mirrors, across two axes. Neither passes judgement on a planet.
- **“Antiscia and parallels always agree.”** Only for points on the ecliptic. A planet's latitude can pull its declination away from its mirror's.
