Once in a blue moon means hardly ever, but the calendar kind comes round every two or three years. There are two definitions of it, and the more popular one began as a misreading.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The older sense comes from the *Maine Farmers’ Almanac* for 1937, which called the third full moon of a season with four full moons a blue moon. The folklorist Philip Hiscock traced the calendar meaning back to that almanac in *Sky & Telescope* in March 1999, and two months later, in the same magazine, Donald Olson, Richard Fienberg and Roger Sinnott worked out the rule the almanac had been using.

The popular sense, the second full moon in a calendar month, came from a misunderstanding. In *Sky & Telescope* in March 1946, James Hugh Pruett took the almanac’s blue moon to mean the second full moon in a month. His reading spread, and it is the one you will meet most often today. Neither sense is an astronomical term. Both are names that the calendar lays over the ordinary lunar cycle.

The blue moon has a counterpart at the new moon, the [black moon (the calendar one)](/astrology/black-moon-calendar/), and a cousin in the [micromoon](/astrology/micromoon/): each names a particular full or new moon rather than a different kind of Moon.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

A lunation lasts about 29½ days, shorter than every calendar month but February, so the date of the full moon slips back by a day or so each month, and every two or three years a month that begins with a full moon fits a second one before it ends. In the same way, a season of about three months usually holds three full moons and now and then four.

In the dates I use, in Universal Time, the calendar kind falls on 31 May 2026, at 9°56′ Sagittarius, after the full moon of 1 May in Scorpio, and on 31 December 2028, at 10°33′ Cancer, after that of 2 December in Gemini. The second is also a total lunar eclipse. Time zones matter here: the full moon of 31 December 2028 comes at 16:48 UT, which is already 1 January 2029 for anyone eight hours or more ahead of Greenwich, east Asia and Australia included.

Counted by the astronomical seasons, from equinox to solstice and solstice to equinox, the seasonal kind falls on 20 May 2027, with the Moon at 29°13′ Scorpio, and on 24 August 2029, at 1°12′ Pisces. The almanac itself used seasons of equal length counted from a fixed date, so its own list doesn’t always pick the same full moon.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

A blue moon is a [full moon](/astrology/full-moon/) like any other, and I read it exactly as one: by the Moon’s sign, with the Sun opposite, and only when it falls within a degree of a point in your chart, or of the point opposite, that something slower is already working on. The blue moon of 31 May 2026 is a [Full moon in Sagittarius](/astrology/full-moon-in-sagittarius/) first, and blue only by the calendar’s count. The one of 31 December 2028 is a full moon in Cancer and a total eclipse, and it is the eclipse, not the name, that can give it weight in my reading: an eclipse counts within about 3° of a natal point, at either end of its axis ([Lunar eclipses](/astrology/lunar-eclipses/)).

So whether a full moon is called blue says something about our months, not about the sky, and nothing about you. That is [how I read](/how-i-read/) every lunation: weather, not a forecast, whatever the calendar calls it.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A blue moon is blue.”** It looks like every other full moon. A bluish Moon is a rare effect of smoke or volcanic ash in the air, and it can happen at any phase, on any date.
- **“Blue moons are very rare.”** The saying makes them sound it, but in the calendar sense one comes every two or three years, and the seasonal kind about as often.
- **“The blue moon is an ancient folk name.”** Its calendar senses go back to a 1937 American almanac and a 1946 magazine article. Nobody has traced an older use of the term for a particular full moon.
- **“A blue moon is a lucky, or unlucky, full moon.”** The calendar gave it its name; the sky didn’t change. In a chart it is read like any full moon in its sign.
