Before software, every astrologer worked from a book of tables, and the tables are still there underneath every chart an app draws. An ephemeris looks forbidding for about five minutes. After that, it's the quickest way there is to watch the sky move.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

An ephemeris, plural ephemerides, is a table of positions: where the Sun, the Moon and the planets are, listed day by day for a month or a year at a time. The word comes from the Greek *ephēmeris*, a daily record. Astronomers and navigators have their own, far more detailed ones. An astrologer's ephemeris gives each body's place in the tropical zodiac as seen from Earth, in signs, degrees and minutes.

Most editions hold more than the planets. You'll usually find the lunar nodes, often Chiron and sometimes Lilith; a column of [sidereal time](/astrology/sidereal-time/), used to work out the houses; and notes, at the foot or in the margins, of the days a planet changes sign or turns retrograde or direct, and of each new moon, full moon and eclipse. Every chart is built from these numbers, whether you look them up yourself or a program does it for you ([Calculating a chart by hand](/astrology/calculating-a-chart-by-hand/)).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Reading one is mostly a matter of knowing where to look.

1. **Check the heading.** Note the month and the year, and above all the time the positions are for: usually midnight or noon in Universal Time, Greenwich time without daylight saving. Two ephemerides for the same day can disagree simply because one is set for midnight and the other for noon.
2. **Find the row and the column.** Each row is a day and each column a body, headed by its symbol: ☉ for the Sun, ☽ for the Moon, then the planets in order ([Glyphs: the cheat sheet](/astrology/glyphs-the-cheat-sheet/)).
3. **Read the figure.** A position such as 16♏53 means 16 degrees and 53 minutes of Scorpio ([degrees, minutes and seconds](/astrology/degrees-minutes-and-seconds/)). The sign's glyph is often printed only where it changes, so read up the column to find which sign you're in.
4. **Watch for R and D.** An R, often written ℞, marks the day a planet stations retrograde; a D marks the day it turns direct. In between, its figures shrink from one day to the next ([Retrograde, explained](/astrology/retrograde-explained/)).
5. **Work out the hour.** Convert your local time to Universal Time, minding daylight saving, then estimate how far each body has moved since the listed time. For most planets it hardly matters. The Moon covers about 13° a day, so at noon it's some 6½° past its midnight figure.

Here's November 2026 as an ephemeris shows it. Mercury has been retrograde in Scorpio since 24 October, when it stationed at 20°58′; its figures fall day by day until it turns direct on 13 November at 5°02′ Scorpio. Venus turns direct the next day at 22°51′ Libra. The new moon comes on 9 November at 07:02 UT, at 16°53′ Scorpio, and the full moon on 24 November at 14:54 UT, at 2°20′ Gemini ([New and full moon calendar](/sky/moon-calendar/)).

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

You won't see an ephemeris in a reading, but every position in your chart came from one, worked out for your minute and your place. Knowing how to read it is still useful, for one reason above all: it lets you check. If you were born on a day the Sun or the Moon changed sign, the ephemeris shows the hour it happened, and whether your birth falls before or after it ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

It's also where the sky's weather comes from. A retrograde, an ingress, a new moon in your fifth house: each is a line in an ephemeris before it's a sentence in a reading. When I describe a transit, I read it as weather, never as a forecast, and an ephemeris is a good reminder why: it lists where the planets will be, not what will happen to anyone. A [horoscope](/astrology/horoscope/) column starts from the same tables; a reading lays them over your own chart ([Chart wheel, and how to read it](/astrology/chart-wheel-and-how-to-read-it/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Ephemerides are an astrologers' invention.”** Astronomers and navigators publish them too, with far more precision. Astrologers use the same positions.
- **“All ephemerides give the same figures.”** They agree on the sky, but not always on the hour: some list midnight, some noon. A wrong assumption there moves the Moon by several degrees.
- **“R means the planet really goes backwards.”** It means it seems to, seen from Earth. Nothing in space reverses.
- **“Apps have made ephemerides pointless.”** Apps read from them. Being able to open one yourself is how you catch an app's slip, or a wrong time zone.
