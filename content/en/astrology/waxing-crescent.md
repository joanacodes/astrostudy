The waxing crescent is the first Moon anyone sees after a new moon: a fine curve low in the west at dusk, gone a few hours after the Sun. Night by night it thickens and climbs, until half the disc is lit.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

In the sky, the waxing crescent is the stretch from just after the [new moon](/astrology/new-moon/) to the [first quarter moon](/astrology/first-quarter-moon/): the Moon 0° to 90° ahead of the Sun, less than half lit, and growing. Waxing is the old word for growing. It lasts about a week, and you find it in the evening, in the west, setting a little later each night, by about fifty minutes on average.

Which side is lit depends on where you stand. From the northern hemisphere, a waxing crescent is lit on the right, curving like the letter D in the making; from the southern hemisphere, on the left. In its first evenings you can often see the rest of the disc too, faintly: that glow is earthshine, sunlight reflected from Earth onto the Moon’s night side.

Astrologers who work with Dane Rudhyar’s eight phases, from *The Lunation Cycle* (1967), divide the month differently. For them, the first 45° after the new moon still belong to the new moon phase, and the crescent phase runs from 45° to 90°, about three and three-quarter days. So for the first three or four days, the sky’s crescent is still the astrologer’s new moon.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Moon gains about 12° a day on the Sun, so during the crescent it moves from the Sun’s own sign to about three signs on. Two of the aspects fall inside the stretch: the semisquare at 45°, where Rudhyar’s crescent begins, and the sextile at 60°, a third of the way to the full moon in the sky’s terms. The system I built for my readings describes the sextile as cooperation and opportunity, an opening that asks to be taken up, and the semisquare as small, local tension that corrects course as it goes. Between them, they sketch the crescent well: a start under way, adjusting as it grows.

None of this needs a telescope or a chart. Look west on a clear evening two or three days after a new moon and the crescent is there, still close to where the Sun went down.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Born under a waxing crescent, your Moon was a little ahead of your Sun, usually one to three signs on, and if it was near 60° ahead, you have a [Sun sextile Moon](/astrology/sun-sextile-moon/). I read the crescent birth as needs beginning to strike out on their own. The Moon has left the Sun’s side, so what you need is no longer simply what you aim for; the two can help each other, and the crescent asks you to make the effort that lets them. That describes a rhythm, not a fate, and the signs, houses and aspects of your Sun and Moon say how it plays out.

The boundaries need care. Near 45° or 90°, a few hours move your Moon from one phase to the next, so on such days the birth time decides ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)). [Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/) explains how to find yours.

In the sky, I don’t read the crescent days on their own. The new and full moons are the markers I use, and only under strict conditions; the days between belong to the month’s rhythm rather than to any one event.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The dark part of the crescent is Earth’s shadow.”** It is the Moon’s night side. Earth’s shadow falls on the Moon only during a lunar eclipse, at full moon; the faint glow you see is earthshine.
- **“The crescent is the new moon.”** The new moon itself can’t be seen. By the time a crescent shows, the Moon has already moved on from it.
- **“A crescent lit on the left is always waning.”** Only from the northern hemisphere. From the southern hemisphere, a waxing crescent is lit on the left.
- **“The Moon grows as it waxes.”** The Moon stays the same size. What grows is the part of its sunlit side turned towards us.
