Castor looks like one star and is six: three pairs of stars bound to one another, 49 light-years away. It carries the name of the mortal Twin, and the letter alpha, though its brother Pollux outshines it.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Castor is α Geminorum. The Yale catalogue gives it magnitude 1.98, the figure for its brightest pair; all six stars together make a single point of about magnitude 1.6 to the naked eye. Through a telescope the two main pairs split apart, and they are slowly opening: about 2″ apart in 1970, about 6″ in 2026. It was first recorded as a double star in 1718. The faint outer pair, Castor C, is two red dwarfs that eclipse each other in less than a day and flare from time to time.

Its zodiac degree is 20°36′ [Cancer](/astrology/cancer/) for 2026; a chart from 2000 gives 20°14′, and one from 1930 19°15′. Around 150 CE it was still in the sign of its constellation, at about 24° Gemini.

Castor lies 10.1° north of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), further north than Pollux. The [Moon](/astrology/moon/), ruler of Cancer, crosses Castor's degree every month and passes several degrees south of the star each time, and so do the planets ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)).

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Babylonian astronomers already saw the two stars as the Great Twins, Meshlamtaea and Lugalirra, names that can both be read as titles of Nergal, king of the underworld. For the Greeks and Romans the twins of Leda, Castor and [Pollux](/astrology/pollux/), were the protectors of sailors. When the blue glow of St Elmo's fire played round a mast in a storm, the Greeks called a single light Helen and a double one Castor and Pollux, after her twin brothers.

Arabic names place Castor at the head of the foremost twin, Ras al Taum al Muqadim, and a seventeenth-century Arabic catalogue calls it the first in the paw, Prima Brachii in its Latin translation. In China it is the second star of the North River.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

Within about a degree of 20°36′ Cancer, a planet or angle in a 2026 chart is conjunct Castor in the tradition's terms; in a 1930 chart the point is 19°15′ ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)). The Sun comes to it on about 12 and 13 July, a [Sun in Cancer](/astrology/sun-in-cancer/) three days before it reaches Pollux. In the sky the twins stand about 4½° apart; in the zodiac their degrees are 2°58′ apart, because longitude measures only along the Sun's path.

For astrology the six stars collapse into one point: a single degree, 20°36′ Cancer, with nothing to say that the faint red pair sits 73″ from the bright ones. The sailors' twins would add their tone to a planet there, for an astrologer who works with stars.
