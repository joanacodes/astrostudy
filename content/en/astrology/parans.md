Most of what astrology says about fixed stars works by zodiac degree: a planet at 0° Virgo today is on Regulus. Parans work by the turning sky instead. They ask which stars stood on the horizon or the meridian at the same moment as a planet did, over the place where you were born.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Once a day, every star and every planet rises in the east, culminates on the meridian, sets in the west and passes beneath the earth at its lowest point. Those four moments match the four angles of a chart: the Ascendant, the [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/), the Descendant and the [Imum Coeli](/astrology/imum-coeli/). When a star reaches one of them at the same moment as a planet reaches one, astrologers who use the method call the star a paran of that planet and let it colour the way the planet is read. Any angle can pair with any angle: a star setting while a planet culminates counts as much as two rising together.

The word is short for paranatellonta, which is older than the method. The Roman poet Manilius gave most of the fifth book of his *Astronomica*, in the 1st century AD, to stars and constellations that rise or set together with particular degrees or decans of the zodiac ([The Egyptian decans](/astrology/egyptian-decans/)), and told it through Andromeda and Perseus, a myth with more future constellations in it than any other. Medieval astrology kept the idea: a manuscript attributed to Alfonso X of Castile pictures Corvus, Cygnus and Draco acting in concert with Gemini. The modern use, a star and a planet on the angles at once, is set out in Bernadette Brady's book of fixed stars, published in 1999.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Because a paran follows the real sky, a star's zodiac degree doesn't decide it. What decides it is where the star actually is, its [right ascension](/astrology/right-ascension/) and its [declination](/astrology/declination/), and the latitude of the birthplace.

Culmination is the simple case. A body crosses the meridian when the sidereal time equals its right ascension, wherever you are, so two bodies culminate together only if they share a right ascension, and then they do so at every latitude. Rising and setting are different: how early a body rises depends on how far north or south it lies and on how far north or south you are. A star and a planet that rise together in Lisbon may not in Oslo. And a star far enough from the equator never rises or sets at all at some latitudes; the Southern Cross never clears the horizon of Europe. Such a star can only make parans by crossing the meridian.

Set beside a [conjunction to a fixed star](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/), the difference shows most with stars far from the Sun's path. The conjunction matches zodiac degrees, which can put a planet "on" a star nowhere near it in the sky; the paran ignores degrees. A planet can be in paran with a star several signs away and miss a star at its own degree. For the same reason, a star at the Midheaven's zodiac degree isn't necessarily culminating: unless it lies on the ecliptic, it reaches the meridian at another time.

[Heliacal rising](/astrology/heliacal-rising/) is the other old way of reading a star by its rising: not against a planet but against the Sun, on the morning a star first reappears before dawn.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Stars aren't part of the readings I prepare, by degree or by paran, so what follows is how astrologers who use them work, not a method of mine ([Fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)).

What they value is that parans are local. Born at the same moment a thousand kilometres further north, someone else would have a different set, because the horizon would cut the sky differently. That is also their cost. Everything rests on the place, and a paran tied to the moment of birth rests on the angles of that moment, which turn a degree in about four minutes ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

If someone brings me a paran, the planet comes first for me: its sign, its house, its aspects. A star's tradition is a footnote to that, however bright the star, and since my notebook never covered stars, I have no figure for how often one holds ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A paran is a conjunction with a star.”** A conjunction matches degrees of the zodiac; a paran matches moments on the angles. The two can disagree completely.
- **“Your parans are the same wherever you were born.”** Rising and setting depend on latitude, so the same moment gives different parans in different places. Only pairs that culminate together stay paired everywhere.
- **“Parans are a modern invention.”** The name goes back to the paranatellonta of Manilius. What is modern is pairing stars with planets on the angles.
- **“A star at your Midheaven's degree is on your Midheaven.”** Only a star on the ecliptic culminates with its zodiac degree. The rest cross the meridian at other times.
