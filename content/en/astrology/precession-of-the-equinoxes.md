Precession is astronomy before it's astrology: a slow wobble of the Earth's axis that takes about 26,000 years to come full circle. Astrologers care about it because it moves, against the stars, the very point the tropical zodiac is built on.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Earth spins on a tilted axis, about 23½° from upright, and that axis doesn't keep pointing the same way. Like a spinning top leaning over, it swings slowly round in a circle. Today its north end points close to [Polaris](/astrology/polaris/); over the cycle it points elsewhere, and Polaris is only the pole star of our era.

As the axis turns, so does the celestial equator, the Earth's equator projected onto the sky ([celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/)). The equinoxes are the two points where that equator crosses the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), the Sun's yearly path, and as the equator swings round, the crossings slide backwards along the ecliptic, against the order of the signs. That slide is the precession of the equinoxes: about 50 seconds of arc a year, a degree in about 72 years, a full circle in about 26,000.

The cause is gravity. The Earth bulges slightly at the equator, and the Sun and Moon pull on the bulge as if to stand the axis upright. A spinning body doesn't straighten when pulled like that. It turns its axis sideways instead, and keeps turning.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

In the second century BCE, Hipparchus compared his star positions with older records and found that all of them had moved the same way, which is how precession was discovered. Ptolemy took up his work in the Almagest and gave the rate as a degree a century, slower than the true one, which modern astronomy puts at about 50.3 seconds of arc a year.

For astrology, the consequence is a gap that keeps growing. The [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/) starts at the March equinox, so it travels with the equinox and keeps step with the seasons. The [sidereal zodiac](/astrology/sidereal-zodiac/) starts from a point among the stars, so it stays put. The two once coincided; today they sit about 24° apart, and the gap widens by a degree every 72 years or so.

Three things follow from it:

- **Signs and constellations no longer line up.** The sign of Aries still begins at the equinox, but the equinox now lies in front of the stars of Pisces ([Constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).
- **Fixed stars creep forward through the tropical signs.** Regulus, the bright star of Leo, stood at 28°50′ Leo in 1930 and stands at 0°11′ Virgo in 2026: in a tropical chart, it has changed sign within a lifetime ([Fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)).
- **The astrological ages come from it.** As the equinox moves from one constellation into the next, some astrologers speak of an age for each, the Age of Aquarius among them. When each age begins is much disputed.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

For the charts I read, precession is already built in. Positions in the tropical zodiac are measured from the equinox of the moment, so a chart for 1950 and a chart for 2026 are each measured against the equinox of their own day, and nothing needs correcting. That's the point of the frame: the signs stay tied to the year, whatever the stars do.

Where precession matters is in comparisons. A Vedic chart of the same birth will show most planets a sign earlier, and precession is the reason; neither chart is wrong. A contact with a fixed star needs the star's position for the year of birth, not a degree copied from an old book. And when someone tells me the stars have moved, so their sign must have changed, the honest answer is yes, the stars have moved, and the tropical signs were never measured from them. ## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Precession puts every horoscope a month out.”** The tropical signs move with the equinox. They're out of step with the constellations, not with the seasons they measure.
- **“Nobody noticed until modern science.”** Hipparchus measured it in the second century BCE, and Ptolemy wrote about it in the Almagest.
- **“The pole star never changes.”** Polaris is the pole star of our era. Over the cycle of about 26,000 years, the axis points elsewhere.
- **“Precession proves the sidereal zodiac right and the tropical one wrong.”** It explains why the two differ. Which one to read in is a choice of tradition, not something precession settles.
