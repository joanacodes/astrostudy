Facies is not a star but a globular cluster, Messier 22, a ball of tens of thousands of stars in Sagittarius that astrologers count among the fixed stars. It lies almost on the Sun's path, near 8°40′ Capricorn in 2026, so the Moon and planets that reach its degree really do pass across it.

## Where it is {#where-it-is}

Facies is Messier 22, also catalogued as NGC 6656 and called the Great Sagittarius Cluster: a globular cluster, a tight ball of very old stars about 10,600 light-years away. Together they shine at about magnitude 5.1, and the cluster covers about half a degree of sky, the width of the full Moon.

A cluster has no entry in the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, so the degree here is worked out from the centre astronomers measure for it: about 8°40′ [Capricorn](/astrology/capricorn/) in 2026 and 8°18′ in 2000, with the cluster spreading about a quarter of a degree either side. It belongs to the constellation Sagittarius and sits in the sign that follows ([constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)).

Its latitude, about 0.7° south of the [ecliptic](/astrology/ecliptic/), is what makes it unusual. [Sabik](/astrology/sabik/), at 7.2°, counts as near the ecliptic; Facies is almost on it ([celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/)). The Sun passes less than a degree north of its centre every year. Mercury, Venus and Mars cross the cluster itself every few years, and the Moon passes in front of it in some seasons, as it did in 2020 and will again in 2028.

## Its tradition {#its-tradition}

Facies is the Latin word for a face, the one in *prima facie*, "at first face". As a name it tells you less about the cluster than the modern ones do.

No old star catalogue I can check lists it. It was first recorded in 1665, by Abraham Ihle, and Charles Messier put it in his catalogue of comet-like objects in 1764, as number 22. Harlow Shapley, who studied it in 1930, counted roughly 70,000 stars in it, and it is one of only four globular clusters in our galaxy known to hold a planetary nebula, the shell of gas a dying star throws off.

The meanings now attached to Facies in astrology books come from compilations I haven't been able to check against their sources, so I don't repeat them here. What the record gives is a face, a number in Messier's list, and a crowd of very old stars ([fixed stars in astrology](/astrology/fixed-stars-in-astrology/)).

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The Sun reaches Facies on about 29 and 30 December, about a week into its stay in Capricorn ([Sun in Capricorn](/astrology/sun-in-capricorn/)). Because the cluster lies on the ecliptic, that is a conjunction in the full sense: the Sun and the cluster share a patch of sky as well as a degree.

Between 1987 and 1990, three slow planets crossed its degree in turn: Neptune in 1987 and 1988 ([Neptune in Capricorn](/astrology/neptune-in-capricorn/)), Saturn in 1989, and Uranus in 1990, which passed less than half a degree from the cluster in June of that year. With a cluster half a degree wide, the one-degree orb is counted from its centre ([conjunctions to fixed stars](/astrology/conjunctions-to-fixed-stars/)).

Facies stays outside my readings, as every star and cluster does; the system I built doesn't place them in a chart ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). Its neighbour in this encyclopaedia, [Aculeus](/astrology/aculeus/), is a cluster of another kind, younger and scattered, in the Scorpion's sting.
