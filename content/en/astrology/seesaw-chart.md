Some charts split down the middle. Their planets fall into two groups that face each other across the wheel, with empty space on either side, like the two seats of a playground seesaw.

## What it looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

[Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) counted the seesaw among his seven chart shapes. In the description Wikipedia gives from its sources, the planets form two groups on opposite sides of the chart, at least 60°, two signs, apart. Other authors set that line elsewhere, and that's why this site gives no count for the shape: there's no single rule to test charts against.

Across the empty stretches, planets of one group can face planets of the other in [opposition](/astrology/opposition/), and even when they don't, the drawing reads as a balance: what sits on one side answers what sits on the other, the way the two ends of an axis answer each other. Each group can be tight or loose, large or small. What makes the shape is the two empty stretches that keep the groups apart.

Close one of those stretches and the chart becomes a [locomotive](/astrology/locomotive-chart/), one long train with a single empty third ahead. Break the groups up into smaller clusters, spread round the wheel, and it moves towards the [splash](/astrology/splash-chart/), with planets nearly everywhere.

## In a birth chart {#in-a-birth-chart}

The count behind this site leaves the seesaw out. Its definitions vary too much between authors: a chart could be a seesaw by one rule and something else by the next, and any figure I gave you would describe my choice of rule rather than your chart. Where there's no agreed definition, I'd rather give you none than a number that only looks precise.

Astrologers who use the shape describe a life that moves between two contrasting sides, often finding it hard to bring them together. The system I built for my readings reads the opposition, the aspect a seesaw is built around, as polarity and integration, and never as conflict in itself, so I read the shape the same way. Two centres of gravity, each with its own business; the empty stretches between them are where you cross from one to the other. Balance here isn't stillness. A seesaw that works keeps moving, and both seats stay occupied. And like any shape, it describes emphasis: the planets in each group, read one by one, decide what the two sides are actually about.

Much of the telling is done by the houses, which only the time of birth can supply. Two groups across the horizon, around the 1st and 7th houses, pair self and others; across the meridian, around the 4th and 10th, home and public life. The Moon can sit in one group or in a gap, and its place changes by about a sign every two and a half days. Which two sides of life the seesaw joins is a question for [the birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/), and I wait for its answer.

## What helps {#what-helps}

- **Name the two sides.** Say plainly what each group is about, by planet and by house. A vague swing becomes a choice you can schedule.
- **Use the crossing points.** Aspects between the groups, oppositions especially, are the bridges. Work on them and the two sides start to talk.
- **Keep both seats filled.** Neglecting one side for long tends to make the other heavier. Visiting both is the balance.
- **Expect to move.** A life with two centres goes back and forth. That's the shape working, not failing.
- **Look at the gaps as well.** The empty stretches belong to neither group; their signs and houses can show what all the back and forth leaves out.

A seesaw only works with weight on both ends, and a seesaw chart reads best when neither group is treated as the real one and the other as a distraction.
