Synastry lays two charts side by side. The composite does something stranger: it melts them into one. Every planet is moved to the point halfway between where it stood in each birth chart, and the result is a chart that belongs to neither person, only to the relationship.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

A composite chart is built from [midpoints](/astrology/midpoints/). Take one person's Sun at 10° Aries and the other's at 20° Gemini: halfway between them, by the shorter way round, is 15° Taurus, and that's the composite Sun. Do the same for the Moon, for each planet and point, and you have a chart no sky ever showed, read as the character and purpose of the relationship itself. Robert Hand's *Planets in Composite* (1975) is a book-length guide to reading one.

Because it describes the relationship and not either person, it can be drawn for any close bond: a couple, friends, business partners, family. It's read together with the two birth charts and the [synastry](/astrology/synastry/), never instead of them.

I don't draw composite charts in my readings. I describe the technique here because it's widely used, and because knowing how it works helps you weigh what you read about one.

## How it is done {#how-it-is-done}

Every pair of positions has two midpoints, exactly opposite each other, and the convention is to take the nearer one, on the shorter arc. That works well until the two positions are almost opposite each other: then a small difference flips the midpoint from one side of the circle to the other.

Midpoints are worked out one planet at a time, so they don't have to respect the geometry of a real sky. In a birth chart, Mercury is never more than about 28° from the Sun and Venus never more than about 47°. In composites, they can be anywhere. Across 2,000,000 pairs of birth dates drawn at random from 1930 to 2009, composite Mercury was more than 28° from the composite Sun in about 1 pair in 18, and composite Venus more than 47° away in about 1 pair in 10; Mercury landed opposite the Sun in about 1 pair in 26, Venus in about 1 pair in 22.

The houses need both birth times, and there is more than one way to draw them. Some astrologers take the midpoints of the two charts' house cusps; others find the composite Midheaven, the midpoint of the two Midheavens, and build the houses from it for the place where the pair lives. The two methods can put a planet in different houses. Both are in use, and neither is the composite's only correct form ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

## What it shows {#what-it-shows}

Astrologers read a composite as the relationship's own chart: what it's like, what it seems to be for, where its energy goes. The composite Sun's sign and house describe what the relationship organises itself around; the composite Moon, what it needs to feel secure; composite Venus, what the two of you value together. A pair with a [composite Sun in Cancer](/astrology/composite-sun-in-cancer/) might build their bond around shelter and belonging; with the composite Sun in the 1st house ([composite Sun in the 1st house](/astrology/composite-sun-in-1st-house/)), the relationship tends to be visible, something others notice before they notice either person.

For fast planets, any composite sign is common: about 1 pair in 12 has the composite Sun in a given sign. For slow planets, two people of the same age simply get their generation's sign, and an age gap moves it to the midpoint between two generations.

## Its limits {#its-limits}

A composite doesn't show how long a relationship will last, whether it will succeed, or whether two people should marry; it never replaces the synastry; and it describes the shared dynamic, not either person's psychology. It's also a symbol, not an object: there is no third person in the room, only two people and what they make together.

Its quirks are part of its limits. A Mercury opposite the Sun, or a Venus far from it, is an artefact of midpoints, not a message, and the houses depend on the method chosen. Some astrologers prefer [the Davison chart](/astrology/davison-chart/) for exactly that reason: it's cast for a real moment, so its sky is a possible one. Neither method is more accurate in itself. If you meet a composite, read it as one perspective on a relationship, next to the two people who are actually in it.
