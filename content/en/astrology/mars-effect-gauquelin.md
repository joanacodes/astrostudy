Michel Gauquelin said he had found something in the statistics of birth charts: not in the signs or the aspects, but in where some planets stood at the moment of birth. His results were argued over for decades, by scientists and by sceptics among themselves. The Mars effect is the best known of them.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Gauquelin reported that sports champions were born more often just after [Mars](/astrology/mars/) had risen or culminated. The independent tests that followed, in the United States and in France, did not find it, and the explanations published since point to how the champions were chosen and how their birth times were reported, rather than to Mars. That's where the evidence stands. It's also a story worth knowing for how fiercely it was fought, on both sides.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

**What Gauquelin claimed.** In 1955 Michel Gauquelin, a French psychologist, stated that he had found no evidence for the zodiac signs or for planetary aspects, but had found positive correlations between the daily positions of some planets and success in professions that astrology traditionally links with those planets. In *L'influence des astres* he suggested that a statistically significant number of sports champions were born just after Mars rose or culminated; his first chart of it used the birth charts of 570 eminent athletes. In Placidus, the house system I use, a planet that has just risen sits in the 12th house and one that has just culminated in the 9th: cadent houses, in astrological terms, rather than the angular 1st and 10th.

**The American test.** A study of American athletes by Paul Kurtz, Marvin Zelen and George Abell, published in 1979, came out negative. Gauquelin replied that it favoured mediocre athletes, ignored his criteria of eminence, and included basketball players and people born after 1950.

**The French test.** The CFEPP, a French committee for the study of paranormal phenomena, issued a preliminary report in 1990 on its test of 1,066 French sports champions. Its results, in 1994, showed no Mars effect, and it concluded that the effect came from bias in Gauquelin's selection of data, pointing to changes he had suggested to its list of athletes. The report came out as a book in 1996. Suitbert Ertel criticised the CFEPP's report for including too many mediocre athletes and for using too high a chance level.

**The explanations.** Later research explains the effect by selection bias: champions born with Mars in a key sector were kept in the sample, and those who weren't were left out. Geoffrey Dean, whose study of [Time twins: Dean and Kelly](/astrology/time-twins-dean-and-kelly/) has its own page, suggested something else: that a small number of parents, at a time when belief in astrology was more common, reported birth times that suited their beliefs. Gauquelin had not found the effect in more recent groups, where a nurse or doctor recorded the birth; he put that down to the growing number of “artificial” birth hours, as more babies were delivered by surgery.

**The row among sceptics.** In late 1975 the astronomer Dennis Rawlins began investigating the claim with Kurtz, Abell and Zelen, all of them later members of CSICOP, the American sceptics' committee now called the Committee for Skeptical Inquiry. Rawlins, a founding member, resigned in early 1980, claiming that colleagues had used incorrect statistics, faulty science and outright falsification in trying to debunk Gauquelin. In “sTARBABY”, published in *Fate* in 1981, he wrote that he was still sceptical of the occult beliefs CSICOP had been set up to debunk, but had changed his mind about the integrity of some of those who opposed them for a living.

A few years before Gauquelin's book, Jung had tried a statistical test of his own, on the horoscopes of married couples: that story is [Jung’s marriage experiment](/astrology/jungs-marriage-experiment/).

## What I found {#what-i-found}

Mars was in my notebook like every other placement. I checked it in its sign and its house, with a tick or a cross, in my own chart and then, with their consent, in the charts of family, friends and strangers. It was not one of the three that held far more often: those were Saturn, Lilith and Chiron, at 75 to 90%. Overall, about a third of what I checked held, 30 to 40%, the Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury among them.

It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general. What it changes is how I read: when something rests on a placement that held less often in my tests, I say so. The method is in [my testing notes](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).
