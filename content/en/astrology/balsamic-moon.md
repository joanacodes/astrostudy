The balsamic moon is the end of the month: a last, fine crescent low in the east before dawn, then a day or two when the Moon can’t be seen at all. Astrologers who follow Dane Rudhyar’s eight phases give this stretch a name of its own.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

In the sky, the waning crescent runs from the [last quarter moon](/astrology/last-quarter-moon/) to the new moon: the Moon less than 90° behind the Sun, less than half lit, and shrinking. In Rudhyar’s eight phases, from *The Lunation Cycle* (1967), the last 45° of that stretch, from 45° behind the Sun to the exact new moon, is the balsamic phase: about three and three-quarter days.

Balsamic is an old word for what soothes and restores, like balsam. Where astrologers first took it from isn’t clear. It is the name the phase carries in Rudhyar’s book, and the one in common use among astrologers who count eight phases.

Seen from the northern hemisphere, the last crescent is lit on the left, curved like a C, and you find it in the east before sunrise. Each morning it is thinner and rises closer to the Sun, until it is lost in the dawn. From the southern hemisphere it is lit on the right.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The Moon is now closing on the Sun from behind, about 12° a day on average. Its sign is usually the Sun’s own or the one before it: with the Sun in Aries, a balsamic Moon is usually in Pisces or in Aries. Inside the phase sits the waning semisextile, the Moon 30° behind the Sun, which the system I built for my readings reads as adjustment between neighbours: awareness that comes in small steps.

The phase ends at the [new moon](/astrology/new-moon/), the moment the Moon reaches the Sun and the next cycle begins. For a day or so either side of it the Moon is too close to the Sun to be seen, the old dark of the moon, and that darkness belongs half to the balsamic phase and half to the new.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Born in the balsamic phase, your Moon was just behind your Sun. I read the balsamic birth as someone attuned to endings: finishing what others leave half-done, closing chapters cleanly, making room before something new begins. It isn’t a life of loss, and nothing in a chart is a sentence. It describes a rhythm that knows how to let go, and often how to prepare the ground. [Your birth lunar phase](/astrology/your-birth-lunar-phase/) explains how the eight phases follow one another and how to find yours.

Some astrologers also look at the new moon just before a birth, the [prenatal lunation](/astrology/prenatal-lunation/), a point Rudhyar’s book also gives weight to. For a balsamic birth, that new moon fell nearly a month earlier, and the next comes within days.

The edge of the phase is where the birth time counts most. Close to the exact new moon, a few hours decide whether your Moon was still behind the Sun or had already passed it ([the Moon, the one to check](/blog/unknown-birth-time/#the-moon)).

In the sky, some people like to use the dark days before a new moon to rest and clear things away. That is a rhythm you can choose, not one the sky imposes, and I keep the new moon itself as the marker.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“A balsamic birth means a life of endings and loss.”** It describes a talent for closing well, not a run of losses. Every life has its endings; this phase describes a way of meeting them.
- **“The balsamic moon is the new moon.”** The new moon is the moment the balsamic phase ends. The balsamic days come before it.
- **“Nothing should be started in the balsamic phase.”** Calendars that say so offer a habit, not a law of the sky. What you begin stands on its own merits.
- **“Balsamic is an astronomers’ term.”** Astronomy says waning crescent. Balsamic is an astrologers’ name, and where it first came from isn’t known.
