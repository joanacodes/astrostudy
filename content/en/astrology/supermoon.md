Several times a year a headline promises a supermoon: a full moon bigger and brighter than usual. The Moon really is a little closer then, and the word for it comes, unusually, from an astrologer.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Moon’s orbit is an ellipse, so its distance from Earth changes through the month: by NASA’s figures, about 363,300 km on average at perigee, its nearest point, and about 405,500 km at apogee, its farthest. A supermoon is a full moon, or a new moon, that happens with the Moon near perigee. Astronomers call it a perigee syzygy, syzygy being the lining up of Sun, Earth and Moon that comes at every full and new moon, or simply a full moon at perigee.

The word itself was coined by the astrologer Richard Nolle, in a 1979 article for Dell’s *Horoscope* magazine, as he has since described it. His definition took in any new or full moon at or near the Moon’s closest approach in that orbit, with a margin he set himself. Other writers use other cut-offs, so two lists of supermoons for the same year often disagree. New moons qualify too, but since nobody can see a new moon, the name is mostly used for full ones.

The opposite, a full or new moon near apogee, is the [micromoon](/astrology/micromoon/).

## How it works {#how-it-works}

Full moons and perigees run on two different clocks. The Moon goes from full to full in about 29½ days, but from perigee to perigee in about 27½. The two drift in and out of step, so the full moon nearest perigee comes back about every 14 lunations, a little more than a year later, and the full moons either side of it are close to perigee as well. That’s why several full moons in a row can carry the name, and why the closest one of each run falls about a month and a half later in the calendar each time.

How much difference does it make? At its nearest, a full moon looks up to about a seventh wider than one at the Moon’s farthest point, and sends us up to nearly a third more light, by NASA’s comparison. Side by side, in photographs, that’s plain. Looking up on a single evening, with nothing to compare, it’s hard to see. A full moon low over the horizon looks huge whatever its distance, but that is the eye’s doing, the moon illusion, not the orbit’s. Tides do run a little higher at a full or new moon near perigee than at others, because the Moon’s pull is a little stronger when it is closer.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

The word came from an astrologer, but it adds nothing to how I read a lunation. I read a supermoon as the full or new moon it is: by its sign, with the Sun in the same sign at a new moon and opposite at a full one, and by whether it falls within a degree of a point in your chart that something slower is already working on. The Moon’s distance isn’t part of that rule. It changes how a full moon looks, not what it means.

Nor do I read a birth at a supermoon differently from any birth at a full or new moon. Your [Moon](/astrology/moon/) is read by its sign, its house and its aspects, and its distance from Earth on the day isn’t among them. If you were born at a full or new moon, the [full moon](/astrology/full-moon/) and [new moon](/astrology/new-moon/) pages say what that phase can describe, and the house needs your birth time ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)).

As weather, then, a supermoon is a bright full moon worth looking at, nothing more ominous. That’s not a small thing: it’s a good night to go outside.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Supermoon is an astronomers’ term.”** It’s an astrologer’s, from 1979. Astronomers say perigee syzygy or full moon at perigee, and there’s no official definition of the popular word.
- **“You’ll see a huge Moon.”** It’s a little larger and brighter than usual, which is hard to notice on one evening. The giant Moon on the horizon is the moon illusion, which happens at any full moon.
- **“The supermoon is the same time every year.”** The nearest full moon comes round about every 14 lunations, so each year’s closest one falls about a month and a half later in the calendar than the last.
- **“A supermoon has a stronger astrological effect.”** In my reading, a lunation counts by its degree and the natal points it meets. The Moon’s distance makes it brighter, not more meaningful.
