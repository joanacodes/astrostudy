Jim Lewis took the angles of a birth chart, which change from one town to the next, and drew them across the whole world. His maps, sold under the name Astro\*Carto\*Graphy, made locational astrology popular in the 1970s and 1980s.

## Who {#who}

He was born James Lewis Slayden in Yonkers, New York, on 5 June 1941, and lived in San Francisco for most of his life. In 1979 he published the first edition of his annual *Sourcebook of Mundane Maps*, and in the late 1970s he worked with Michael Erlewine, the author of software for local space astrology, on the first Astro\*Carto\*Graphy program. In 1980 he applied for a patent on his hand-drawn method, which laid a transparent overlay on a map; it was granted in 1981 and expired in 2000. He service-marked the name as well.

His books were *The Astro\*Carto\*Graphy Book of Maps: The Astrology of Relocation*, written with Gail Guttman and published by Llewellyn in 1981, and *The Psychology of Astro\*Carto\*Graphy*, written with Kenneth Irving, who published it after Lewis's death, in 1997. He also contributed an essay, “And the future will be nothing less than the flowering of our inwardness”, to A. T. Mann's collection *The Future of Astrology*.

He lectured around the world, trained students in seminars and later set a certification exam: those who passed received a certificate as a professional Astro\*Carto\*Grapher. The award named after [Marc Edmund Jones](/astrology/marc-edmund-jones/) came in 1978, and in 1992 the United Astrology Congress, meeting in Washington, D.C., gave him its Regulus Award for Discovery, Innovation and Research. He died in San Francisco on 21 February 1995, at 53, and his will provided for an Astro\*Carto\*Graphy Trust.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He turned the angles into lines. A chart's angles belong to its place: move the birthplace and the Ascendant and Midheaven move with it ([Relocation charts](/astrology/relocation-charts/)); Lewis drew, for each planet, the line of all the places where it stood on one of the four angles at that minute. His maps were a way of seeing at once where each planet would be what astrologers call angular ([Angular planets](/astrology/angular-planets/)).

He built on a long line of map astrology. Tying the sky to places goes back to late antiquity; before him, the British astrologer Sepharial and the Canadian Edward Johndro had worked on it, and a method drawn from findings by Don Neroman, Gustav Schwickert, Cyril Fagan, Roy Charles Firebrace and Donald Bradley was developed from about 1930 to the 1960s. Other astrologers of his own day built similar techniques, among them the [local space astrology](/astrology/local-space-astrology/) whose software Erlewine wrote. What Lewis added was the finished map, its name and a school of practitioners to read it ([Astrocartography](/astrology/astrocartography/)).

He made a technique into a trade. A patent, a service mark, a certificate and, after his death, a trust and a trademark all followed the maps. The trademark was cancelled in 2011, and when newspapers write about the technique today, as *The New York Times* did in 2020, they call it astrocartography.

His maps also carry the oldest problem in my work. Every line hangs on the minute of birth: an hour's error slides the whole map sideways, and a map drawn from a guessed time is a guess drawn neatly ([What changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). I don't draw these maps in readings, and I wouldn't point anyone to a city because of one.

The same congress gave its award for theory and understanding to [Demetra George](/astrology/demetra-george/); his was for discovery. His maps also had a successor among the stars: [Bernadette Brady](/astrology/bernadette-brady/) later built her work on the fixed stars round the [paran](/astrology/parans/), a star and a planet on the angles at the same moment, close kin to the crossing points of the lines on his maps.
