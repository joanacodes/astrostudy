Masha’allah's name is an Arabic phrase of acceptance, a believer's way of taking whatever God ordains, good fortune or ill. He carried it through a long career among the first astrologers of Abbasid Baghdad, and through the Latin translations of his books into the classrooms of medieval Europe.

## Who {#who}

He was a Persian Jewish astrologer, astronomer and mathematician who lived from about 740 to 815. He came originally from Khorasan and lived in Basra, which gave him the name al-Basri; his own name is probably an Arabic rendering of the Hebrew Shiluh. If his birth date is right, he was barely in his twenties in 762, when the caliph al-Mansur had a group of astrologers led by Naubakht choose the moment to found Baghdad, and he was among them. He is also said to have worked on the building of an observatory. In the tenth century the bibliographer Ibn al-Nadim remembered him as virtuous and, in his day, a leader in “the science of judgments of the stars”.

Twenty-one works are attributed to him, and the titles Ibn al-Nadim recorded cover a working astrologer's whole day: a *Big Book of Births* in fourteen volumes, books on questions and on the beginnings of actions, on the yearly revolutions of a nativity, on conjunctions, religions and sects, on rains and winds, on the sick, on prices, on the astrolabe. Only *On Prices* survives in its original Arabic, and it is the oldest scientific work known in that language. The rest lives mostly in Latin; in the twelfth century Abraham ibn Ezra put two of his treatises into Hebrew, and eleven have been translated from the Latin into English in modern times.

His most famous reader may be one he never had. Most of Chaucer's *Treatise on the Astrolabe* (1391) follows a Latin text long credited to Masha’allah, as John Selden noticed in 1613 and Walter Skeat confirmed. In 1981, however, Paul Kunitzsch argued that the treatise was really by Ibn al-Saffar.

## What they changed {#what-they-changed}

He wrote for the questions clients brought and the ventures they wanted to start. His books on questions and on the beginnings of actions belong to [horary astrology](/astrology/horary-astrology/) and [electional astrology](/astrology/electional-astrology/), and his *On Reception*, on how one planet receives another, is read in English today. Neither branch is part of my work: I read a birth chart for tendencies, not a moment for an answer or a date ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). His book on the yearly revolutions of a nativity is closer to me. That annual chart, which astrologers now call the [solar return](/astrology/solar-return/), is one I use, as weather for a year.

He read history in the slow planets. His astrological history of the world was timed by the conjunctions of Jupiter and Saturn, and it survives only in quotations by a Christian astrologer, Ibn Hibinta ([Jupiter–Saturn great conjunctions](/astrology/jupiter-saturn-great-conjunctions/)). I read people, not eras, and leave that kind of history to historians.

He gave the signs a place in the cosmos. Masha’allah pictured the universe as ten orbs rather than Aristotle's eight. The ninth, without stars, carried the twelve signs and turned with the equinoxes; the eighth carried the fixed stars and drifted a degree a century, so that the constellations slid past the signs while the seasons stayed with them. In his picture, the Sun entering a sign of the ninth orb was what began a season. It is a medieval answer to a question people still ask me: why a [tropical zodiac](/astrology/tropical-zodiac/) sign and the constellation of the same name no longer coincide ([Constellations versus signs](/astrology/constellations-versus-signs/)). The real drift is faster than his, about a degree in 72 years, but the principle is the one I work with.

When the Latin West took up horoscopic astrology again in the twelfth century, more than seven centuries after [Firmicus Maternus](/astrology/firmicus-maternus/), it read Masha’allah in translation: John of Seville, for one, put his letter on eclipses and conjunctions into Latin. The next generation's great teacher was [Abu Ma’shar](/astrology/abu-mashar/); the world they shared is [Persian and Arabic astrology](/astrology/persian-and-arabic-astrology/).
