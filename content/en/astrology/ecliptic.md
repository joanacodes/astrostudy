If you could see the stars behind the Sun, day after day, you'd watch it work its way round them in a year and come back to where it started. That circle is the ecliptic, and every astrological chart is drawn on it.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

The Earth goes round the Sun once a year, so from here the Sun seems to move against the background stars, about a degree a day, until it returns to its starting point twelve months later. Its path is the ecliptic. Seen another way, the ecliptic is the plane of the Earth's orbit, extended until it meets the sky.

The planets' orbits lie close to that plane, so they all travel near the ecliptic too, a little north or a little south of it. The Moon's path is tilted about five degrees to it and crosses it at two points, the lunar nodes: the [North Node](/astrology/north-node/), where the Moon heads north, and the South Node, where it heads south. Eclipses happen only when a new or full moon falls near those crossings, with the Sun, the Moon and the Earth lined up on the ecliptic, and that's where the name comes from: the line of eclipses ([Why eclipses follow the nodes](/astrology/why-eclipses-follow-the-nodes/)).

The ecliptic isn't the sky's equator. The celestial equator is the Earth's own equator projected outwards ([celestial equator](/astrology/celestial-equator/)), and because the Earth's axis is tilted, the two circles sit at about 23.4° to each other. They cross at two points, the equinoxes, and the ecliptic's furthest points north and south of the equator are the solstices.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The [zodiac](/astrology/zodiac/) is laid along the ecliptic, and a position in a chart is a distance along it. Counted from 0° Aries, that distance is a body's [zodiacal longitude](/astrology/zodiacal-longitude/): 226°53′, for instance, is 16°53′ Scorpio. How far a body sits north or south of the ecliptic is its [celestial latitude](/astrology/celestial-latitude/), and a chart wheel leaves it out. A conjunction on paper can therefore be two bodies a few degrees apart in the sky, one above the line and one below it.

The ecliptic also gives a chart its angles. The Earth's daily turn carries the whole ecliptic past the horizon once a day. The degree of it rising in the east at your birth is your [Ascendant](/astrology/ascendant/); the degree crossing the meridian is your [Midheaven](/astrology/midheaven/), which is why the Midheaven is rarely the point straight overhead. And because the ecliptic is tilted, it rises steeply at some hours and at a slant at others, which is why some rising signs pass in well under two hours and others take much longer.

The ecliptic's own reference points aren't quite fixed either. The Earth's axis slowly wobbles, and the equinoxes, where the ecliptic meets the celestial equator, slide back along it by about a degree every 72 years ([precession of the equinoxes](/astrology/precession-of-the-equinoxes/)). The tropical zodiac moves with them, so its signs stay tied to the seasons.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

In a reading, the ecliptic is the ground under everything else. The wheel of a [birth chart](/astrology/birth-chart/) is the ecliptic drawn flat, with the zodiac around its rim and each body set at its longitude. When I read a planet in a sign, I'm reading its place along that line; when I read an aspect, I'm reading the angle between two places on it.

Two things follow that I keep in mind. The angles and the houses come from where the ecliptic met your horizon and meridian, so they need the time and the place of birth ([what changes without a birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/)). And a chart flattens the sky. It shows how far along the ecliptic each body was, and leaves out how far north or south it stood, which has techniques of its own, measured from the celestial equator: [declination](/astrology/declination/) and [out-of-bounds planets](/astrology/out-of-bounds-planets/). The longitude is what I read; the rest belongs to those pages.

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“The ecliptic is the sky's equator.”** That's the celestial equator. The ecliptic is the Sun's yearly path, tilted about 23.4° to it.
- **“The planets sit exactly on the zodiac line.”** They wander a little north and south of it, the Moon by up to about five degrees. A chart records their longitude and sets the rest aside.
- **“The Midheaven is the point straight overhead.”** It's where the ecliptic crosses the meridian, which is rarely the point overhead.
- **“There's an eclipse at every new and full moon.”** Only when the lunation falls near one of the nodes, which happens roughly twice a year, in the [eclipse seasons](/astrology/eclipse-seasons/).
