Asked as a yes-or-no question, this one has a plain answer. The more interesting part is the reason, because the philosophers who made astrology their example of what science is not never agreed on what exactly it lacks. Here's what they argued, what scientists signed and tested, and where my own notebook stands.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

No. Judged by what science asks of a theory, astrology isn't a science, and philosophers of science have used it again and again as their example of a pseudoscience: something that borrows the look of science without meeting its standards. It has also been put to controlled tests. The best known, [Carlson's double-blind test (1985)](/astrology/carlsons-double-blind-test-1985/), found birth charts no better than chance, and [Does astrology work?](/astrology/does-astrology-work/) gathers the other tests.

I won't soften that, and I won't make fun of anyone for asking, or for finding that their chart rang true. It's a fair question, and the people who answered it disagreed with each other in ways worth knowing.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

Most of the research here is argument: philosophers trying to say where science ends, with astrology as their test case. Then came the scientists.

**Karl Popper: it can't be falsified.** Popper proposed that what makes a theory scientific is that it can be falsified, that some possible result would prove it wrong. By that criterion astrology is a pseudoscience, and it was his most frequent example. He called it "pseudo-empirical": it "appeals to observation and experiment", but "nevertheless does not come up to scientific standards".

**Thomas Kuhn: it failed, and couldn't learn from failing.** Kuhn didn't think lack of falsifiability was the problem. Astrologers had made predictions that "categorically failed", and failing doesn't make a field unscientific. What does is what comes next. An astronomer whose prediction fails has a puzzle to work on; an astrologer could only explain the failure away, never use it to revise the tradition. As Kuhn put it, astrologers "had no puzzles to solve and therefore no science to practise".

**Paul Thagard: it stood still.** In "Why Astrology is a Pseudoscience" (1978), Thagard looked at the community as well as the theory. A field is pseudoscientific, for him, when it progresses less than its alternatives and its practitioners make little effort to compare it with them, staying "selective in considering confirmations and disconfirmations". Astrology had changed little in nearly 2,000 years, while psychology now offered other explanations of behaviour. He added a caution: astrology "should be judged as not pseudoscientific in classical or Renaissance times". The label describes what it is now, not what it always was.

**The scientists of 1975.** That year, 186 astronomers, physicists and other scientists signed "Objections to Astrology", published in The Humanist. Astrology's tenets, they said, had no scientific foundation, and there was no mechanism by which the planets could act on us. One astronomer, Carl Sagan, declined to sign. He didn't think astrology valid, but he found the tone authoritarian, and a missing mechanism, though certainly relevant, unconvincing on its own.

**The test.** Shawn Carlson's experiment, published in Nature in 1985, was agreed in advance with astrologers, who helped set the claim to be tested: 28 astrologers tried to match more than 100 birth charts to personality profiles, without knowing whose was whose, and did no better than chance. [Is astrology real?](/astrology/is-astrology-real/) and [Did my sign change?](/astrology/did-my-sign-change/) take up two questions that often come next.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

I came to astrology to mock it, so before I read for anyone, I tested it: my own chart first, like an invoice, every placement with a tick or a cross, then family, friends and strangers, with their consent. Overall, about a third of what I checked held, **30 to 40%**. **Saturn, Lilith and Chiron held 75 to 90% of the time.**

It's a notebook, not a study, and it proves nothing about astrology in general. I don't claim that my count is a scientific study: it's a hand count in a notebook, not audited. Nor do I claim to know how astrology works. Nobody knows exactly how it works, me included, and saying so is a promise not to pretend.

That's why my readings are offered for reflection and entertainment, and why, when something in a reading rests on a placement that held less often in my tests, I tell you, so you can weigh it for yourself.
