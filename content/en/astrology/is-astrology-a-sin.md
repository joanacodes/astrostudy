People ask this quietly, often after years of being curious about their chart and uneasy about it at the same time. It deserves a straight answer, and part of that answer is that it isn't mine to give.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Whether astrology is a sin depends on your faith, and on what you mean by astrology. I'm not a theologian, and I won't tell you what your tradition allows. A priest, minister, imam or rabbi you trust can, and so can your tradition's own texts.

What I can tell you is what the objections have usually been, and what I do and don't do. Religious authorities have mostly objected to divination, the claim to know the future or hidden things, and to fatalism, the idea that the stars decide a life. I don't predict the future, I claim no gift, and I lift no curses ([How I read](/how-i-read/)). That describes my practice. It settles nothing about yours.

## Why it matters {#why-it-matters}

The objections are old, serious and specific, and it helps to know them:

- **Divination.** The Catechism of the Catholic Church counts consulting horoscopes and astrology among the forms of divination to be rejected: it sees in them a wish for power over time and history, contrary to the honour owed to God alone.
- **Fate against free will.** Augustine, in the fourth and fifth centuries, held that astrology's determinism conflicted with free will and responsibility, and pointed to twins conceived at the same moment who lived different lives. The modern research on people born minutes apart has its own page ([Time twins: Dean and Kelly](/astrology/time-twins-dean-and-kelly/)).
- **Trust placed in the stars.** Martin Luther denounced astrology in his *Table Talk*: what is done by God, he said, ought not to be ascribed to the stars.
- **Prediction, above all.** In the seventh century, Isidore of Seville split astrology in two: describing the movements of the heavens was knowledge; making predictions from them was theological error. In medieval Islam, astronomers such as al-Biruni and Avicenna refuted much of predictive astrology, on religious as well as scientific grounds, and the theologian Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya argued against it as close to divination.

Traditions haven't spoken with one voice. Thomas Aquinas, following Aristotle, allowed that the stars rule the body while holding that God rules the soul ([Medieval European astrology](/astrology/medieval-european-astrology/)), and William Lilly called his textbook of 1647 *Christian Astrology* ([William Lilly’s Christian Astrology (1647)](/astrology/william-lillys-christian-astrology-1647/)). Even Ptolemy argued that nothing in astrology was irrevocably ordained ([Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos](/astrology/ptolemys-tetrabiblos/)).

What the objections share is a concern about what a reading claims: to foretell, to decide, to stand where trust in God, or in your own conscience, should stand. Those are fair questions to put to any astrologer, me included.

## What to do instead {#what-to-do-instead}

- **Ask within your faith.** Take the question to someone you trust in your own tradition. They know your community, your texts and you.
- **Read your tradition's own sources,** rather than an astrologer's summary of them, mine included.
- **If astrology stays in your life, keep it in its place.** Use it for reflection, never for decisions: what you do should rest on what you feel and know, not on what a chart was said to show.
- **If someone has frightened you with religion and astrology together,** with talk of curses, spirits or punishment, and a price for removing them, that's a scam, whatever words it uses ([Curse removal scams](/astrology/curse-removal-scams/)).
- **If guilt or fear about it is weighing on you,** that deserves care: from your faith community, and if it helps, from someone trained to listen ([Astrology and mental health](/astrology/astrology-and-mental-health/)).

The same question is often asked about tarot, and the two practices are different enough that the answer may differ too ([Astrology and tarot](/astrology/astrology-and-tarot/)).
