In the 1920s, long before [the traditional revival](/astrology/traditional-revival/) went back to the oldest books, a surveyor in Hamburg went the other way: out beyond Neptune, to points no telescope had found, and into the arithmetic between the planets. His circle became the Hamburg School, and in America, Uranian astrology.

## What it was {#what-it-was}

[Alfred Witte](/astrology/alfred-witte/), born in Hamburg in 1878, was a surveyor, an astrologer and an amateur astronomer. His first astrological writing dates from 1913, and between 1919 and 1925 he tried out a range of techniques: the houses, formulas that add and subtract planetary positions in the manner of the Arabic parts, and for a while a new scheme of rulerships. He checked his assumptions against what was happening around him rather than against old authorities, and he wanted astrology treated as a science.

On 31 October 1925 an association was founded in Hamburg around his teaching, the Astrologenverein “Hamburger Schule”, with Friedrich Sieggrün and Ludwig Rudolph among his early collaborators. While astronomers were still searching for a planet beyond Neptune, Witte proposed four points out there, which he named [Cupido](/astrology/cupido-uranian-point/), Hades, Zeus and Kronos; in 1927 Sieggrün proposed four more, [Apollon](/astrology/apollon-uranian-point/), Admetos, Vulkanus and Poseidon. The claim brought Witte widespread ridicule in his later years. Astronomers have since found thousands of objects beyond Neptune, but none of these eight, and some members of the school now describe them as the centres of mass of clusters of such objects rather than as planets.

The rest was arithmetic. Witte revived the use of [midpoints](/astrology/midpoints/), the degree halfway between two planets, and Rudolph printed and published the school's rules as the *Regelwerk für Planetenbilder*, the “Rulebook for Planetary Pictures”, in 1928, with new editions in 1932 and 1935. To read its combinations at a glance, the school developed a dial of 90° ([the Uranian 90° dial](/astrology/uranian-90-dial/)).

In the 1930s the method crossed the Atlantic. Richard Svehla, who owned the Phoenix Bookshop in Cleveland, lectured on it at the All-American Astrological Convention in Chicago in 1936, coined the name “Uranian astrology” for its American branch, and published his translation, *Rules for Planetary Pictures*, in 1939.

At home, the school was in danger. The Nazi state banned the *Regelwerk* on 2 October 1936, and copies were later burned, though Rudolph kept some safe. After Hitler's deputy Rudolf Hess flew to Scotland on 10 May 1941, hundreds of astrologers, faith healers and occultists were arrested around 9 June, and Rudolph was interned. Witte, watched by the Gestapo but not interned, was found dead in Hamburg on 4 August 1941; Carl Otto Fleischhauer's 2003 study of him records that he took his own life.

His students brought the work back. The school was refounded in Hamburg on 27 December 1947, and the fourth edition of the *Regelwerk*, edited by Rudolph and Hermann Lefeldt, was the first to include Sieggrün's points. Then it divided: Lefeldt combined Witte's hypotheses with the houses, while others worked with midpoints alone, setting aside the twelve houses and the rulerships, and in time gathered under Ruth Brummund as *Uranische Astrologie*.

## What it gave astrology {#what-it-gave-astrology}

Midpoints as a working method. Witte revived the midpoint rather than inventing it, but he built a system on it, and [Reinhold Ebertin](/astrology/reinhold-ebertin/) drew the foundations of his own handbook largely from Witte's rulebook. Ebertin wrote that it “appeared to be revolutionary and provoked an unusual and mostly adverse criticism”, and that he had used it extensively in his own research.

A family of hard aspects. Witte, and Ebertin after him, found the aspects that divide the circle by eight the most potent in a natal chart: the conjunction, the [semi-square](/astrology/semi-square/) of 45°, the square, the [sesquiquadrate](/astrology/sesquiquadrate/) of 135° and the opposition, all of which the 90° dial shows at a glance. I read the semi-square and the sesquiquadrate too, as minor aspects kept to a degree or two, and both as tension.

Midpoints themselves aren't among the techniques my readings use: I stay with the planets, in their signs, houses and aspects ([how I read](/how-i-read/)). The same arithmetic does sit under one technique I describe on this site, the [composite chart](/astrology/composite-chart/), which puts each planet of a relationship at the midpoint of the two people's positions. And what I recognise in Witte is his habit of checking rules against events instead of quoting authorities, which is how I tested my own chart, a tick or a cross beside each placement ([the invoice method, and what held overall](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/)).

Ebertin kept the midpoints and the dial, dropped the hypothetical points, and gave his version a name of its own: [cosmobiology](/astrology/cosmobiology/).
