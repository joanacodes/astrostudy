A line in a reading can land so close that it stops you. This page is about why that moment, on its own, can't tell you whether the line was true of you, or whether you made it true by finding yourself in it.

## The short answer {#the-short-answer}

Subjective validation is real, and it reaches every reading, mine included. A statement that touches something personal feels correct, and once it feels correct it's hard to tell whether it described you or whether you recognised yourself in it. Studies of astrological profiles have found that people struggle to tell a description that fits few people from one that fits almost everyone. So a reading that feels right isn't, on that feeling alone, evidence that the chart described you.

## What the research found {#what-the-research-found}

In 1949 the psychologist Bertram Forer called the effect he had found the *fallacy of personal validation*, after a classroom demonstration where every student received the same personality sketch, taken from an astrology book, as if it had been written for them. That experiment has its own page, [the Barnum (Forer) effect](/astrology/barnum-forer-effect/).

The term *subjective validation* came later. It first appeared in *The Psychology of the Psychic*, published in 1980 by David Marks and Richard Kammann, who taught psychology at the University of Otago in New Zealand. They hadn't set out to debunk anything. By their own account, they thought the psychology of perception might be "about to go through a psychic revolution", and they wanted to be part of it. They examined the best-known psychic claims of the 1970s, tried to replicate the experiments, found flaws in how the originals had been run, and concluded that no evidence for psychic powers had yet been produced. The later chapters turn to the question that matters here: why people believe anyway. In the second edition, in 2000, Marks treats subjective validation as related to the Barnum effect and more general than it.

Studies of astrology have measured the same pull. Paul Rogers and Janice Soule, in 2009, gave people a vague personality profile presented as coming from Western or from Chinese astrology. Believers in astrology rated it a better description of themselves than sceptics did, whatever its supposed source. Their paper also notes that people given such profiles consistently fail to tell common personality descriptions from uncommon ones. And Nick Allum, writing in 2010 and citing earlier work, points to a detail that concerns any astrologer who asks for a birth time: the more birth detail goes into an astrological prediction, the more readily people tend to believe it.

The psychologist Raymond Nickerson counts a close cousin of this, not distinguishing a message that shows special ability from one that doesn't, as a form of [confirmation bias](/astrology/confirmation-bias/). And Ray Hyman's account of [cold reading](/astrology/cold-reading/) shows how a reader in the room can put the same pull to work.

## What I found {#what-i-found}

I ask for your [birth time](/blog/unknown-birth-time/) before I read, because there's no reading without it. By Allum's account, all that detail also makes a reading easier to believe. It doesn't make it truer, and I'd rather say so here than have you find it out.

My notebook's rule reads almost like an answer to this bias. A tick only if a placement described me in a way I couldn't have written about anyone else; a cross if it was vague, wrong, or would have fitted my neighbour just as well. By that rule, the kind of sketch Forer handed out gets a cross. It doesn't remove subjective validation, though, because the person judging was the person described: me first, then family, friends and strangers online, who each marked their own lines, knowing the chart was theirs.

With that said, this is what the count gave. About a third held, 30 to 40%, and the Sun sign, the rising sign, Venus and Mercury were among the placements that held at that rate. [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/), [Lilith](/astrology/black-moon-lilith/) and [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/) held 75 to 90% of the time. It's [a notebook, not a study](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), and it proves nothing about astrology in general.

So when a reading of mine leans on a placement that held less often, I tell you. And when a line feels uncannily right, I'd ask you the question my rule asks: could it have been written about your neighbour?
