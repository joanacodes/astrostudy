Hidalgo was the first centaur anyone found, in 1920, though no one could call it that for another 57 years. It is also one of the few bodies astrologers meet that carries a man's name rather than a god's: a priest who rang his church bells in 1810 and started Mexico's war of independence.

## What it is {#what-it-is}

Hidalgo is a dark body about 50 km across, possibly with two lobes. Walter Baade found it on 31 October 1920 at the Bergedorf observatory in Hamburg; he would also find [Icarus](/astrology/icarus/), in 1949. Its orbit is a long ellipse. At its closest it comes 1.9 times the Earth's distance from the Sun, to the inner edge of the asteroid belt; at its farthest, 9.5 times, almost to the orbit of [Saturn](/astrology/saturn/). One circuit takes about 13 years and 9 months, and the orbit is tilted 43° to the Sun's path, perhaps after a close encounter with Jupiter: as recently as 1922 it passed within 0.89 times the Earth–Sun distance of the giant planet.

On average it stays between the orbits of Jupiter and Neptune, which makes it what astronomers now call a centaur. But centaurs weren't recognised as a class until [Chiron](/astrology/chiron/) was found in 1977, so for decades Hidalgo was simply an odd asteroid, and until Pluto turned up in 1930 it was the most distant minor planet known. A route that reaches out to Saturn is usually a comet's, and some astronomers suspect Hidalgo once was one.

## How it works {#how-it-works}

The name came from a meeting. German astronomers in Mexico for the total eclipse of 10 September 1923 had an audience with President Álvaro Obregón and asked his permission to name the asteroid after Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla. Hidalgo (1753–1811) was a Catholic priest and teacher who, on the night of 15 to 16 September 1810, rang the bells of his parish church at Dolores and called the people to arms against Spanish rule. That call, the Grito de Dolores, is remembered as the start of Mexico's War of Independence, and Mexico honours him as the Father of the Nation. He was captured and executed the following year.

That makes Hidalgo an awkward body for anyone who reads small bodies through their names. Most of them bring a myth, told in many versions over centuries; this one brings a biography, a real man's life with its politics and its violence. Reading that life into the charts of strangers born two centuries later is a bigger leap than reading a myth, not a smaller one. Every asteroid that happens to share your own name raises the same problem on a smaller scale ([Personal-name asteroids](/astrology/personal-name-asteroids/)).

The orbit raises a second difficulty. Tilted 43°, almost as steeply as [Eris](/astrology/eris/)'s, it often carries Hidalgo far above or below the planets, and a chart records only its position projected onto the zodiac, so a close conjunction on paper can join two bodies that are far apart in the sky. Its stretched path also means it crosses some signs quickly and lingers in others.

## In a reading {#in-a-reading}

Hidalgo isn't in my readings, and since a centaur is, I owe a reason. Chiron was on the list in my notebook, and with Saturn and Lilith it held far more often than anything else, 75 to 90% of the time ([The three that held](/blog/saturn-chiron-lilith/)). Being the first of its class doesn't give Hidalgo Chiron's record. It was never on the list, and the system I built for my readings computes no centaur but Chiron ([How I read](/how-i-read/)).

If you place Hidalgo yourself, remember its 43° tilt before trusting any conjunction, decide in advance whether you'll use it at all, as with every name on the long list, [Karma](/astrology/karma/) among them, and never let it speak louder than the planets ([How to use asteroids without drowning the chart](/astrology/how-to-use-asteroids-without-drowning-the-chart/)).

## Common myths {#myths}

- **“Hidalgo is named after a god.”** It's named after a man: a Mexican priest who started his country's war of independence in 1810.
- **“Chiron was the first centaur ever found.”** Chiron, in 1977, was the first recognised as one. Hidalgo had been travelling on a centaur's orbit, unrecognised, since 1920.
- **“Hidalgo is an ordinary main-belt asteroid.”** It only touches the belt at its closest point; at its farthest it is nearly as far out as Saturn.
